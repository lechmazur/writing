Ines Varga said yes to everything, which was how she had become the only sky mechanic on Halloran Station. [19] The xenobiology crew asked her to string heated perches along the cliff gantries, and she strung them. [36] They asked her to recalibrate the drone cameras at minus forty, and she did that too, smiling through a cracked visor. [57] Dr. Okonkwo called her mechanically warm, and meant it kindly, the way one praises a good radiator. [74] Ines privately agreed. [77] Her kindness was a maintained system, oiled by habit since the winter her brother Tomas fell from a gantry she had not finished bolting. [101] Saying yes kept her hands busy and her chest quiet. [111]

The station existed because of the lanternwings. [118] Each dusk, thousands of them rode the planet's field lines home to the cliffs, drawn to a point where the magnetic flux knotted above the station like a fist. [147] The researchers called it the nexus. [153] At twilight it glowed faintly green, and the creatures folded their glassy wings and hung from the perches in rows, breathing. [174] Ines loved that moment most, the hush of beating wings at rest, when a whole sky went still at once. [194]

Nobody else noticed what she noticed. [200] The lanternwings woke strangely. [204] Their larvae, sealed in membrane sacs along the cliff, never stirred or cracked open; they simply began, one evening, to show patterns on their skins, interference fringes shifting like oil on water. [236] Okonkwo logged these subdued awakenings as metabolic noise. [244] Ines, who had spent twenty years reading stress fractures in alloy, recognized a hologram when she saw one. [262]

She asked, for once, instead of being asked. [270] "May I borrow a molted wing and the alignment laser?" [280]

Okonkwo shrugged, surprised. [283] "For what?" [285]

"To prove that time moves sideways." [291]

She explained it badly in the mess hall and better on the gantry, her breath freezing between words. [309] A hologram, she said, stored the whole picture in every fragment; cut the plate in half and each half still showed everything, only dimmer. [333] The lanternwing membranes worked through holographic principles, and she believed each one recorded not a single creature's past but the entire colony's present, spread across every roost on the continent. [363] They did not remember forward or backward. [370] They remembered across. [373]

"And why does that matter to you?" Okonkwo asked. [382]

Ines tightened a bolt that did not need tightening. [391] "Because if time can go sideways, then nothing is only behind us." [403]

The test required the nexus at full strength, which meant twilight, which meant the colony returning. [419] Ines mounted the molted wing in a frame beneath the green knot of sky and waited while ten thousand bodies settled around her, their small heat rising off the perches she had built. [452]

Then the hush came. [456]

The field-shield housing over the nexus sensor had frozen shut. [466] She jammed a pry bar into the seam and leaned on it, levering until the ice split with a crack like a snapped bone and the magnetometer spun awake. [495] The nexus poured through the membrane. [501] She fired the laser. [505]

Above the frame, faint as breath on glass, an image assembled: not this cliff, but the northern roosts four hundred kilometers away, lanternwings hanging in the same rows, in the same stillness, at this same instant. [541] Okonkwo checked the northern drone feed on her wrist and went quiet. [553] The feeds matched, wing for wing. [559]

"A shared now," she whispered. [564] "They live the moment wide instead of long." [572]

The image shifted, sliding east through roost after roost, and Ines watched each one bloom and fade. [589] It was not Tomas, and she had known it would not be. [601] But it was proof of a world where a moment did not end when it passed you; it simply continued somewhere beside you, still breathing, wings folded. [628] Something in her chest, long bolted down, loosened a quarter turn. [639]

Along the cliff, the larval sacs flickered with new fringes, waking the only way they knew, softly, all together. [658]

Okonkwo put a gloved hand on her shoulder. [666] "You'll need help writing this up," she said. [674]

Ines, for the first time in years, considered saying no, just to feel what it was like to choose. [693] Then she said yes, and meant it, and the cold air carried her answer sideways across the sleeping sky. [712]
