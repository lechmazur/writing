Every evening at the sixth chime, Ilse Varn tuned her violin on the sky-bridge that joined the Counting House to the Foundry, three hundred feet above the orchard nobody visited. [30] The clerks who crossed her wore brass hearing-cones set to the city's approved frequency, and her music slid off them like rain off a tin roof. [56] She played anyway. [59] Her mother had played there before her, until the night she climbed down into the orchard and did not climb back. [80]

The orchard was the last green thing in Cogsend, a square of apple and quince trees enchanted to bloom forever, and it was policed by statues. [106] They stood among the roots in verdigris coats, gilt flaking from their faces, and they were alive: they turned their heads toward any heartbeat that quickened, and they closed their stone hands around it. [140] The Regulators said the statues protected the trees from thieves. [150] Ilse had begun to suspect they protected the city from the trees. [162]

That spring, the locusts came up. [168] They surfaced every seventeen years, and one of them had climbed the sky-bridge and split its back on her music stand, leaving its shell behind like a lantern with the candle gone. [200] Ilse held it to the failing light. [207] Amber, weightless, perfect down to the hooked feet, and empty along one clean seam where something had pushed its way out. [228]

On a whim she wedged it against her violin's bridge, and when she drew the bow the note came out resonantly soft, a hush with a pulse inside it, lower than the Foundry's grinding and somehow closer. [265] Far below, a statue lifted its head. [272] Not quickly, the way they lifted for thieves. [280] Slowly, as if remembering. [284] Ilse stopped playing, her own heart slamming, but she was too high to hunt, and the statue lowered its head. [304]

She spent the next week learning what it had heard. [314] The muted violin did not carry melody down that far; it carried rhythm, the slow lub and hush of a resting heart, and the statues answered rhythm. [341] When she bowed at sixty beats, they turned toward her. [351] At fifty, they raised their hands. [357] At forty, the tall one by the quince tree, the one holding a stone violin she had always told herself was a coincidence, tapped its chest twice, and Ilse knew. [387]

Knowing was not understanding. [391] The city itself was built on that difference. [399] Each hour the great clock struck twice: first the chime, by which people knew the hour, then the gear-knock, by which the machines understood it, and between them lay eleven seconds in which every gear in Cogsend held still, even the iron locks that bolted the statues to their pedestals. [449]

Her mother had not been caught stealing fruit. [457] She had gone down to wake the statues, and the orchard had kept her as one of them, waiting for someone who could keep a calm heart long enough to answer. [488]

At midnight Ilse climbed down the service ladder with the violin strapped to her back and the locust shell tucked in her collar. [511] Blossoms fell on her like tarnished snow, pink gone brown at the edges from a century of soot. [529] She set the shell on the bridge, breathed until her pulse slowed to forty, and played that pulse into the dark. [550]

Stone heads turned. [553] Stone hearts, somewhere under the verdigris, answered in kind, and forty beats became a single rhythm moving through the orchard like sap. [575]

The clock chimed. [578] Everyone in Cogsend knew it was midnight. [585] In the eleven seconds before the machines understood, the iron locks went slack, and the statues bent, took hold of the trees they had guarded for a hundred years, and uprooted them. [617] Roots tore free with a sound like a held breath let go. [629] Then the statues stepped off their pedestals, carrying apple and quince on their shoulders, and walked toward the streets where nothing had bloomed in living memory. [655] The gear-knock came too late to hold them. [663]

The tall statue paused beside Ilse and laid one cold hand over her heart, feeling the rhythm there, and Ilse finally understood what her mother had meant to give the city. [694] Not fruit, not trees, but the memory that beauty was something you carried, not something kept behind stone. [712]

By dawn the clerks crossing the sky-bridge had taken off their hearing-cones to stare at the orchard replanted in the Foundry square, and Ilse played for them resonantly soft, the empty locust shell humming on her bridge, until one by one their hearts fell into time. [758]
