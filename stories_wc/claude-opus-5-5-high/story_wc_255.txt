The phoenix rose on a Tuesday, out of the cold mouth of Maro's furnace, and everyone who watched it fell asleep. [21] Ilse did not fall asleep. [26] She had seen the bird the way she saw everything, as a tumble of silvers and charcoals, bright and dim but never red, and whatever the phoenix poured into the eyes of the others had found no door in hers. [66]

Three days later the glassblower's courtyard was still full of sleepers. [77] Two apprentices lay curled by the annealing oven, the water-seller slumped against the well, and Maro himself sat upright on his bench with a blowpipe across his knees, breathing slow as a banked coal. [111] Ilse fed them sugar water with a spoon and painted them while they slept, because painting was what she did when she was frightened. [135]

Her canvases had always been wrong. [141] Patrons had laughed at her olive-green sunsets and her lavender bread, and the guild had struck her name from its roll. [162] Only Maro had bought them, hanging them beside his own flawed work, the bowls with trapped bubbles and the goblets that leaned like drunks. [186] "A perfect thing is finished," he liked to say. [195] "A flawed thing is still talking." [201]

On the fourth morning, a woman came through the gate carrying a basket of cuttings. [216] Everyone in the city knew of Nettle, who traded awakenings after the phoenix rose, and everyone knew she never spoke. [236] Her voice, people said, had been the price of her own waking, a century ago. [251] Now she talked in flowers. [256]

She knelt on the flagstones and laid out a sprig of rosemary beside Maro's boots, then a poppy of some color Ilse would never know, then a closed fist of moss. [287] Ilse knew enough of the old language to read it: remembrance, sleep, and payment held tight. [303] Give me what he loves best, it said, and I will awaken him. [316]

Ilse looked around the courtyard at the perfect glass Maro kept for rich buyers, the clear decanters, the flawless lamp globes, each worth a season's bread. [342] Nettle's eyes went to them too. [348] Then Ilse went to the windowsill and came back with a sliver of brass no longer than her thumbnail. [367] It was a reed tongue from Maro's old concertina, the one he had played so badly that the neighbors threw shoes, and it was cracked along its length. [395] She set it on the moss with the wide, hopeful face of a child offering a pebble. [412]

Nettle frowned and snapped a stem of yarrow in half: broken, worthless. [424]

Ilse did not argue. [428] She had spent her life being told she saw things wrongly, and she had learned that the quickest way to win was to look as if she had not understood the question. [460] She fetched one of Maro's lopsided bowls, the one with a crack he had never mended, and packed its bottom with earth. [482] Into it she pressed a dandelion she had pulled from between the flagstones, a weed, a flower nobody arranges. [501] Then she laid the broken yarrow across the bowl's rim and tucked the reed into the crack, where it stood like a small brass leaf. [526]

Nettle studied it a long time. [532] Broken things, it said, still grow. [538]

What Ilse did not say, because it would have sounded foolish, was that she had watched the sleepers for three days the way she watched everything, by shape and motion rather than color. [571] Each evening the wind had rattled the cracked reed on the sill, making a sour little buzz, and each evening Maro's eyelids had flickered. [595] She guessed that the phoenix's song was too perfect for anything perfect to answer, and that only a wrong note could catch in it. [619]

Nettle lifted the reed, set it between her lips, and blew. [630] The sound was dreadful, a goose stepped on, a hinge in pain. [642] The apprentices sat up coughing, the water-seller swore, and Maro opened his eyes and said, "Who's murdering my concertina?" [661]

Nettle tucked the reed into her basket as her fee, bowed, and left the bowl behind. [677]

Later, Ilse painted the dandelion in its cracked bowl. [686] She made the petals gray, because that was how she saw them, and Maro hung the canvas above his furnace, where the next phoenix would have to look at it. [716] "It's wrong," he told her, delighted. [722] "It's still talking." [725]
