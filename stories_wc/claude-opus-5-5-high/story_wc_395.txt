Ines Varga kept the memory of Commonplace, a social network that had outlived most of its users. [17] Her title was Corporate Memory Keeper, which meant she sat in a windowless room on the fourth floor and decided which dead accounts deserved preservation and which would be compressed into silence. [49] Colleagues called her brightly attentive, the way people describe a bird that notices crumbs. [63] She did notice things, and the thing she had noticed for years was a question nobody paid her to answer: where do deleted files go? [88] Engineers told her they simply stopped existing. [95] But Ines kept finding residue, orphaned thumbnails and broken song links, like footprints leading away from a house that had been torn down. [118]

The residue that changed everything belonged to Tamsin Rook. [127] Tamsin had vanished eleven years earlier, a missing persons case the police had filed cold after her account was erased overnight. [148] A detective named Okafor still emailed the archive every April, asking if anything had surfaced. [163] Nothing ever had. [166]

Then, on a Tuesday night, Ines found a playlist with no owner, titled only 417. [181] She pressed play. [184] The first song was a folk ballad no streaming service still carried, a woman singing about a lighthouse keeper who left the lamp burning after she was gone. [212] It ran exactly four minutes and seventeen seconds. [220] So did the second, and the third. [227]

Once Ines saw the number, she could not stop seeing it. [238] Tamsin's old user ID, recovered from a cached comment, ended in 417. [250] The deletion log was stamped 4:17 in the morning. [259] And one surviving thumbnail, blurred to a smear of blue and gold, showed an embroidered patch from the Heron mission, which had launched on April seventeenth and broken apart over the Pacific on its way home. [295] Tamsin's brother had been its flight engineer. [302]

The songs kept playing as Ines worked, each one a voice nobody remembered, and between them she heard the hiss of the room, the fans, the absence of anyone else awake. [333] It felt less like searching than like listening for someone in another room. [346]

By the ninth song she found the part that made her hands go cold. [360] Tamsin had not been a user who happened to be deleted. [371] She had been an employee, the Corporate Memory Keeper before Ines, with administrator rights to erase anyone, including herself. [390] Her personnel file had been purged too, but the building access records survived in a backup no one audited, and they showed her badge opening one door, again and again, the week before she disappeared. [425] Basement storage, room 417. [429]

Ines took the stairs because the elevator logged its riders. [439] The room held decommissioned servers, dust, a folding chair, and a steel cabinet whose drawer slid open without a key. [459] Inside lay the patch itself, frayed at the edges, a heron stitched in gold thread above the names of six people who had fallen out of the sky. [487] Beneath it was a hard drive and an index card. [497]

The card read: To whoever keeps the memory next. [506] Deleted files go wherever someone refuses to let them vanish. [516] The company purged my brother's crew from every feed to save storage, so I kept them here. [533] I am not missing. [537] I simply chose to be kept by no one. [546] Please leave the songs playing. [551]

Ines plugged the drive into her laptop on the cold floor. [562] It held what the company had thrown away: the crew's birthday videos, a child's drawing of a rocket, strangers' condolences, and ten thousand other lives Tamsin had caught on their way to nowhere. [595] Upstairs, the playlist was still running, and Ines understood at last that the number had never been a code. [614] It was a trail meant for someone who would notice. [624]

In April, Detective Okafor's email arrived on schedule. [632] Ines read it three times, the patch warm in her pocket, where she had carried it every day since. [651] Answering honestly would mean handing over the drive, and the company's lawyers would reach it before any family did. [670] It would mean dragging a woman back into a record she had spent her life keeping for others. [688] She typed the lie carefully: No new information has surfaced. [698] Then she sent it at 4:17, because somewhere, she suspected, someone was still watching the timestamps. [714]

Down in room 417, the old servers hummed, and the empty chair beside them felt, as it always would, recently occupied. [735]
