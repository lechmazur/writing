The tide went out farther than it had in eighteen years, and at dusk Maren Ostby went with it. [19] She was seventy-one, wore her late husband's waders cinched with twine, and carried a trowel, a hand lens, and the green notebook she had filled every day of her working life. [50] Folklorists who only read, she liked to say, were just librarians with better stories. [64] She dug things up. [68]

Out on the flats of Haddock Bar, the wrecks stood where they had always stood, a loose ring of ribs and boilers around the one thing the sea had never quite finished swallowing: her grandfather's orchard greenhouse. [105] Local sailors blamed the bar for the wrecks. [113] Local grandmothers blamed the greenhouse, and said the lights in it pulled captains off course. [128] Maren had spent five decades cataloguing stories like that for other people's villages and never once written down her own. [148]

She had been nine the summer the storm put the greenhouse under. [160] She remembered the cold apples, the brass latch, the smell of whitewash and tar. [174] She did not remember her grandfather at all. [182] Not his voice, not his face, not the three days her mother swore Maren had been missing before they found her asleep on the greenhouse floor inside a circle of pale little mushrooms. [215] He drowned trying to reach her, people said. [223] The ring, people said, had taken what it wanted in trade. [234]

She had come back because she was chronically curious, and because at seventy-one she had started losing other memories too, the ordinary way, and she wanted the stolen ones back before the ordinary thief got there first. [271]

The greenhouse sat tilted in the silt, its iron ribs furred with weed. [284] Inside, the apple trees were black spars. [291] Overhead, some panes still held, and on them she saw what she had forgotten she knew: stars, painted in white lead, the whole winter sky laid across the roof. [320] Someone had wanted the trees to know what season it was. [331]

But the storm had broken half the glass, and the constellations were broken with it. [346] Orion had no belt. [350] The Pleiades were two sisters short. [356] Maren stood beneath them with her hand lens and did what she had always done with a torn manuscript: she studied what was missing. [380] Every lost star was a lost pane, and every lost pane had fallen somewhere. [394] She knelt, sighted up through the gaps, and marked each spot in the mud beneath with a shell. [412]

When she stood back, the shells made a circle. [421] She dug with her fingers, and there, under an inch of silt, were the shards, painted side down, and threaded between them the dry brown caps of fairy ring mushrooms. [451] Marasmius oreades, the book name. [456] They could dry for years and revive in a single rain. [467] The stars had fallen exactly on the ring, as if someone had once lain here looking up. [484]

She had detected it; now she needed the rule. [493] Every tradition she had catalogued said roughly the same thing about rings: what they take, they keep until habits break. [513] The tide abandoning its usual line was one habit broken. [523] The other would have to be hers. [530]

Maren always entered a circle sunwise. [536] She always wrote before she touched. [542] She set the green notebook face down on a rotten crate, left the pencil in her pocket, and stepped into the ring backward, widdershins, bare-handed. [567] Then she lay down in the cold silt, the way a child would, and looked up through the broken stars. [587] Seawater seeped around her shoulders. [592] Beside her ear, a dry cap drank and softened and opened. [603]

It came back the way cold comes back into fingers, all at once and stinging. [618] A big man with a paint pot, laughing, telling her Orion was a hunter who kept losing his belt. [637] His hands, cracked and warm, lifting her over the latch. [647] His voice in the storm, not afraid, saying lie still, look up, count the stars, I will come get you. [667] She had counted. [670] He had come, and put his coat over her, and gone back out for the boat. [686] The ring had not so much stolen him as kept him, the way you hold a coat for a child too small to carry it. [711]

The tide turned. [714] Maren got up, muddy to the neck, and did not reach for the notebook. [728] Some things you keep by writing them down. [736] Some you keep by walking home under the real sky, decades later, naming every star out loud. [753]
