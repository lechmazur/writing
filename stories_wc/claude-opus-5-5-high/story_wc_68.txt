Oskar Venn called himself a philosopher because no one had ever paid him to stop. [15] He lived in the great storm drain beneath Carrow, where the sea had come in forty years ago and never fully left, and where the ruins of the upper city stood waist-deep in green water like men too tired to climb out. [57] The plague had ended in spring. [63] Nobody rang bells about it; the bells had been melted for coffin nails, which was the kind of decision Oskar admired for its honesty. [87]

Now the Reclamation Council had a story, and it was nailed to every post above the waterline. [104] The sickness, the notices said, had risen from the drowned harbor, from the old navigators' guild and its brass instruments, its tide tables, its superstitious habit of keeping time by hand-wound clocks instead of the Council's signal gun. [142] All brass would be surrendered and smelted. [149] The sewers would be filled with rubble. [156]

The navigators had their own story, shouted from the rooftops of the sunken quarter: the plague had come with the Council's new canal, dug through the marsh in the dry year, releasing whatever the marsh had been keeping. [194] Two stories, each convenient for its teller. [201] Oskar distrusted convenience the way other men distrusted rats. [210]

He was not a rebel by temperament. [217] He was a rebel by arithmetic. [223] If a thing was true, it would leave marks, and marks could be read. [237]

The drain walls told time better than any council. [246] Every flood since the sea came in had laid down a band of silt: gray for winter storms, brown for the summer runoff, a thin black seam wherever the city had burned. [278] Oskar had spent eleven years counting them with a candle and a butter knife, and he could walk his finger down a wall the way a priest walked down a genealogy. [309]

So he dug. [312] He cut a trench through the floor of the old junction chamber, through the sedimentary layers one season at a time, scraping, sifting, writing each band on the back of a Council notice. [345] The plague layer was easy: a pale crust of lime from the corpse-carts, beginning six bands down and running almost to the surface. [368] Just beneath it lay a slick of blue marsh clay that belonged nowhere in a harbor drain. [385] Canal spoil. [387]

And far below both, in a brown band laid down thirty years before any sickness, his knife struck metal. [406] It was a cog no wider than a thumbnail, its teeth cut with a precision no living smith in Carrow could manage: a seafarer's chronometer cog, the escape wheel from a navigator's clock. [439] Green with verdigris, perfectly harmless, lying three decades deep in clean silt while the city above it stayed healthy. [458]

Oskar sat on the lip of his trench and agonized, which he did thoroughly, as he did everything. [476] The trench did not prove the navigators right. [484] Clay under lime was sequence, not cause; a philosopher who forgot that deserved to live in a sewer. [502] But it proved the Council wrong, and the Council was the side holding the furnace. [517] If he stayed silent, every chronometer in Carrow would be poured into ingots, and the old way of finding longitude, of carrying home's hour across open water, would end with the last navigator's cough. [551] If he spoke, the navigators would wave his trench like a flag and call it vengeance. [567] He wanted neither story. [571] He wanted the method to survive, because a method outlives the people who lie with it. [587]

At low tide he climbed to the Council steps carrying a plank. [599] On it, pressed into wet plaster, was a column of sediment cut whole from the trench wall, every band labeled in his cramped hand, the cog set into its brown stripe like a period at the end of a sentence. [639] A clerk told him to go back underground. [647] A navigator told him he was a hero. [655] Oskar told them both, without raising his voice, that they were illiterate. [667]

Then he taught them to read. [673] He did it slowly, pointing, counting bands aloud, while the crowd thickened and the tide turned. [689] He did not tell them what caused the plague; he told them what had not, and how anyone with a knife and patience could check. [714] By dusk a dozen people had gone down into the drains with candles. [727] The Council postponed the smelting, pending review, which was cowardice, but useful cowardice. [740]

Oskar went home to his sewer, pried the cog loose, and fitted it into a broken clock he had fished from the harbor years ago. [765] It began, grudgingly, to keep time. [771] He wrote the hour on the wall, at the top of the newest layer, where someone would one day dig for it. [793]
