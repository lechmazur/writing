Ada Kuo wrote fortunes for a living, and she had not slept in thirty-one hours. [15] Her desk sat at the top of the old glass spire on Lake Varn, a weather station abandoned after the instruments went digital, and the only heat came from a coil stove that ticked like a patient clock. [53] The factory in the city wanted four hundred new fortunes by noon. [65] She had written three hundred and twelve. [72]

Outside, the lake had frozen so smoothly that it held the whole sky upside down. [87] In the gray before morning there was no horizon at all, only a seam where the stars went down into the ice and came up again, a mirrored line that made the spire feel suspended between two identical nights. [126] Ada called this the upturned hush, because the silence out there seemed to hang from the ceiling of the world rather than rest on its floor. [152]

She had a deadline, and she had perhaps an hour before exhaustion hit, and she had a piece of shell. [172] The nautilus segment was no longer than her thumb, a curved slice cut clean through so the chambers showed, each pearled room sealed off from the last. [199] Her grandfather had given it to her the winter she was nine, the winter they lived in the tuning shop above the laundromat, when he repaired pianos nobody played and let her strike the tuning forks against her knee. [238] "The animal only lives in the newest room," he told her. [249] "The rest it keeps for balance." [255]

Ada was homesick, but not for a place. [263] She was homesick for a year, for the specific Tuesday light of 1998, for the hum of a struck A settling into the wood of a rebuilt upright. [291] She could not buy a ticket there. [298] So she mapped it. [302]

On the back of the rejected fortune slips she had drawn the spiral of the shell, and in each chamber she wrote a memory, smallest at the center: the smell of felt hammers, her grandfather's thumb dented from the tuning lever, the radiator that knocked in three-quarter time, the night he took her onto a frozen pond and told her to lie flat and listen. [367] She had not understood then what she was supposed to hear. [378] He had pressed his ear to the ice and smiled like a man receiving a letter. [394] "There is music nobody has written down," he said. [403] "Someone has to be its conductor." [409]

She had wanted that job ever since, without knowing how to apply. [421] She had tried composition school and left with debt and a drawer of scores nobody performed. [437] Fortune cookies were what remained, tiny messages cracked open by strangers who never asked who wrote them. [454] Lately her fortunes had turned strange. [460] The factory manager had emailed twice about one that read, "The ice is keeping time for you." [477] Customers, he said, preferred lucky numbers. [483]

Now, tracing the outermost chamber of her drawing, Ada found it empty. [495] She had filled every room with the past and left nothing for the present. [509] The stove ticked. [512] She stood, pulled on her coat, and went down the spiral stair and out onto the lake. [529]

The cold took her breath and gave it back as smoke. [540] She walked until the spire was a dim needle behind her, then lay down on the black glass the way she had at nine, the shell in her fist, one ear against the ice. [574] At first there was only the hush. [581]

Then the east began to pale, and the dawn came up twice, once in the sky and once beneath her, the two lights moving toward each other along the mirrored horizon until they met. [615] The warmth was nothing, a fraction of a degree, but the lake felt it. [629] The ice groaned, low as a cello. [636] A crack ran singing away from her like a bow drawn across a saw, another answered from the far shore, and then the whole sheet began to sound, booms and chimes and deep pulses, a symphony performed for no audience in a hall made of sky. [682]

Ada lifted her hand without deciding to. [689] When she let it fall, the next boom rolled out from beneath her palm. [703] It was not magic, only timing, the conductor's single art: knowing when the silence is ready to end. [721]

Back in the spire, before exhaustion took her, she wrote eighty-eight more fortunes, one for each key on the piano her grandfather never finished. [745] Each was a bar of what she had heard, small enough to fit inside a cookie. [761] The last one she wrote in the outermost chamber of her map: "The newest room is yours." [778] Across the city, strangers would crack them open and never know they were playing their parts. [794]
