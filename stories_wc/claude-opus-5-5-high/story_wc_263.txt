In Harrowmere, nobody had turned a page of the calendar since the sun went down for the winter and did not come back. [23] The book still hung by the chapel door, its sheets blank and curling, and people said the days had simply stopped needing names. [46] Ilse Varga did not believe them, because she could hear what they were not saying. [61]

She had always been the moonstruck one, the girl who fell asleep on the roof with her mouth open to the sky, and the gift had come with the moon: words that never left a throat still reached her ears, soft as moth wings. [105] Behind the baker's closed lips lived a melody he would not sing. [117] The weaver's hands wove plain gray while her silence described a tapestry of herons. [131] The children, told to be quiet in the dark, carried whole unwritten stories behind their teeth, and Ilse heard them all, pressing against the town like water behind ice. [160] It hurt to listen to so much held breath. [169]

Her grandmother had left her an astrolabe of blackened brass, its rete pierced with star-pointers shaped like tiny flames. [188] Along its rim was engraved an old northern saying: We are remembered stardust, and what we forget, the stars keep for us. [210] Grandmother had used it to fix the feast days; Ilse used it for falling stars. [225]

Night after night, across that calendar no one would write, she lay on the roof and sighted every streak of light through the alidade, marking where each began and where it vanished. [257] She drew the paths in charcoal on the whitewashed wall of her room. [270] At first they looked like scratches. [276] Then, after perhaps forty nights or perhaps four hundred, the scratches leaned together like the spokes of a wheel, and every one of them pointed at the old stone archway on the ridge above town. [311]

Nobody went there anymore. [315] The arch had been built to frame the first sunrise after the long dark, and Tomas Brek, who kept the calendar, used to stand beneath it and announce the new year. [346] Then his wife Mara had died in the first week of winter, and Tomas had climbed to the arch and not come down. [369] The town, ashamed of its own singing while he grieved, had gone quiet with him. [384]

Ilse climbed the ridge with the astrolabe inside her coat. [394] The aurora was up, green and violet curtains folding over the sky, and the archway stood inside that light like a door into a burning church. [420] The stones glittered. [423] Up close she saw why: fine silver dust had settled into every crack, drifted there by falling stars, and it shimmered with faint pictures, a heron, a loaf, a boy on a sled. [456] The stardust remembered everything the town had refused to make. [466]

Tomas sat beneath the keystone, wrapped in blankets, his eyes red-rimmed and fixed on the horizon. [482] He had not slept, the villagers said, since Mara. [491] He did not speak when Ilse knelt beside him, but she heard him anyway. [505]

If I close my eyes, I will forget her face. [515] If I write the new year, she stays in the old one. [527]

Ilse understood then that the whole town was waiting on one man's refusal to rest. [542]

She set the astrolabe in his lap and turned the rete until its flame-shaped pointers lined up with the glittering cracks, and in the dust above his head a woman's face formed, laughing, snow in her hair. [579]

"The stars keep what we forget," Ilse said aloud, reading the rim. [591] "You can sleep, Tomas. [595] She is not going anywhere." [600]

He looked at the face a long time. [608] Then something in his shoulders gave way, and he wept, and after the weeping he lay down on the cold stones beneath the arch and slept like a child who has been forgiven. [641]

The moment his breathing slowed, the dust lifted. [649] It poured from the archway in a luminous river, down the ridge and into the dark town, and Ilse heard the silence break open below her: the baker's melody finally sung, a child shouting the first line of a story, the clack of a loom changing color. [696] It was the most beautiful sound she had ever heard, and it was built on a grief that would never fully leave, and she did not think those two things could be separated. [729]

In the morning, if it was morning, someone had written a single word on the first blank page of the calendar: Mara. [751] Beneath it, in a dozen different hands, the town had begun to fill in the days. [767]

Ilse went home, lay down on her roof beneath the fading lights, and for the first time in a long while, she rested too. [791]
