Above the clouds, where the air was thin enough to ring, stood the opal library, and Oren Vale was the last person who still climbed its stairs. [27] He was its glass-orchid caretaker, a title that had once required an examination and now required only that he refuse to leave. [49] The orchids grew along the reading galleries in long rows, their petals blown from glass so fine that a draft could make them hum. [73] Each one held a name. [78] That was the library's purpose, or had been, before the scholars left and the shelves became deserted marvels that nobody came to marvel at. [102] When a person was forgotten below, in the cities under the cloud floor, their name drifted up and settled into a bud, and the caretaker's task was to keep the bud singing. [134] A silent orchid was a name gone for good. [143]

Oren worked in a gently systematic way, because hurry cracked glass and so did grief. [158] At dusk he took his taper and went to light the lamps, one per gallery, in the same order his teacher had shown him forty years before. [185] The warmth rose, the cold air rushed in beneath it, and the draft passed through the great bamboo wind chime hanging in the central dome. [210] The chime's voice woke the orchids, and the orchids answered with their names. [223] That was how it had always worked. [230]

This autumn, one bamboo tube had cracked. [237] The split ran from its lip nearly to the knot, and instead of its low note it gave a dry rattle, like a cough behind a door. [264] Night by night, a few more orchids in the eastern gallery stayed dark. [277] Oren could not cut a new tube; the bamboo had come from a forest that no longer existed. [295]

So he went down, into the lower floors, to the museum of extinct feelings. [309] The library measured time in its own way, and the old caretakers said a life was a walk across the museum, hall by hall, each feeling going extinct as you passed it. [341] Oren had walked most of it. [347] He passed the case of Unbruised Trust, where he had been a boy, and the alcove of Waiting-For-Letters, where he had been a young man. [372] In the last hall, under a bell jar, lay a dried sprig of foxglove labeled Regret That Heals. [390] Foxglove, his teacher had said, stops a heart or steadies it, depending on the dose. [405]

He had left his sister in the cities below to take this post, promising to write, and had written less each year until he could no longer remember her face, and then, shamefully, her name. [440] What he remembered was her song. [446] She used to hum it hanging laundry, a tune with one long low note at the bottom of every line, the same note the chime had lost. [473] He had not sung it in decades. [480]

He climbed back to the dome as the lamps guttered. [490] Methodically, he relit each one, restoring the draft, and when the chime began to stir he stood beneath the cracked tube and sang. [513] His voice was thin and wrong at first. [521] But the opal walls had been shaped for echoes, and the song came back to him fuller than he had sent it, and at the bottom of each line the low note rolled around the dome like a returning tide. [561] By the echo of a long-forgotten song, the chime was whole again. [573]

In the eastern gallery, the dark orchids brightened and began to sing. [585] Oren walked the rows, listening, writing each name in his ledger so it would outlast the glass. [602] Near the end of the last row, a small orchid, newly budded, sang a name he had not heard in thirty years. [624]

Liesl. [625]

It had drifted up only this season, which meant no one below remembered her anymore, and the one who should have was standing here, having forgotten first. [652] Its bud had been among the first to go silent when the tube cracked. [666] The regret rose in him the way foxglove works, a squeeze in the chest that might have killed him and instead made his heart beat slower, steadier. [693]

He wrote her name in the ledger in large letters. [703] Then, because a crack does not heal and neither does a song hold forever, he did the one systematic thing left: he began teaching the tune to the echo each night, singing it into the dome until the walls could almost hum it on their own. [749] Somebody, someday, would climb the stairs after him, and he wanted the library to be able to sing her back. [769]
