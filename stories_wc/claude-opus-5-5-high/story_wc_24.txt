The station at Skarvnes stood on a shelf of rock above the fjord, and its only business was the horizon. [20] Surveyors from the capital wanted to know exactly where the sea met the sky, and how far that line wandered when the air changed its mind. [46] Signe Hald did not measure the horizon herself. [54] She was the hydraulic clock shepherd, which meant she kept the flock. [66] There were twelve of them in the long stone room below the lamp, copper cylinders taller than she was, each dripping into the next through a throat of glass. [95] She cleared their siphons, skimmed their green film in summer, wrapped them in felt when the frost came, and knew them by the sound of their falling water the way a herder knows ewes by their coughs. [132]

Every evening she climbed to the platform with the brass whistle. [143] She would squint west, past the islands, until the sun's lower rim came down to meet the sea. [161] The line out there was a razor edge, thin enough to cut the light in half, and at the instant the gold touched it she blew. [187] Across the water, on the far headland, Aksel heard the note, marked his own reading, and answered with a lamp. [207] The difference between them told the surveyors how much the air had bent the world that day. [224]

Tonight, after blowing the whistle, she went down to read the flock against the mantle clock. [240] The mantle clock had been her father's. [247] It sat on a shelf by the door, walnut and enamel, the one clock at Skarvnes that ran on a spring instead of water, and it was the station's reference because he had trusted it. [282] She wore the mantle clock key on a cord around her neck, a small brass cross that had worn a pale mark on her skin. [307]

The flock was forty seconds slow. [313] That alone meant little; water clocks drifted. [320] What was eminently peculiar was that all twelve had drifted together, by nearly the same amount, as if they had agreed on it among themselves. [345] For years she had wanted to know what clocks really measured. [356] Signe had no one to ask. [362] Her father had been dead four winters, the director in the capital answered letters by spring, and Aksel knew lamps, not water. [384]

So she did what her father had taught her when a sum refused to close: she went backwards. [402] Each clock carried a float and a scribing pen that left a line on a paper drum, and she unrolled the drums across the table and began counting backwards through the hour marks from the whistle. [438] Seven, six, five. [441] At each mark the lag was smaller, but it was there. [452] Four. [453] Three, and there it was, a soft bend in every trace, the place where all twelve drips had slowed at once. [474]

She checked the weather log. [479] At three o'clock the wind had died and a skin of cold air had slid down off the glacier and settled over the water. [503] The stone room had cooled with it. [510] Cold water is thick water; it falls more slowly through a narrow throat. [523] The flock had not been keeping time at all. [532] It had been keeping the temperature, faithfully, second by second, and calling it time because no one had asked it anything else. [554]

She sat back. [557] The fjord below was so still that the islands stood on their own reflections, and she could hear a drop gather and fall at the far end of the room. [587] The same cold layer that had slowed her clocks was lying over the sea, and cold air under warm was exactly what lifted the horizon and made the sun seem to set late. [620] The drift was not an error. [626] It was a second instrument, recording the very bending the surveyors wanted, and it had been doing so for years while they corrected it away. [651]

She took the key from her neck and opened the mantle clock. [663] Inside, the spring was a flat coil of steel, and she understood that it too was measuring something: its own slow loosening, the patience of metal, the hand that had last wound it. [696] Every clock measured itself and called the result the hour. [706] The only honest thing was to compare them, and to write down how they disagreed. [721]

She wound the spring eight turns, as her father had, and listened to it tighten. [736] Then she lit the lamp and signaled Aksel, slowly, in the old code: tomorrow, bring a thermometer to the water's edge. [757] Outside, the last blue drained from the razor line in the west, the fjord kept its silence, and the flock went on dripping, telling the truth about the cold. [786]
