The talks at the grain elevator had failed for the ninth night, so Marta Hollen climbed the astronomical clock tower with a cuckoo clock weight in her coat pocket. [29] It was iron, cast like a pinecone, and it had hung from her grandmother's kitchen clock for sixty years. [48] Tonight it would have to do more. [55]

The town of Vessel lay on both banks of the Drava, German on the east shore, Slovene on the west, and on the island between them stood the old cooperative elevator, eight concrete silos that everyone owned and nobody trusted. [95] The spring flood had taken the only bridge, and since then each bank had argued over who would pay to replace it, who would store whose wheat, whose name would go on the plaque. [129] The mill had cut power to both sides in protest, and the valley sat under the hush of borrowed starlight, the only glow coming from the brass mirror at the top of the tower, which caught the stars and threw them down across the clock's great face. [176]

Marta kept the choral library on the fourth landing, three hundred years of hymnals and part-books stacked beside the gear train. [197] She had catalogued every harmony the town had ever sung, and she knew something the negotiators had forgotten: the harvest anthem had been written for both banks at once, a German line answered by a Slovene one, the two voices braided so tightly that neither made sense alone. [245] The clock had chimed it every harvest until the war, when soldiers took the lunar dial's counterweight for scrap and the mechanism froze. [268]

She had invited the two delegates to meet her there, and they came, suspicious, climbing from opposite stairwells. [286] Anselm Rieder smelled of diesel; Dorota Kranjc carried a lantern she had not lit. [300]

"You said this was about the elevator," Anselm said. [309]

"It is," Marta answered, and then, because Dorota's shoulders had gone stiff at the German, she repeated it in Slovene: "Je." [330] She had learned long ago that people heard kindness faster in their mother tongue, so she spoke the way the anthem sang, interspersing languages, a phrase for him, a phrase for her. [362]

She showed them the empty chain where the counterweight had hung, then held up the pinecone of iron. [380]

"It is too light," Anselm said at once. [388]

"Alone, yes," Marta said. [392] "Dorota, your father repaired this tower's escapement in nineteen sixty; I have his notes filed with the choir minutes. [411] Anselm, your grandfather cast the elevator's bucket chains." [419]

She did not wait for them to decide whether to be offended. [431] She had watched both of them at the talks, Anselm rubbing the knuckle he had broken hauling sandbags, Dorota staring at the water that had drowned her orchard, and she understood that neither was fighting about money. [468] They were afraid of being the one who gave first. [478] So she gave for them. [483]

She hooked the cuckoo weight to the chain and told Anselm to add his wrench, told Dorota to add the iron key to her flooded cellar. [509] They hesitated, then did it, and together the three objects made nearly the right mass. [524] Marta braced her whole body against the winding crank, and when Anselm and Dorota each seized a spoke to help her, the frozen gears groaned, shed rust like red snow, and began to turn. [558]

The moon dial crept across the borrowed stars. [566] Then the bells found the anthem, the first German phrase ringing out over the dark east bank, the Slovene answer rolling west, and below them, in both villages, windows opened. [596] Marta sang with the bells, her alto steady, and she did not stop when Dorota joined on the Slovene lines or when Anselm, gruff and off-key, found the German ones. [626]

When the last chord faded, she unrolled the paper she had brought: a drawing of the elevator's silos cut into rings and laid on their sides from the island to either shore, a covered footbridge of concrete and steel, owned by no bank because it touched both. [673] "We rehabilitate the elevator instead of fighting over it," she said, first in German, then in Slovene. [690] "We build bridges from silos." [695]

Anselm looked at Dorota. [699] Dorota lit her lantern at last and held it over the drawing so they could both see. [716]

"Jutri," she said, "the talks resume at the elevator." [725]

"Morgen," Anselm agreed, and above them the clock, heavy now with three kinds of iron, kept perfect time. [743]
