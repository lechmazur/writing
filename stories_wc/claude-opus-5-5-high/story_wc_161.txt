The first lie Wren Talley told in Halloran's Books was that she had once trained pigeons for a Belgian racing syndicate. [21] The second, told a week later to the same man, was that she had learned the trade from a monk in Portugal. [43] Teodor Halloran only nodded, marked something in his ledger, and let her keep renting the attic. [59] It was a narrow room above the poetry stacks, smelling of glue and foxed paper, with a single skylight that opened onto the roof where her birds lived. [87] She ran courier flights for the few clients who still wanted messages nobody could hack: a vineyard, two lawyers, a woman who wrote to her sister across the valley. [116]

The truth was plainer, and she had buried it under so many inventions that she sometimes forgot where it lay. [136] Her mother, Maren, had been a singer who never recorded anything. [147] Maren wrote her songs on whatever paper was near, receipts and napkins and the backs of seed packets, and tucked them into the books she was reading. [174] When she died, Wren, nineteen and furious with grief, sold the whole library to a stranger in a single afternoon. [194] Only years later did she understand that the songs had gone with it. [207]

The stranger, she eventually learned, had been Teodor. [215] That was why she had come, though she had not told him. [227] All winter she searched the shelves at night, shaking out volumes, finding only pressed violets and bus tickets. [245] Around her throat she wore the one thing she had kept: a chip of Mars meteorite no bigger than a lentil, set in a wire cage. [271] Maren used to say that songs were like that stone, knocked loose from one world and landing, scorched but whole, in another. [293]

In March, Teodor climbed the attic ladder with his ledger under his arm. [306] "You shake books like someone looking for money," he said. [316] "But you put the money back." [322]

She could have invented something. [327] Instead, for the first time in years, she told the truth, and it came out small and splintered. [345]

Teodor opened the ledger. [349] Every book he had ever sold was there in his cramped hand: title, date, buyer, and, where he knew it, an address. [371] "Your mother's books have a mark," he said, tapping a small pencilled M. [384] "I sold most of them the first year." [392]

A paper trail, then, running out across the city like roots. [403]

Through spring they followed it together. [409] Wren wrote to each buyer, then carried a boxed pigeon to every doorstep so the answer could fly home to the skylight. [431] Slips began returning: a verse on a laundry ticket, a chorus on a pharmacy receipt, a bridge scrawled beside a recipe for plum cake. [455] Wren sang each one aloud under the skylight so the words would have a voice again, and Teodor, below, stopped shelving to listen. [478] Outside, the lilacs in the alley opened, browned, and dropped, and the paper in her hands grew brittle faster than she could copy it. [502]

One song would not come home. [508] It was the lullaby Maren had kept in her battered Rilke, and the ledger showed the Rilke had never been sold. [529] Yet it was nowhere on the shelves. [536]

On a still August afternoon, Wren lay on the attic floor and watched the dust motes turning in the shaft of light, the way she watched them before every release to read the drafts. [570] She had sketched their swirl a hundred times, a slow clockwise spiral. [582] Today, with the skylight shut, the spiral bent toward the eaves, pulled by a breath of air rising from somewhere it should not. [605] She mapped it on graph paper, mote by mote, until the lines converged on a gap beneath the last floorboard. [625]

The board ran over open joists, above the drop to the poetry stacks. [638] Wren balanced along a beam with her arms out, the meteorite swinging at her throat like a plumb line, and pried the board loose. [662] Wedged in the cavity lay the Rilke, fallen, Teodor guessed later, from a stack he had carried up the day he bought the library. [686] Inside was a seed packet, the ink half-faded, a pressed marigold crumbling across the words. [701]

She called down to him instead of hiding it. [710] He climbed up, sat beside her in the falling light, and she sang it through once, her mother's melody blooming as the paper withered in her lap. [737]

When he asked where she had learned to handle birds, she almost said Belgium. [751] "From my mother," she said, and let the true story stand. [762]
