Nobody in Harrow Junction had ever managed to thank Odile Marsh, which was exactly how she liked it, or so she told herself. [23] For eleven years she had arrived before floodwater receded and vanished before the first casserole, routing pallets of bottled water and insulin through towns that knew her only as a signature on a manifest. [57] Gratitude was a debt, and debts meant staying. [65] The trouble was that she had started talking to vending machines. [76] So when the river finally withdrew from Harrow Junction, she did not leave. [89] She walked instead to the old railway station, a Victorian brick hulk swallowed so thoroughly by ivy that it looked like a hedge with a clock face, and she sat on a platform bench that had not seen a train since 1974. [131] In her pocket was a fragment of moon rock the size of a thumbnail, sealed in a cracked acrylic cube. [151] An astronaut's widow in Galveston had pressed it into her hand after a hurricane, saying, "You fed my whole street, so you carry a piece of my husband's sky until you find someone who needs it more." [188] Odile had tried to refuse. [193] The widow had simply closed Odile's fingers around it, the way you close a door on an argument. [211]

Now she turned the cube over and made a plan that any competent logistician would have called insane. [229] She knew how supplies moved when official channels failed: not by trucks but by rumor, passed hand to hand, each carrier adding a little freight of their own. [257] At the laundromat she told Mrs. Pruitt, loudly, that a piece of the moon would be shown at the station at dusk on Saturday, and that admission was one favor owed to a stranger. [291] "Also, I'm told it cures loneliness," she added, grinning like a woman telling a joke at her own funeral. [310] It was the only joke she had left, and she told it the way drowning people wave. [327]

The rumor chain did the rest, and it did not do it faithfully. [340] Mrs. Pruitt told the pharmacist that the moon was returning something it had borrowed. [354] The pharmacist told the vicar that a ghost train would arrive to collect unpaid debts. [369] The vicar, who was not supposed to spread such things, told the entire Tuesday choir that the railway was reopening and the council was lying about it. [396] By Saturday, three hundred people believed three hundred different things, and all of them brought something to trade. [414]

What followed was the most courteous riot in the county's history. [425] Teenagers cut the padlock on the station gate and then apologized to it. [438] The choir queued in single file to trespass. [446] A retired electrician hotwired the platform lamps, left a signed note for the power company, and was thanked by six strangers, each of whom now owed him a ladder, a pie, or an afternoon. [480] Somebody said, "Excuse me, sorry," and tore a curtain of ivy off the ticket window, revealing a timetable that still promised the 6:15 to Leeds. [505]

Odile stood on the bench and held up the cube, and nobody looked at it for long. [522] They were too busy looking at one another, trading favors, writing names on paper plates so they would remember whom they owed. [544] She had meant only to catalyze a gathering and slip away before anyone noticed the catalyst. [560]

But Mrs. Pruitt found her by the ticket window. [569] "You're the logistics woman from the flood," she said. [578] "My sister got her dialysis because of you, and I have been looking for you for three weeks." [596] Odile laughed too loudly and said she only accepted thanks in writing, in triplicate, with a stamped return envelope. [615] Mrs. Pruitt did not laugh. [620] She wrote Odile's name on a paper plate, then her own beneath it, and under that the words Sunday lunch, every week, indefinitely. [643] "That's what I owe," she said. [649] "You can't pay it off by leaving." [656]

Odile looked at the plate for a long moment, then at the moon rock, and understood at last what the widow had meant. [679] Loneliness had no cure you could carry; it had only a cure you could owe. [694] She placed the cube in Mrs. Pruitt's palm and closed the old woman's fingers around it, the way you close a door on an argument. [719] This, the town would later agree, was when the story changed: Harrow Junction stopped being the place the trains forgot and became the place that kept count. [746] Above them the station clock, stopped for fifty years, read 6:15, and somewhere down the chain a new rumor was already leaving the platform: that the logistician was staying. [775]
