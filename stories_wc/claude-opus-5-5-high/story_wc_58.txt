The last ship sailed from Carrow on the ninth of October, its lanterns shrinking over black water until the harbor held only gulls and the smell of tar. [28] Oren Vask watched it go from the seawall, his trunk still sitting in his rented room, because his map had a blank here and he had never yet left a blank behind. [60]

For eleven years he had traveled the coasts writing obituaries for the living. [73] People paid him in bread or coin to hear how they would be spoken of when gone, and most wept, and some changed. [96] After each one, he marked his map with the place the listener had begun to mend: a kitchen in Dorne, a fishing skiff, a hospital stair. [122] He believed healing had a geography, that grief loosened in certain rooms and not others, and if he learned the pattern he might find the place for himself. [150]

The blank on Carrow was the old palace. [158] Its roof had fallen in the year the royal line fled north, but the garden remained, walled and shadowed, the chestnuts dropping their split green husks onto paths nobody swept. [188] Beech leaves lay in drifts the color of old brass. [198] Frost furred the stone basins, and the last roses hung brown and heavy, still giving off a thin, stubborn sweetness. [218] It was the most beautiful place he had ever stood alone in, and he felt the beauty press on him like a hand. [241]

Marit Hale lived in the gardener's cottage against the east wall. [252] She had been a weaver for the palace, and she was the only one who had not left. [270] When he knocked, she looked at his satchel and said, "You're the one who writes the dead before they're dead." [290]

"I write what people leave behind," he said. [298]

"Then write me, because I've left a great deal." [307]

They sat on a bench beneath a copper beech, and she opened her fist. [321] In her palm lay a single thread, crimson gone to rust, fine as hair. [335]

"Weft," she said. [338] "From the throne-room carpet." [342] "The warp is strung on the loom and never moves; the weft is the thread that goes away and comes back, over and under, again and again." [369]

She had spent nine years on that carpet, and in those years her daughter, Tessa, grew from a child into a girl who stopped waiting at the palace gate. [398] The queen had promised that the finished carpet would pay off the family's debt. [412] It did, the week Tessa sailed to the mainland on the last ship of that year, believing her mother had chosen silk over her. [436]

"I never told her why," Marit said. [443] "I thought the reason would sound like an excuse." [452]

Oren's method was simple and costly. [458] He did not imagine the subject gone; he projected his own presence into the space the absence would leave, and let it speak. [481] He shifted to the far end of the bench, where a girl might once have sat swinging her feet, and he became the daughter reading this in some mainland kitchen. [511]

Then he wrote. [514]

Marit Hale is survived by a carpet no one walks on and a daughter who does not know she was the pattern. [536] Every absence of those nine years was a thread carried away so that it could return: each night at the loom bought a morning of freedom Tessa would never have to weave. [568] She stayed in the empty garden because it was the last place her daughter would know to find her. [587]

He read it aloud. [591] Marit took his hand, her knuckles rough as knotted wool, and wound the crimson thread once around both their wrists, binding them together while the chestnuts fell. [618] They sat that way until the light went violet. [627]

"You'll carry it to her," she said. [634] "In spring." [636]

He would, and he knew then that he had been wrong about the map. [650] Healing was not a point where a person stood; it was the distance crossed, the weft pulled from one edge of a life to the other, the explanation finally carried over water. [682]

That night, in his rented room, he unrolled the map and did not mark Carrow with a dot. [700] He drew a long red line from the palace garden toward the mainland, and a second, fainter one, toward a house in Wrenmouth where his own mother still set out a cup for him. [734] Then, for the first time, he began to write his own. [745]
