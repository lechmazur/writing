Oda Venn kept the last observatory above the clouds, and the clouds did not care for her. [17]
Every night the storm climbed the mountain like a drunk uncle up a staircase, cursing, flashing, knocking over the stars. [37]
She drank nettle tea warmed on the lightning rod and talked to the pigeons, who were the only colleagues who had not retired, died, or descended to sell telescopes for soup. [68]

The observatory's entrance was a portico of cobalt glass, forty panes set in iron by astronomers three centuries dead. [87]
When lightning struck behind it, the glass threw blue shadows across the cloud sea below, and in those shadows were shapes the old keepers had etched: the Heron, the Broken Wheel, the Sleeper, constellations nobody used anymore. [124]
Oda had spent eleven years scraping rust from its frame. [134]
She believed, in the way lonely people believe things, that old shadows could be taught to cast a new dawn. [154]

The radio was an accident. [159]
She had rigged a receiver from a dead seismograph to listen for weather, and instead it gave her a boy. [179]
His voice came through fragmented frequencies, chopped by static into shards: "the dawn is (hiss) not coming from (crack) the east (whine) it is coming from us." [206]
He was preaching in the valley town of Marrow, somewhere beneath the storm, to people who had not seen a clear sunrise in six years of ash-weather. [233]
His sermons were earnest and badly built, like a boat made of hymnals. [246]
Oda, who had read every eclipse log in the archive, could not stand it. [260]

So she answered. [263]
She reversed the receiver's coil and sent her own voice down on the same broken band, reading from the old keepers' notebooks, which were written half in science and half in song: "When the Heron swallows the sun, do not kneel; count to nine hundred and look up." [311]
The static ate half of every line. [318]
What reached Marrow sounded lyrically prophetic, which was more than Oda intended and exactly what the town wanted. [336]

The rise of the prophet took three winters. [344]
The boy, whose name crackled through as Tamsin, began repeating her fragments in the square, and the fragments grew congregations. [364]
Oda heard them through the hiss: first a dozen voices, then hundreds, chanting lines she had lifted from a moldy almanac. [385]
She should have been flattered. [390]
Instead she found her allegiance splitting like light through the portico. [401]
Some nights she was loyal to Tamsin, feeding him verses because his town needed hope and she needed to be heard. [422]
Other nights she was loyal to the dead astronomers, whose careful numbers were being hammered into charms and sold on strings in Marrow's market. [446]
And on the worst nights, when the pigeons ignored her, she was loyal only to the sound of her own voice coming back to her from a thousand throats. [475]

Then Tamsin made a promise she had never given him. [485]
"On the day of the Heron," his voice stuttered up the mountain, "the dawn will unfold forever, and there will be no more night." [509]
Oda checked the logs twice. [514]
The Heron was an eclipse, total, due in nine days, and it would bring the deepest dark Marrow had ever known. [535]
When it came, the crowds would break, and broken crowds look for someone to blame; the ones who had begun calling her the Cloud Voice would blame Tamsin, and the ones who loved Tamsin would climb the mountain with torches. [575]

She could have gone silent. [580]
Instead she spent nine days on a ladder with a tin of solvent, clearing the last clouded panes of the cobalt portico, while lightning split the sky and nearly split her. [611]
On the morning of the eclipse she broadcast one fragment, over and over, knowing the static would choose which words survived: "The dark is (hiss) the old shadow (crack) keeping its promise (whine) wait for the blue." [648]

The Heron swallowed the sun. [653]
In the sudden dark the storm, as it always did, came roaring, and Oda stood inside the portico and prayed to nothing in particular that it would strike. [681]
It struck. [683]
The rod took the bolt, and through forty cobalt panes the old constellations spilled across the cloud sea in blue light so enormous that Marrow, looking up into the eclipse, saw the Heron, the Wheel, and the Sleeper waking. [722]
Then the sun slid out, ordinary and gold, and no one blamed anyone. [735]

A week later, a soaked boy with a cracked radio on his back climbed through the portico, asked whether she was the Voice, and accepted nettle tea from the lightning rod without complaint. [768]
"Whose side are you on?" he asked. [775]
Oda looked at the pigeons, the storm, the boy, and the blue shadows shivering on the floor. [792]
"The morning's," she said. [796]
