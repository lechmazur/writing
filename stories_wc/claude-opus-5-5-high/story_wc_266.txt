On the veiled ridgeway, the fog came in from both seas at once, and the wise ferry guides said that was why the path had learned to whisper. [28] They did not row boats. [33] They ferried people on foot along the narrow spine of stone between the northern harbor and the southern one, a rope tied from each traveler's waist to the guide's belt, so that no one wandered off the edge into the white. [74] The rope was called the leash, and Wren had worn one for six years. [88] She was an apprentice, nineteen, and she had never walked the ridge untethered. [101] Old Ansel, who had guided longer than anyone, was gently persistent about it. [114] Every evening he held up his ticket punch, the brass one with the star stamp, and said, "Whenever you're ready." [134] The star was only for first solo crossings. [142] Every other ticket got a plain round hole. [150] Wren always answered that she was not ready, and Ansel always nodded, and neither of them mentioned her mother. [169]

Her mother had been a guide too, until one spring she bought a ticket to cross alone to the south harbor, where a ship was hiring navigators, and then never used it. [201] She stayed, and grew quiet, and died with the ship still sailing somewhere without her. [216] "Unused courage doesn't vanish," Ansel had told Wren at the funeral. [227] "It waits up on the ridge, where the fog keeps it." [238]

The leash broke on an ordinary Tuesday, at the hour the guides called the long blue. [254] Wren was walking behind Ansel with three travelers when a gust swung her against a boulder, and the old rope, frayed where it rubbed her hip, simply parted. [282] By the time she looked up, the fog had closed like a curtain. [295]

She did not shout. [299] Guides were taught that shouting in fog only tells you how lost you are. [313] Instead she listened, and after a while she heard it: the twilight vigil beginning. [327] Every evening, at the stone huts along the ridge, the guides who were not walking stood in their doorways and hummed the old shore song, low and rocking, the one mothers sang when the tide came in under the windows. [367] They kept it up until full dark, for anyone who had lost the path. [381] Hush now, the water knows the way, hush now, the stones will hold. [394]

The song came from above her, not ahead. [402] A straight line would have been quicker and would have walked her off a cliff. [417] The guides' first rule was to traverse the ridge through indirect routes: follow the sheep tracks as they switched back, keep the sound of surf on your left, trust the long way. [449] So Wren went sideways, then down, then up again, feeling each stone with her boot before she gave it her weight. [470] Her heart was loud, but the song was louder, and her feet began to keep time with it. [488]

One switchback brought her to a place she had never seen: a hollow in the rock below the highest hut, sheltered from the wind, where a cairn stood waist high. [518] Tucked between its stones were dozens of paper tickets, soft with damp, and none of them had been punched. [537] Journeys people had paid for and been too afraid to take. [548] She understood then that Ansel had not been speaking in riddles. [559] The guides had been bringing them here for years, keeping them, waiting. [571] Her mother's ticket was near the top, the ink of the name still legible: Maren Holt, north to south, single. [591] Wren held it a long time while the lullaby rose and fell above her like water on sand. [609] Then she put it inside her coat and kept climbing. [619]

Ansel was standing in the doorway of the high hut, humming with the others, a lantern at his feet. [638] He did not look surprised. [643] He had gotten the travelers to shelter, he said, and then he had simply started singing, because that was what you did for someone who had to find her own way. [674] Wren held out her mother's ticket. [680] "This isn't mine," she said. [685] "It is now, if you use it." [692] He took out the brass punch, turned the ticket so the south harbor faced up, and waited, the way he had waited every evening for six years. [719] Wren looked down the far slope, into the fog that hid the southern shore and the ships that still came there. [740] "Tomorrow," she said, "at the long blue." [747] "You already crossed alone once tonight," Ansel said, smiling, and closed the punch, and a small star of lantern light fell through the paper onto her palm. [774] Below them the guides kept singing, hush now, the stones will hold. [786]
