The doctor on the mainland gave Ilse Varga six months, and she thanked him the way she thanked the ferryman, briefly and with exact change. [25] Then she went home to the island to finish the orchard. [36] Nobody else called it finishing. [41]

The temple orchard of Saint Brannoc had stood for four hundred years inside its ring of living stone, pillars that thickened by a hair each decade, sweating lime in the rain and swallowing the lichen that settled on them. [80] Between the pillars grew figs, quinces, and old oaks, and at dusk the bats came, and the council blamed them for every split and rotting fig. [106] In spring they had voted to net the orchard and smoke out the roosts. [120] Ilse, caretaker for nineteen years, had asked for one season to try something else, and they had given it to her because she was dying and it seemed rude not to. [151] She did not mind being pitied if it bought her time. [162]

What she had was the botanical diary. [169] Every caretaker kept one, and Ilse's predecessors had filled eleven volumes with bud dates, frost damage, and grafting notes, most of it dull as tax records. [195] But the first volume, written in a cramped brown hand, contained drawings no one had bothered to understand: oak limbs bent into arches, each wound with a spiral of metal, and beside them the stone pillars rising to meet them like hands. [237] Ilse read it the way she did everything, gently and systematically, one page a night, testing each claim against the orchard before turning to the next. [263]

By the third week she understood the temple's architectural secret, and it made her laugh out loud among the quinces. [283] The pillars had never been carved. [289] The founders had grown them, wrapping copper wire around oak branches and bending them toward seams of limestone, where the copper's slow green bleed fed the stone and the stone climbed the wood. [322] Over generations the oaks became the skeleton and the rock became the flesh. [335] And the arches, the diary insisted, had been left hollow at the crown on purpose. [350] Beside one drawing the founder had written a single line: Give the bats a house and they will guard yours. [370]

Ilse checked the crowns with a ladder and a lamp. [380] Every one had been plugged with mortar, some time in the last century, by a priest who had preferred his figs whole. [402] Since then, the later volumes showed, codling moth and canker had crept upward year by year, while the roofless bats roosted wherever they could and got the blame. [430]

So she began to remodel. [435] She did not ask permission, since a woman with five months left has little use for committees. [452] She chiseled the mortar from the crowns one arch at a time, never more than one a day, so the stone would not crack from shock. [478] Where young oaks had sprung up untrained, she bought copper wire from the chandler, wrapped their branches in careful spirals exactly as the diary drew them, and bent them toward the nearest seams, starting arches she would never see close. [518] Each evening she recorded the day's work in the twelfth volume, beneath the date and the weather. [535]

The council chairman, Pell, came up the hill in July with two men and a roll of netting. [553] Ilse was on the ladder, clearing the ninth crown. [562]

"You're vandalizing a listed temple," he said. [569]

"I'm restoring it to the plans," she said, and handed down the first volume without climbing down herself. [587] "Come back at dusk and count." [593]

He came back, mostly to prove her wrong. [601] They sat together on the temple steps while the bats poured out of the reopened arches in a dark, whispering river and went not to the figs but to the moths clouding above the canopy. [636] Ilse showed him the ruined figs she had saved in a jar: the holes were small and ragged, the work of larvae, not teeth. [660]

"They were never stealing from us," she said. [668] "We evicted our own night watch and then blamed them for the burglaries." [681]

Pell was quiet a long time. [687] "And when you're gone, who finishes the arches?" [695]

"Whoever reads the diary," Ilse said, as calmly as if discussing the weather. [708] "The oaks take forty years, and the stone takes longer, and none of us were ever meant to see it done." [729]

That night she wrote in the twelfth volume: nine crowns open, four young oaks wired, council nets withdrawn. [747] Below it, for whoever came next, she added a line of her own: Treat the neighbors as tenants of the same house, because they are. [772] Then she blew out the lamp and went to sleep, unhurried, while the bats worked above the orchard. [790]
