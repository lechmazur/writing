The sign went up at dawn: CLEAN SLATES, FRESH STARTS, NO QUESTIONS. [12]

Wren Hallow had cut every slate herself from the panes the glass orchard shed each autumn, and she had set them out mindfully scattered, one against a trunk, one in a fork of branches, one half-buried in the moss, so browsers would stumble on them the way they stumbled on books. [63]

Here the books grew on the trees. [70] Glass apples, glass pears, each one ringing faintly with a story when the wind off the cloud sea moved through the orchard. [92] The whole library floated, roots and reading benches and all, on a slow white whirlpool that had no bottom anyone had found. [114]

* * *

By noon the rush was over. [120] A widower had bought a slate to forget his temper. [130] Two students had bought one each to forget a failed exam. [141] Then nothing, the long glassy hush of a first day when the curious have come and the loyal do not yet exist. [163]

Wren sat on her stool and looked at her wrist. [173] The parole band glimmered there, a thread of silver light sunk under the skin, brightening whenever her hand drifted toward a book. [195]

Six years ago she had been the best ending-thief in the orchard. [207] She would pluck a pear, pry out its last chapter with a jeweler's knife, and sell the finale to readers too impatient to wait. [231] Hundreds of stories in the orchard still rang hollow at the top, their endings sold off by her hand. [250] The terms of her release were simple: she could sell nothing that had been written. [265] So she sold blanks. [269]

* * *

What she had never told the parole board was that she knew only endings. [283] She had read ten thousand last pages and not one first one. [295] Somewhere, the old librarians said, there was a place where stories begin, a first seed from which every glass tree had sprouted, and Wren had decided, sometime in her fourth year of confinement, that if she could stand there she might learn how to give something back. [342]

The lull was her chance. [347] She took the bottle from under the counter. [355] Moonlight, bottled on the night of her release, still sloshing faintly blue. [367] Moonlight in the orchard did a strange thing: it passed through glass and lit only what was missing. [385] She uncorked it and walked. [390]

* * *

Counting. [391]

Under the bottled glow, the stripped fruit burned like lanterns where their endings should have been. [407] Wren did not look away. [412] She tallied them in chalk on her own palm, tree by tree: nine hollows here, fourteen there, thirty on the old pear by the eastern bench. [438] Quantifying absence was the only map she trusted. [446]

And the numbers leaned. [450] Every tree had more missing on the side facing inward, as if the gaps were not only her thefts but something older, a pull, a draining toward the center of the orchard. [482] She followed the rising count. [487] Twelve, twenty, forty. [490]

At the center there was no tree at all. [499] Only a clearing, and in it a circle of bare moss where the moonlight poured down into nothing, brighter than anything she had seen. [523] The greatest absence in the library. [529]

She understood slowly, the way a slate cools. [537] Stories did not begin in a seed. [544] They began in whatever space was still unwritten, and the whole orchard had grown outward from a gap. [562]

* * *

Incision. [563]

Wren knelt and lifted the slate already waiting there, the one she had placed at dawn on an instinct she had mistaken for salesmanship. [587] She drew the old jeweler's knife, the only tool the board had let her keep. [602] Her wrist flared. [605] The parole band glimmered hot, uncertain, watching. [612]

She had never made anything; she had only taken. [621] But the terms forbade selling what had been written, not writing what had never been. [636] She pressed the blade into the glass and began to incise a first line, her first ever, slow and crooked: Once, a thief learned to count what she had taken. [666]

The moonlight rushed into the grooves. [672] Somewhere behind her, a hollow pear chimed, softly, as though a missing page had been answered. [688] The glimmer at her wrist dimmed, not dark, but steadier, like a lamp turned down for reading. [705]

* * *

When she returned to the stall, a customer was waiting, turning a blank slate over in her hands. [723]

"What's it for?" the woman asked. [729]

Wren wiped the chalk tally from her palm and, for the first time that day, did not say forgetting. [748]

"Beginning," she said. [751]
