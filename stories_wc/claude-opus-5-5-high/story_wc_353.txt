The hedgehog uncurled on the ninth of March, and Ilse took that as permission. [14]
For forty years she had waited for it, some hedgehog or other, beneath the porch boards, because a hedgehog knows when the ground has softened enough to be trusted. [43]
Then she took her gouges and her boring spoons down into the siskin alder grove, where no siskins had sung since the valley emptied. [67]

She was a tree-song sculptor, the last one anyone knew of. [78]
She shaped living alders with slots and hollowed chambers so that wind moving through them would sound, low and breathy, like a far-off congregation. [102]
When the mill closed and the families left, the birds seemed to leave with them, and the valley became a place where even the creek ran under ice without comment. [132]
Ilse stayed because somebody had to make it sing. [141]

The trouble was that her trees kept splitting. [149]
She was symphonically minded; she heard every grove as an orchestra waiting to be seated, oboes in the thin trunks, cellos in the old ones, and she carved toward the chord in her head. [183]
Every winter the frost found her cuts and opened them, and every spring she walked among instruments that only rattled. [203]

This year, following a hedgehog track through the leaf litter, she found one she had not made. [220]
It stood at the grove's far edge, where the old telegraph line had once run. [235]
An alder had grown up against a rotted pole and swallowed its crossarm, and someone, long ago, had carved the whole tangle into a kind of harp. [262]
Six strands of copper wire still ran from the crossarm down to a root, and at the center, gripped by bark like a knuckle in a fist, sat a white porcelain insulator. [294]
The wires passed over it as over a bridge. [303]

The sculpture was cracked too. [308]
Ilse knelt and studied the fissures the way she might have studied a score. [322]
They were not random. [326]
Where the old maker had cut against the tree's lean, the wood had torn in long pale seams. [344]
But on the downwind side, where the chambers followed the way the alder had bowed for decades under the valley wind, the grain had closed around the cuts and healed. [374]
The cracks were a record of where the maker had listened and where he had only insisted. [391]

She sat back on her heels. [397]
She had been insisting for forty years. [404]

That afternoon she did something she had never done. [413]
She put her back against a young alder, closed her eyes, and let it move her. [429]
The sway was small, almost nothing, a slow push east and a slower return. [443]
She matched it with her shoulders, then her hips, until she could feel where the trunk wanted to give and where it wanted to hold, the way you learn a stranger's grief by walking beside them long enough to fall into their step. [486]
The chord in her head went quiet, and something else took its place, not a symphony but a single, patient line. [507]

At dusk she returned to the harp. [514]
She loosened the wires on the torn side and left the healed side alone. [528]
The insulator had been made to keep a current from bleeding into wood, and she understood now why the old maker had chosen it: porcelain did not drink sound the way bark did. [561]
It kept each note separate from the tree while letting the tree carry it. [575]
She laid two fingers on the lowest wire. [583]
She did not pluck it. [588]
She waited until the alder leaned east, and in that leaning she let her hand strum downward with it, across all six strands, as slowly as the tree itself would have done. [620]

The sound was barely louder than breath. [627]
It went down through the porcelain, down through the grain, and out into the roots of the grove, and the young alders around her, the ones she had carved and scarred, hummed faintly in answer, as if they had only been waiting for someone to move the way they moved. [677]
Ilse did not strum again. [682]
She stood with her hand on the wire and let the note thin into the dark. [698]
Somewhere above her, small and uncertain, a siskin tried one phrase, stopped, and tried it again. [714]
She held very still, the way the hedgehog had, and listened. [725]
