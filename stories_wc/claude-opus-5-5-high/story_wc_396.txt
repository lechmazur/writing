The question left Joss Harl's mouth and hung over the lighthouse orchard like frost that had not yet decided to fall. [21] "Will you light the lamp tonight, Marit, or leave our boats to the floes?" [35] Below the cliff, the ice shifted and groaned, white plates grinding against black water. [49] Old Marit Sten stood beneath her apple trees, their branches wrapped in burlap, and drew in the breath she would answer with. [71]

Ines Vell felt the breath begin, and she let her shadow go. [83]

Peacekeepers on Harrow Point wore shadows laced with tarred thread, stitched to their heels on the day they swore the oath. [104] The law was old and narrow: a peacekeeper could not argue, could not judge, could only act in the weighted breath before a reply, when the world slowed to let the heart catch up. [138] Most of her colleagues used that stretched moment to confiscate knives. [149] Ines had always wanted more from it. [156] She felt what people felt, easily and without fuss, and for eleven years that feeling had gone nowhere, pooling in her like meltwater with no channel. [182] She wanted to map it onto something she could do. [192]

Tonight she knew what Marit felt: grief, hardened into certainty. [202] Three nights ago the keeper's crystal ball, the one that read the ice and told the lamp where to point, had shattered on the orchard stones. [228] Joss's nephew Tavi had been standing over the pieces. [237] Marit's granddaughter Lise had been screaming. [243] Everyone had decided the rest. [248] If Marit answered no, the fishers would storm the tower, and the floes would take whoever the fighting did not. [268]

Ines's shadow slid down the path to Hollis Secondhand, the vintage store wedged against the cliff below the orchard wall. [288] Its front window faced the trees, and behind the glass stood forty mirrors salvaged from wrecked ships, tilted every which way, each one keeping what it saw for seven days before letting go. [321]

In her coat pocket, Ines closed her hand around the broken crystal ball fragment she had picked from the grass that night, a curved sliver no longer than her thumb. [351] Glass that had once been whole remembered its shape, the keeper's lore said, and would show where it belonged. [370] Her shadow carried its shape the way shadows carry the shape of anything held, and she laid that dark sliver against the first mirror. [394]

A glimpse: Lise, nine years old, carrying the ball from the tower to show the stars inside it to the apple trees. [416] The next mirror, angled lower: Lise at the orchard wall, leaning out over the drop to catch the ice in the ball's reflection. [439]

Ines moved her shadow from frame to frame, calmly, the way she walked frightened dogs out of traffic, trusting the fit of each piece before she saw it. [467] She did not need all forty. [473] She needed the ones that answered the sliver's pull, and one by one she began to reconstitute the night. [492]

The wall stones loosening under Lise's knees. [499] Tavi, sent up to trade oil for apples, dropping his basket and running. [512] His hands closing on her collar. [518] The ball slipping from her fingers as he hauled her back, bursting on the stones, and the girl screaming not from injury but from the sight of the drop she had nearly taken. [551] Then Tavi, a fisher's boy standing in the keeper's orchard, saying nothing while Marit ran toward them, because a boy from the ice did not expect to be believed. [580]

Ines understood then what the crystal ball had never been able to show, for all its reading of floes and currents. [601] It predicted patterns. [604] It could not predict a courageous anomaly: a boy who had every reason to stay on his side of the wall and crossed it anyway. [629]

The breath was ending. [633]

Ines drew her shadow home, and the mirrors' borrowed light came with it, settling into the sliver in her pocket until it glowed faintly through the wool. [660] The law said she could not speak for anyone. [669] It said nothing about placing a piece of glass in an old woman's palm. [683]

She did it before the breath ran out, and Marit looked down, and the reconstituted night played across the sliver in miniature: her granddaughter's knees on loose stone, a boy's hands, the fall. [716]

The air left her slowly. [721] It was not the answer she had been holding. [730]

"I'll light it," Marit said. [735] Then, to Joss, quieter: "Send your nephew up tomorrow." [744] "Without the ball I'll need someone who reads the ice by eye, and who knows when to cross a wall." [764]

Ines felt the thread at her heels go slack and warm, as if something long dammed had finally found its channel. [785]
