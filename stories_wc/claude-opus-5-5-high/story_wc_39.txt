At dawn's earliest whistle, when the wind first found the crack beneath the eaves, Silas Penn uncorked an empty jar and held it up to catch the note. [28] The loft had been a net-mender's once, before the dunes crept inland and swallowed its lower half, and now it belonged to swallows, a vixen with one torn ear, and him. [59] Sand hissed through the floorboards like a slow hourglass. [68] He corked the jar, and the whistle hummed faintly inside the glass, trapped and shining. [83] Along the rafters stood two hundred others. [90]

He had filled them over eleven years of wandering: a trawler's winch, a fishwife's cry of "mackerel, fresh," a chapel bell since melted for scrap, the laugh of a man who had died the following winter. [126] Nobody else kept such things. [131] In town he sold poems for a penny apiece, scratched on the backs of tide tables, and the children bought them for the rhymes while the old folk bought them for the words the children no longer knew. [169] Silas stood between them like a man on a sandbar, belonging to neither shore. [183] He meant to change that; he meant to become the translator between generations, the one who could carry a grandmother's meaning into a grandson's ear without spilling it. [211] He had failed at it once, with his own father and his own son, and had walked away from both before the silence between them could be named. [239]

That morning, digging a new jar-shelf into the drifted sand, his fingers struck wood. [253] He pulled out a music box, its lid inlaid with a mother-of-pearl gull, its hinges green with salt. [271] Inside, scratched in a careful child's hand, was a name: Agnes Rowe. [283] He wound it. [286] The comb plinked four notes and stuck, sour as brine. [296]

Agnes Rowe was ninety-one and lived above the chandlery, where her grandson Kit ran the shop and shouted at her as though volume could bridge the years. [323] She spoke mostly in old harbor cant now, words for weather and knots that nobody under sixty understood, and Kit had begun to call it wandering. [349] Silas had sold her a poem every Tuesday for three years. [360] She always asked for one about her mother. [368]

He spent a week gleaning. [373] From the jars he unstopped the old fishwife's cry, and in its falling cadence he heard the shape of a lullaby. [394] From the harbormaster, humming at his ledger, he caught a second phrase; from the tide sucking under the pier, the rhythm. [415] Each fragment went into a jar, and each jar went onto the rafters, glinting in the grey light like a row of patient saints. [439] Then, by lamplight, with a sail needle and the patience of a man who had nothing but time, he bent the comb's rusted teeth back into tune, one at a time, listening to the jars and matching them. [477] The sourest note he could not fix, so he did what he did with his poems when a line was too bitter: he found the nearest sweeter note and let it sweeten the phrase without lying about it. [515]

On Tuesday he climbed the chandlery stairs with the box beneath his coat. [528] Kit followed, suspicious, wiping tar from his hands. [536] Silas wound the key and set it in Agnes's lap. [546] The tune rose thin and bright, and the old woman's face opened like a window thrown wide on a summer morning. [567] She began to sing, in cant, a song about a girl waiting on the dunes for a boat named Faithful. [587] Kit looked at Silas, helpless. [592]

So Silas translated, line by line, softly, the way you carry water in cupped hands. [607] "A lull-wind means calm water," he said. [614] "The Faithful was your great-grandfather's boat. [620] She says she hid this box in the net loft the day it didn't come home, so the song would stay where she last waited." [645] Kit knelt, and when his grandmother reached for his hand, he let her take it, and for once he did not shout. [669]

Silas went back down the stairs before either could thank him. [680] It was not the redemption he had once wanted, the one with his own son's face in it. [698] It was gleaned, a handful of grain left at the edge of someone else's field, but it was enough to carry home. [720]

The next dawn, at the earliest whistle, he sat among his jars beneath the dunes, positively wistful, and wrote a penny poem for a son who might someday buy one. [750] Then he uncorked a fresh jar and caught the vixen's cubs yelping in the grass, so that someone, someday, would know how this morning sounded. [775]
