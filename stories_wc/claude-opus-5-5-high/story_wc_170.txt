The terrace at Carrow was built to echo, and in spring it echoed everything: swallows, dripping gutters, the scuff of a child's shoe. [23] Along its edge ran the prism parapet, a rail of cut glass blocks the old wardens had set there to split sunlight into sermons. [47] After rain it did something the wardens never intended. [56] Water beaded in every facet, and the low evening light came through soft and bent, laying bands of rose and violet across the flagstones and into the tall mirrors that lined the terrace wall. [90] The mirrors showed only what was. [96] That was the law in Carrow, carved above them: LET NO GLASS FLATTER. [109]

Ilse Vane came in the hush before the bells, the long held breath when the bell-ringers climbed the tower and the town waited. [132] People said she painted mirages, and people said she did not exist, and both were nearly true. [149] She carried no canvas, only a brush of fox hair and a jar of rainwater, and when she painted, she painted on the wet light itself, coaxing the parapet's colors into shapes that trembled for a moment above the stones: a ship, a doorway, a field of barley that had drowned in the flood nine years ago. [206]

Tonight a girl was waiting for her. [213] Pim Aldous sat on the parapet step with her knees pulled up, humming. [226] The tune was one every child in Carrow knew, the skipping song about the miller's three cats, but she hummed it wrong, the way Ilse had taught the children to hum it, with a small held rest after every third beat. [267] Three and a pause. [271] It was not a mistake. [276] It was a heartbeat. [280]

Ilse knew it better than her own, because it was not her own. [293] When she was six, her heart had stumbled and stopped in a fever, and Pim's grandmother, Maud, the town nurse, had held her against her chest and sung the miller's song in that broken rhythm until Ilse's small heart caught the pattern and followed it back to life. [341] It had beaten in Maud's time ever since. [349] A borrowed heartbeat, the old woman called it, and borrowed things, she said, are meant to be passed along. [368] Maud had died that winter. [373]

"My mother won't get up," Pim said, without looking around. [383] "She looks in the mirror at home and it shows her the empty chair, so she thinks that's all there is." [404]

Ilse sat beside her, perceptively calm, reading the girl the way she read weather in glass: the chewed sleeve, the careful dry eyes, the hope held so tight it had gone hard. [436] She did not promise anything. [441] Promises were a kind of flattery. [447]

"Mirrors in Carrow were taught to be afraid," she said instead. [458] "After the flood, the wardens wanted no one fooled again, so they made the glass show only what is." [477] "But what is was never the whole of it." [486] "Your grandmother knew that." [490] "She sang to things that were already gone." [498]

The echo carried the words down the terrace and back, softened, as though the stones agreed. [514] For months Ilse had been teaching the children, under cover of their games, to fold Maud's rhythm into every rhyme they knew, so that the terrace was always full of it, the mirrors steeped in it, a lullaby hidden in plain sound. [556] The wardens heard only nonsense verses. [562] The glass heard a heart that had once refused to stop. [573]

Now she stood, dipped her brush in rainwater, and laid it against the parapet until the brightest band of violet lifted free. [595] She painted it onto the largest mirror, not a picture, only a pulse of color: three strokes and a rest. [615]

"Sing," she told Pim. [619]

Pim sang the miller's cats, and Ilse sang under her, and on the last line she did what Maud had asked: she passed the heartbeat along, pressing her palm to the glass and gracing it with the rhythm that had carried her for thirty years. [664] For one terrible moment her chest was silent. [672] Then something inside her knocked, uneven and unfamiliar and entirely hers. [683]

The mirror shivered. [686] Pim's reflection stayed, but behind it the glass opened like a window onto other evenings: her mother on this terrace, thin but laughing; her mother at the window, not yet ready; a spring where barley grew again below the town. [726] None of it certain. [730] All of it possible. [734]

"Bring her here tomorrow," Ilse said, breathing carefully around her new heart. [746]

Above them the ringers laid their hands on the ropes, and as the first bell spoke, every mirror on the terrace pulsed in answer, three and a pause, until dusk and bells and glass kept one time. [783]
