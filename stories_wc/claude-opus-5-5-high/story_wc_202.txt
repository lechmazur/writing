The market hung from the chimneys of Vell like laundry nobody had come to collect. [15] Rope bridges sagged between stalls of bottled fog and quicksilver, and every gust set the whole bazaar swaying above the slate rooftops. [37] Maren walked it the way her grandmother had taught her to walk a gallery at the lighthouse: slowly, one hand on the rail, eyes on the smallest thing. [65]

She was a translator, though not of languages anyone in Vell would recognize. [78] She spoke for the painted. [83] Every portrait, every harbor scene, every crowded fairground in oil held figures who had been put there by painters and then forgotten by everyone else, and those figures lived in a thin country behind the varnish, elusively tangible, like breath on cold glass. [126] Maren could hear them only through one thing. [134]

Around her neck, on a braided copper wire, hung a curved shard of glass the size of a palm, ribbed like a seashell: a fragment of the great lens from the Harrow Point light. [168] Her grandmother Ada had pressed it into her hand when Maren was seven, the week the city pulled the tower down. [189] "When you don't know which way is true, look through it," Ada had said. [203] "Light only ever bent one direction in that glass." [212]

Between childhood and now, Maren had gathered other anchors of intuition: the itch behind her ear when a painted mouth moved, the smell of salt when a lie was near, the way her grandmother's voice came back to her in storms. [253] But the shard was the first, and the one she trusted most. [265]

Today the itch had started at the stall of Teodor Brandt, who ground pigments from rust, ash, and old letters. [285] Among his canvases leaned a large one she knew from schoolbooks: The Rescue of the Calliope, commissioned by the city fifty years ago. [308] It showed Harbormaster Quill at the prow of a longboat, lantern raised, hauling sailors from black water. [325] Every child in Vell learned his name. [332] Nobody learned Ada's. [335]

Maren lifted the shard and looked. [341] The glass pulled the painting apart like a tide going out. [352] Behind Quill's shoulder, where the storm clouds thickened, the painter had hidden a lighthouse no bigger than a grain of rice, and in its lantern room, rendered in three strokes, stood a woman with her sleeves rolled up, turning the lens by hand. [395] The clockwork had failed that night; Ada had told her so a hundred times, and a hundred times the grown-ups had smiled. [417]

Now the tiny figure turned toward her, and Maren heard the voice she translated in dreams. [433] "Six hours," the painted woman said. [439] "Six hours I turned it, so Quill could see the rocks." [450]

"Who painted this?" Maren asked Teodor, her voice unsteady. [459]

The old man squinted at the corner. [466] "Lise Farrow." [468] "She was sweet on your grandmother, they say, and the council paid her to paint a hero." [485] He shrugged. [487] "She painted two." [490]

Maren thought of the demolition, the plaque on the new seawall bearing Quill's name, the way Ada had stopped correcting people. [511] History was a painting too, and most people only looked at the middle. [524]

She reached up and began to unfasten the copper wire from around her neck. [538] For twenty years the knot had held; her fingers shook as it gave. [551] She pressed the shard flat against the canvas, over the grain-of-rice tower, the way you would set a lens back in its frame. [574]

Light came. [576]

It did not come from the sky, which was gray, or from Teodor's lamp, which was out. [593] It came from the painting, a single sweeping beam that rose from the tiny tower and passed across the swaying market, across the fog-sellers and the mercury merchants and the children on the rope bridges, and every one of them stopped. [634] In that sweep Maren was seven and twenty-seven at once, standing on the gallery beside Ada, standing here, and the instant did not end so much as widen, the way a beam seems to hang forever over the water before it moves on. [677] In the beam, everyone could see the woman at the lens. [688]

"Her name was Ada Venn," Maren said aloud, translating for the first time not from the painted to herself, but from the painted to the world. [714] "She kept the light." [718]

When the beam faded, the shard stayed where she had set it, fused to the varnish as if it had always belonged there. [741] Maren's neck felt bare, but her hands were steady. [750] She did not need the glass to know which way was true anymore. [763] Teodor was already writing a new label for the painting, and he was spelling Ada's name first. [780]
