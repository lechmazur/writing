The clouds began their migration on the first calm morning of autumn, drifting inland from the sea in long white herds, and Ilse Varro took the girl down to the lagoon. [31] It lay beneath the dunes like a dropped mirror, its floor paved with obsidian, its water so still that the passing clouds seemed to swim along the bottom. [59]

Nell was eleven. [62] Three weeks earlier Ilse had carried her out of a burning shed on the ridge road, and now the girl kept her bandaged hands tucked inside her sleeves and would not look at anyone. [96] Her mother had asked Ilse to visit, because Nell had asked only one question since the hospital: whether the skin would ever stop being ugly. [121] Ilse had not known how to answer in the kitchen. [131] She thought she might know how to answer here. [140]

She walked the shoreline the way she walked into any smoke, gracefully alert, reading the air before her feet, noting where the sand shifted and where the wind folded over the dune crests. [173] Nell trailed behind, dragging her sneakers through black sand that glittered like crushed night. [187]

"This beach was fire once," Ilse said. [194] "Lava ran into the sea and shattered, and the sea ground the pieces down for a thousand years." [212] "Now people come from the mainland just to walk on it." [223]

Nell said nothing. [226]

At the far end of the lagoon stood the old fig. [237] Its trunk was split by a charred hollow tall enough for a child to stand inside, but its crown was green, and wide leaves rocked slowly in the wind like kelp in a tide. [271]

"Twenty years ago a man burned the grove that grew here," Ilse said. [284] "Forty trees, a packing house, and most of the dune grass." [295] She set down her canvas bag. [301] "He was my father." [305]

Nell looked up for the first time that day. [314]

Ilse did not explain the rest: the trial, the letters she still wrote to him every month though he rarely answered, the way the town had looked at her until she put on a turnout coat and started running toward fires instead of away from his name. [361] Some people told her compassion for him was a kind of foolishness. [373] She had decided long ago that it was simply a direction, and she would keep walking it. [390]

From the bag she took a grafting knife, a bundle of fig cuttings wrapped in wet paper, and a spool of red thread. [413] "These came from the new orchard up the road," she said, kneeling at the rim of the hollow, where the old wound had rolled into a thick lip of living bark. [444] "Scar wood is the strongest part of a tree. [453] If you graft into it, the new branch holds." [462]

She cut a clean notch, slid a cutting into the living bark, and asked Nell to hold it steady. [481] The girl hesitated, then drew one bandaged hand from her sleeve and pressed two fingers to the wood. [499] Ilse bound the join with red thread and sealed it with wax she had darkened with a pinch of black sand, so the sun would warm it and help the wound close. [531]

"You think scars are only ugly," she said. [539] "I brought you here to prove they can sing." [548]

From her collar she drew a cord of red thread, faded almost pink, strung through a small brass coin stamped with a ship and a language neither of them could read. [579] Her father had drilled two holes in it when she was six, on a night before he became someone else. [599] She looped the thread over her thumbs, wound the coin, and pulled. [611]

It spun into a blur and hummed, a low sweet note pouring out of the two small wounds in the metal. [632] "No holes, no song," Ilse said. [638]

Then the wind came down off the dunes, and the fig answered. [650] Air moved through the charred hollow and made a deep, round tone, like someone blowing across a bottle, and above them the clouds kept migrating, their shadows sliding over the obsidian floor. [682]

Nell stood very still. [686] After a while she held out her bandaged hands, and Ilse looped the red thread over the girl's thumbs, careful of the healing skin. [710] It took four tries. [714] On the fifth, the coin sang, and the tree sang with it, and the girl laughed for the first time in three weeks. [737]

That night Ilse wrote her monthly letter. [744] She told her father about the graft, and the girl, and the song, and she folded a pinch of black sand into the envelope so he would remember what fire becomes when it is given long enough. [781]
