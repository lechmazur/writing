Every night at eleven, Pell Arnaud walked the Calloway labyrinth with a folding rule and a ledger, measuring sorrow. [19] The town had agreed long ago that grief should be accounted for like rainfall. [33] Mourners walked the hedges by day, and the boxwood grew wherever they wept, an inch for an ordinary loss, a hand's width for a child. [58] Pell's job was to trim it back and write the figures down, so the clerk could post them on the chapel door each Sunday: thirty-eight inches of sorrow this week, down from forty-four. [91] People found the numbers comforting. [96] It meant someone was keeping track. [102]

Tonight a storm had come in off the marsh, and lightning kept printing the tower on the sky, white and then gone. [124] Between flashes the moon showed through torn cloud, and wherever its light touched the wind-snapped hedges, the green stems crept back together like fingers lacing. [149] The labyrinth always healed in moonlight. [155] Pell had stopped finding that strange years ago, the way you stop finding bread strange. [170]

What he had never stopped finding strange was the yew. [180] It stood at the labyrinth's center, older than the tower, older than the town, and it was the only thing on the grounds that never grew. [206]

His wife, Marit, had kept the observatory at the top of the tower for nineteen years. [222] Three winters ago she had died of a fever in their bed below, and the morning after, Pell found her sextant lying broken among the yew's roots, its arc bent, its mirror cracked. [255] She had gone out into the cold on her last night, sick and alone, and he had slept through it. [275] Only the tree had seen why. [281]

He had applied for the maintenance post the following week. [291] The hedge nearest the yew grew more than any other, and he measured it honestly and wrote his own sorrow in the ledger without a name beside it. [319]

Tonight the storm had done him a service. [327] Visitors pinned their dreams to the hedges on slips of paper, an old custom meant to give the sleeping mind something to walk beside, and the wind had torn hundreds loose and flung them across the paths. [364] Pell gathered them the way he gathered clippings, marking where each had landed on his grid of the maze. [383] A drowned brother, a horse that could speak, a kitchen full of snow. [396] When he finished mapping the scattered dreams, the dots were not random. [408] They formed a single narrow line from the tower door to the yew, the path the wind had followed, the path a woman might walk. [433] And the line ended not at the trunk but beneath one low branch, pointing upward at an angle. [451]

He climbed the tower with the sextant in his coat. [461] In the observatory he laid it on her worktable and began, finally, to deconstruct it: the bent arc, the index arm, the cracked mirror he had never dared to lift. [491] He set each piece on the sill in the moonlight, out of habit, the way he set out a split cutting. [512] The mirror closed like a healing lip, a thin silver seam and then nothing. [526]

The storm had quieted, and the tower held its breath. [536] In the silent corridor below, a pin slid from the bundle of dream slips he had left on the landing, and he heard it strike the stone, clear as a bell. [567] In that same instant the clamp, rusted fast for three years, gave under his thumb, and he read the angle she had locked beneath it: forty-one degrees. [594]

He went down through the healed hedges with the reassembled instrument and stood where the line of dreams ended. [613] Forty-one degrees above the horizon, framed exactly in the crook of the low branch, hung the moon. [630] It had come back to the same place, as moons eventually do. [642] And in the bark of that branch, at the height of a woman's reaching hand, someone had scratched a line of small, careful figures. [666] She had written, "Moon at forty-one, Pell asleep, sorrow none." [676]

She had not gone out in despair. [683] She had gone out to take one last measurement, and the tree had watched her find the number she wanted. [703]

Pell stood a long while in the moonlight, feeling something in his chest pull closed along an old break. [722] Then he opened the ledger, found the column for the yew hedge, and wrote, in his plain maintenance hand, a zero. [743]

In the morning he would trim the boxwood as usual. [753] There would be less of it. [759]
