In the orchard district of Quincewell, the fog had not lifted for three hundred years, and the apple trees stood inside it like drowned men who had forgotten how to fall. [31] Ilse Varro made mirrors there, because in a place without distance people needed something that could show them where they ended. [52] She was known for being firmly grounded, a woman who kept her boots laced even in bed and had never once, in forty years, remembered a dream. [79] Her sister Mara was the opposite. [85] Mara dreamed so hard that her dreams sometimes slipped loose, as dreams did in Quincewell, and wandered the rows of dead trees until morning. [109] Usually they came back. [113] This one had not. [117]

For three days Mara had lain on her cot breathing slow and even, unwakeable, her face as smooth and empty as unsilvered glass. [140] Before she slept she had described it: a range of glass mountains beyond the fog, peaks so clear you could see the stars through them. [165] No one in Quincewell had seen a star in living memory. [176] Except old Teodor, the last astronomer, who had died in spring and left Mara his sextant, its arc bent and both its mirrors shattered. [200]

Ilse understood the instrument better than her sister ever had. [210] A sextant was a mirror maker's argument: one glass caught the star, another caught the horizon, and the eye laid them together until sky touched earth. [236] It measured a distance by erasing it. [243] She spent two nights at her bench recreating the broken mirrors, grinding the index glass to Teodor's old tolerances, silvering it thin as frost. [267] For the horizon mirror she did something no astronomer would have allowed: she left half of it clear, and half she backed with a sliver of the looking glass that had hung above Mara's cot since childhood. [304] That glass had held her sister's face every morning for thirty years. [316] If anything could recognize Mara's dream, it would. [324]

She went out upon the shimmer of twilight frost, the one hour when the fog thinned enough for rime to glitter on the branches and the dead orchard seemed briefly made of crystal. [357] Her boots bit the frozen grass. [363] She found the dream three rows past the old cider press. [374] It hung between the trunks like a faint mountain range folded small, all cold facets and blue light, and inside its peaks tiny points burned that she realized, with a lurch, were stars. [407] When she stepped closer, it drifted away. [414] Runaway dreams did not come to strangers, and Ilse, who had never dreamed, was the strangest thing in the orchard. [434]

So she stopped chasing. [438] She stood still and began to breathe the way Mara breathed asleep, a rhythm she knew from a lifetime of shared rooms: in for four, a small catch, out for six. [469] Her breath clouded the frozen air in time with the sleeper at home. [482] The dream paused. [485] Then, slowly, it began to breathe with her, its glass peaks brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming, until she could not tell whose lungs were keeping the time. [513]

She raised the sextant. [517] Through the clear half of the horizon mirror she saw the fog, the orchard, her own boots white with rime. [537] In the silvered half, swung down from the index glass, the mountains appeared. [550] She moved the arm by hairs, bringing the dream's lowest peak down to meet the ground at her feet, the way Teodor had once brought stars down to the sea. [580]

The instant they touched, the boundary went. [587] She felt the mountains rise around her, cold and enormous, the stars close enough to cut her, and she understood why Mara had not wanted to come home. [615] The dream's breath lengthened, pulling hers after it, toward staying. [625] Ilse pressed her heels into the frozen earth. [633] Someone had to be the horizon. [639] She held the four-count, the catch, the six, steady as a bench vise, and the mountains, finding that the ground would not follow, folded down into the silvered glass and stayed there, faintly breathing. [673]

At home she knelt by Mara's cot and tilted the mirror so her sister's slack face met the peaks inside it. [694] Two breaths, perfectly matched, and the glass went clear. [703] Mara opened her eyes. [707] "You were there," she whispered. [712] "At the bottom of the mountains, standing in the snow like you were the only solid thing in the world." [732]

Ilse unlaced her boots for the first time in years, and that night, beside her sister, she dreamed of stars, and her dream stayed. [756]
