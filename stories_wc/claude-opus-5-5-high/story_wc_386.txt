The shrine had been carved into the glacier's blue heart, and its candles lit the ice the way old starlight lights a sky, arriving long after its source had gone out. [31] Ilse Varga came there every evening after diving, her hair still stiff with salt, to read what strangers had dreamed. [51] It was the custom in Skarvik that pilgrims who slept one night near the fjord left their dreams on slips of paper tucked into the niches, because the drowned, the village said, could not visit the people who mourned them. [91] Grief shut the door too tight. [97] So the dead slipped into the sleep of strangers instead, borrowing their windows. [110]

Ilse believed it more than most, because she heard the drowned when she dove. [124] They did not speak in words down there, only in pressure and cold, a tug at the wrist, a hum inside the regulator. [147] She had taken up salvage for money, but she stayed for the other thing, the hope of bringing up something pure from all that wreckage. [172] On the wreck of the ferry Kestrel, which had gone down with nineteen souls, the hum had never stopped. [191]

The official story was simple: Captain Brask had been drinking and had steered into calving ice. [207] His widow, Hedda, still bought her bread in the next town because no one in Skarvik would sell to her. [227]

But the dream slips told another story, if you read them the way Ilse did, sorting them by who appeared at the edges. [250] A trucker from the south dreamed of a wedding and noticed a boy in a red coat standing at the reception hall window. [273] A German hiker dreamed of her grandmother's kitchen, and there was the boy again, outside the glass, shaking a snow globe. [294] Forty-one slips over three winters, forty-one cameos, and in every one the boy held the globe up to a window, and the snow inside it rose instead of falling. [323]

Snow that rises is not snow, Ilse thought. [331] It is smoke. [334]

The next morning she went down to the Kestrel's galley, where the hum was loudest, and found the globe wedged beneath a buckled stove. [358] Its glass had slumped like candle wax, and the little plastic village inside had melted into a single blackened lump. [378] Ice does not do that to a thing. [386] Fire does. [388] She knew whose it was before she scraped the base clean and read the engraving: for Tomas, from Papa. [407]

Aurel Lind had been the Kestrel's engineer. [414] He had survived; his son had not; and for three winters he had kept the shrine's candles burning with a devotion the whole village admired. [439]

She found him there that night, trimming wicks, his breath hanging in the amber air. [454] Ilse set the melted globe on the altar between two candles, and the ice walls caught its shape and multiplied it into a hundred small ruined moons. [481] Aurel stopped moving. [484]

She could have exposed him with a sentence. [492] She had seen the taped fuel line below the stove, the kind of patch an engineer makes when he means to fix it properly tomorrow. [517] She had heard, too, how quickly he had told the inquiry about Brask's flask. [531] But the drowned had not come to strangers for three winters to ask for his ruin, and Ilse had learned to weave truth carefully, one strand at a time, so it would hold weight instead of cutting. [568]

"Your son has been visiting people who never met him," she said. [580] "Forty-one of them." [583] "Always at a window, always showing them this." [591] She laid the slips beside the globe. [598] "He isn't angry." [601] "He wants the window open." [606] "He wants Hedda Brask to be able to look through it." [617]

Aurel read the slips slowly, one after another, the red coat, the rising snow, the small hand pressed to glass. [637]

"He never came to me," he whispered. [644]

"You locked the door," Ilse said gently. [651] "Grief does that." [654] "So does a lie." [658]

He wept then, the way ice weeps, without sound, and she let him. [671]

In spring Aurel stood before the inquiry and told it all, and afterward he walked the long road to the next town and knocked on Hedda Brask's door. [699] Ilse was not there to see what passed between them. [709] But that week Aurel tucked a slip into the ice, the first in his own hand: he had dreamed of an open window, and a boy in a red coat climbing through it, and snow falling, at last, the right way down. [751]

Ilse kept the melted globe on the altar, where the candles filled its ruined glass with light that had come from somewhere else, and was still, somehow, enough. [779]
