By the year the Looms began composing every song in Carrow, Pell Varrow had failed her mastery examination four times. [20] The engines hummed in their glass halls, spinning out sonnets, cathedral plans, and weather charms faster than any mage could think, and the Guild no longer needed people who were merely adequate. [52] Pell was not even that. [57] Her fire spells sputtered, her wards leaked, and the only art she had ever mastered was mist walking, a shepherd's trick for crossing fog-filled ravines that the examiners called charming before they marked her down. [92] So when the Guild needed someone to climb the Hollin Spire, they sent the one mage they could afford to lose. [113]

The spire rose from the northern ice like a frozen needle, and its summit stood so far above the clouds that no road, lift, or flying charm reached it. [142] Only the cloud sea itself touched it, and mist, if you trusted it, would hold. [157] Pell stepped onto the white and climbed for a day and a night, each footfall a small act of faith, until she came out into air so thin and bright that the stars looked scratched into the dusk. [195]

At the top waited the garden folly. [202] Old Archmage Tessaly had built it sixty years ago, a mock ruin of three broken arches in a garden of ice, a joke about how grand things decay. [230] But the central arch was whole, and its keystone was the reason Pell had come. [245] Tessaly had cut it from a stone that accommodates impossibilities. [255] Beneath the arch, snow drifted upward, frost ferns bloomed in cups of cold water, and the light of every sunset that had ever touched the spire lingered, long after the sun had gone. [288] It was a dim light, amber and rose folded into the blue of night, yet it made everything it touched unbearably clear. [310]

The Looms could recombine anything that existed, but they could not make one thing that could not be, and the Guild believed the keystone would teach them. [337] Pell braced the arch with a clumsy binding, pried the stone loose, and strapped it into the harness on her back. [358]

The folly sighed. [361] In the fading glow she saw what the light had been keeping: a chipped teacup on a bench, a child's mitten frozen to a railing, the words TESS WAS HERE scratched crookedly into the plinth. [396] The greatest mage of her century had carved her name like a bored apprentice. [410] Pell stared at it longer than she meant to, then turned and stepped back onto the mist. [427]

It held for a hundred paces. [433] Then it began to thin. [438]

Mist walking was simple, her grandmother had taught her: the cloud bears what you believe you should be carrying, and nothing else. [460] The examiners had laughed at that, too. [467] Now her boots sank to the ankle, then the knee, and below her the cloud sea opened on a mile of dark air. [490]

She thought of the Looms dissecting the keystone, generating ten thousand clever impossibilities a minute, each one perfect, each one meaning nothing, and she thought of the teacup left out for no reason except that someone had enjoyed tea there once. [531] The folly had never been about impossibility. [538] The impossible was only the stone's way of making room for the small, for a mitten and a scrawled name and a light that refused to leave because someone had loved watching it. [571]

The mist was at her waist when she began to unstrap the harness, her numb fingers fumbling each buckle like a failed exam question. [595] The keystone slid from her shoulders and did not fall. [605] It rose, patient as a moon, drifting back up the spire toward its arch, and the mist beneath her thickened into something like a floor. [630] High above, the folly caught its breath, and the old sunsets bloomed again, dim and brilliant, a coal glowing inside the ice. [652]

Pell looked at her hands. [657] A little of that light had stayed on them, faint as pollen, and it did not wash off in the cold. [678] The Guild would strip her rank for this, and she found she did not mind; she had never held much of it. [700]

On the long walk down, she tried the only fire spell she had ever half learned, and it flickered in her glowing palm, crooked and small and entirely her own. [730] She thought she might scratch her name somewhere, badly, when she reached the bottom. [744]
