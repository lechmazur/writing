The rain stopped an hour before dawn, and the lagoon lay cobalt and still, as though the storm had rinsed all its blue into one bowl. [26] Odile carried her loom down to the shore the way she had every morning for thirty-one years, a narrow backstrap loom rolled around a band of cloth as thick as a loaf. [58] The village called her the quiet poet of the shore, and called the cloth her woven dawns. [75] Each sunrise she added one row in whatever colors the sky offered, and with each row she spoke a single line of a poem, once, to the water, never writing it down. [107] It was one poem, begun the morning her husband's boat did not come home, and she had always meant to finish it. [129] Lately her hands shook, and she had begun to fear the poem would outlast her, a sentence without its period, a road washed out halfway. [154] She wanted, before that happened, to learn how an unfinished thing could still be beautiful. [169]

She anchored the far end to an old mooring post, tied the strap around her waist, and sat on the wet sand. [191] Near her knee lay a drake's tail feather, the small curled one a mallard wears above his tail, glossed green-black and bent into a hook that never quite closed its circle. [222] She picked it up and turned it in the thin light. [233]

Down the shore, two children were quarreling over a kite. [243] It was the Barros twins, Lua and Teo, each holding one handle of a two-line kite their grandfather had built, a red diamond with a tail of knotted rags. [272] Each wanted to steer, so each had seized a side, and the kite lurched between their wills like a gull in crosswind. [294] Odile watched them, warmly attentive as she was to everything on this shore: herons, tide lines, the griefs of neighbors. [314] Their grandfather had been buried in March. [321] This was the first time they had flown his kite without him. [333]

Teo yanked left, Lua yanked right, and as the sun cleared the reef, the kite string snapped with a sound like a plucked wire. [357] The red diamond wheeled once, free and astonished, then sailed out over the lagoon and settled on the water far beyond wading. [379] The twins stood holding their handles, each with a length of limp line, and began to shout that it was the other's fault. [402] Then Lua began to cry, and Teo, not knowing what else to do, threw his handle into the sand. [421]

Odile called them over. [425] She did not scold them or promise the kite would drift back. [437] She asked only if she might borrow the broken lines. [447] Suspicious, sniffling, they unwound them for her: two lengths of the same white cord, frayed where they had parted. [466]

"This is how I tend my dawns," she said, laying both cords across the warp. [481] "Some mornings the sky is all one color, but most mornings it argues with itself, rose against gray, gold against storm. [502] I don't choose between them. [507] I find where they can lie together." [514]

She passed Teo's cord over and Lua's cord under, then reversed them, drawing each until the tension matched. [532] The twins leaned closer despite themselves. [538] Where the two cords crossed, the frayed ends touched but did not join, and she left them that way. [557]

"Aren't you going to tie them?" Teo asked. [565]

Odile held up the drake's feather. [571] "The duck grows this every year and drops it every summer, and it never once closes into a ring. [590] Would you call it ugly?" [595]

Lua shook her head. [599]

Odile tucked the feather into the row beside the gap, its green curl bowing toward the frayed ends as though listening. [620] Then she beat the row down, and the light over the lagoon turned from cobalt to copper, a burnished hush settling on the water, the kite a small red coal adrift in it. [653]

"What breaks between two hands still holds the sky," she said to the water. [667]

It was not an ending. [672] It did not need to be. [678]

Beside her, Teo took his sister's hand, and together they watched their grandfather's kite ride the bright water toward the reef, unfinished, and lovely, and going on. [705]
