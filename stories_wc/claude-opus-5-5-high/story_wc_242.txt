The emergency phone in Utility Tunnel C had been installed in 1961 for track workers, and nobody at the transit authority remembered it still worked. [25] Mara remembered, because she was paid to answer it. [34] Her official title was booth operator, which meant she sat on a folding chair beside a green steel cabinet forty feet under Carroll Street and logged every call that came through. [65] Almost none were emergencies. [69] The line's number differed by one digit from a pharmacy refill service, so the calls were mostly wrong. [87] Her shift ran between the hum of streetlamps, from the moment the sodium lights above the vent grate buzzed off at dawn to the moment they buzzed on again at dusk. [118] She liked working inside that silence, where the only other sound was the trains shouldering past behind the wall. [137]

Over six years she had built friendships through wrong numbers. [147] There was Delia, a night nurse who called every Tuesday to say she had dialed wrong again, on purpose now. [167] There was Mr. Okafor, a widower who had dialed to refill his dead wife's heart pills, and who kept calling after he understood, because Mara never told him to stop. [197] There was a boy named Teo who wanted cough syrup and got, instead, a woman who could name every station on the G line backward. [222]

Strangers were easy. [225] A stranger's call had a shape: a mistake, a laugh, a little talk, a goodbye. [240] Family had no shape at all, which was why she had spent those same six years not calling her sister. [260] She had cultivated the distance deliberately, the way her mother used to cultivate basil on a fire escape, pinching off every flower before it could open. [286]

On the cabinet shelf, wrapped in a chamois cloth, she kept the reason. [299] It was a single disc from her mother's glass harmonica, a bowl of blown glass no wider than a saucer, its rim painted with a thin gold band to mark the note: G sharp. [333] After the funeral, Tova had wanted to sell the instrument, and Mara had pried this disc off the spindle and walked out with it, so that nobody could play it whole again. [365]

On slow afternoons she wet her finger from a thermos cap and ran it in a loop around the rim until the glass began to sing. [391] The tunnel caught the note and carried it down the concrete in both directions, so that for a minute the whole place sounded melodically haunted, as if her mother were humming somewhere past the junction boxes. [427]

Her wrong-number friends had all heard it. [434] Delia said it helped her sleep. [440] Teo recorded it once and looped it as a ringtone. [450] Mr. Okafor asked what it was for, and Mara said it was for endings, because that was what she had been doing down here, teaching people how to hang up. [480]

In March he called to say he had cancelled his wife's prescription at last, and that this was his final call. [501] "You taught me that part," he said. [508] "But nobody teaches the next part." [514]

"What next part?" [517]

"An ending is only half a thing, Mara. [525] Somebody has to teach it how to begin." [533]

Then he gave her a number, and it was not a wrong one. [546]

Her friends, it turned out, had talked to each other. [556] Teo had posted his ringtone, and a woman in Queens who restored antique instruments had written underneath that she knew that exact G sharp, because it was missing from the harmonica in her living room. [591] Delia had messaged her. [595] Her name was Tova. [599] Mr. Okafor had then made the call Mara never would. [609] Tova had never sold the instrument. [615] For six years, Mr. Okafor said, Tova had been playing their mother's waltz with a hole in it, skipping the missing note every time the melody came around. [643]

Mara sat with the receiver against her collarbone while the lamps above the grate began, faintly, to hum. [661] Her shift was ending. [665] She unwrapped the disc, wet her finger, and let it sing once more in the tunnel, a clean loop of sound with no gap in it. [691] Then she dialed the number slowly, correctly, each digit placed like a note. [704]

When Tova answered, Mara did not say hello. [712] She said, "I have our G sharp," and heard, on the other end, her sister begin to cry, which sounded to her exactly like a door opening. [739]
