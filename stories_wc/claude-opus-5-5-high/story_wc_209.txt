Ines Varga was known for murals so vivid that city councils asked her to tone them down, so the letter inviting her to paint the amber pavilion in autumn surprised her. [31] The pavilion stood above an old ice cellar at the edge of the Halvard estate, its walls glazed in honey-colored glass that turned every afternoon to syrup. [58] Her grandmother Marta had tended its night garden for thirty years. [69] Ines brought the shoebox of postcards Marta had mailed her through childhood, when Ines was a girl who could not sleep and wrote to her grandmother by flashlight. [97] Every card ended the same way: "The moonflowers opened again tonight. [108] I will tell you why when you are older." [117] Marta died before Ines was old enough, or before either of them admitted she was. [132]

The caretaker, a stooped man named Bram, lifted the cellar hatch to show her the last of the winter snow, packed in straw each January and meant to last until the leaves fell. [165] Its meltwater trickled through clay pipes into the beds, he explained, and Marta had sworn the flowers drank it like a promise. [187] "When the snow is gone, they stop," he said. [196]

Ines was not a woman who announced her intentions; she was quietly driven, the kind who kept working after everyone else had gone home. [220] By day she primed the long east wall in the burning cobalts and vermilions that had made her name. [239] By night she sat among the beds with a lantern and the postcards, watching the white trumpets unscrew themselves toward the dark, trying to understand why anything would choose to open when no one was looking. [275]

The pavilion's west end held a salvaged cathedral window, a tall lancet of etched glass Marta had installed herself. [294] In daylight it showed only frosted lilies, pale and ordinary. [304] Each week the drip from the cellar slowed, and each week Ines sorted the cards again, until on one she noticed a line she had read past a hundred times: "Look through my window before the birds." [341]

On the night Bram said the snow was down to a single gray fist, she stayed until the lantern guttered. [361] Then came the dawning hush, that minute when the crickets have stopped and the birds have not begun, when the whole world seems to hold its breath on someone's behalf. [391] Faint light slid low across the lancet, and Ines saw that the glass was not one pane but several, sandwiched, each etched with a different design. [417] Only at this angle, in this thin gray light, did the hidden layers align. [431] The lilies dissolved into moths, dozens of them, soft-winged and drinking from open moonflowers, and beneath them a small girl sat awake with a flashlight in her lap. [459] Along the bottom, in Marta's cramped hand, the deepest layer read: "They open for the ones who come in the dark." [480]

Ines sat down on the cold tiles. [487] She thought of every night she had lain rigid in her childhood bed, ashamed of being the only one awake, and of the postcards arriving like small lanterns, patient, never scolding. [518] Marta had never only been describing flowers. [525] She had been telling a sleepless child that there was nothing wrong with opening late, that some beauty exists because something is out there, awake, looking for it. [553] Ines had spent her whole career shouting in color, she realized, so that daylight people would finally see her. [572] Now she had to reckon with the girl in the glass, who had never needed to shout at all. [591]

The birds began. [594] Below the floor, the last of winter's snow let go with one small sound, and by the next evening the moonflowers stayed shut. [617]

Ines scraped the cobalt from the east wall. [625] Over the following weeks she painted it in umber, pewter, and the bruised violet of four in the morning, a garden whose blossoms could only be found by holding a lamp close. [657] Bram said visitors would complain it was too dark. [666] Ines tucked the last postcard behind the lancet's frame, where the dawn would find it, and told him the right ones would bring their own light. [692] Then she stayed, as she always would now, until the hush. [703]
