The Marguerite had not flown properly in forty years, but she still floated, tethered above the salt marsh like an old aunt who refused to leave a party. [28] Tamsin Reel, apprentice to the Cartographic Office, lived in her gondola alone. [40] Alone was not quite the word. [46] Every evening the marsh-cutters shouted up news through a brass horn, the post-boat rang its bell, and a hundred thousand starlings came home to roost in the rigging, gossiping so loudly that Tamsin answered them. [81] She had company in every direction and not one person within reach. [93]

Master Holloway had posted her there with a single instruction: note everything, because the world was finished and the Office needed something left to find. [118] Every coast had been drawn, every mountain named. [126] Tamsin did not believe him. [131] She wanted to chart the next great discovery, and she suspected it was hiding somewhere nobody bothered to look, the way lost buttons hide in plain sight. [158] So she was diligently curious about the airship itself, which everyone else had stopped seeing. [173]

On the promenade deck, beneath decades of soot, lay a hopscotch board painted for the children of first-class passengers. [192] In a steward's locker she found a polyglot's phrasebook, the name Ilse Varga, ship's interpreter, inked inside its cover. [211] Only one page was dog-eared, folded so sharply it had worn white. [223] It held one question rendered in eleven languages, Please, which way to the lantern room, and in the margin, in faded pencil: ask the children. [248]

Tamsin checked every deck plan she had copied from the Office archive. [260] None showed a lantern room. [265] The Marguerite had once been famous for lamps that burned without fuel, fed by glowcells that drank sunlight all day and spent it all night. [290] When she was retired, nobody could find the reservoir, and the engineers decided the light had leaked away. [308] Tamsin thought light, like people, did not leave so easily. [318]

She swept the hopscotch board with a goose feather. [327] Its ten squares were numbered in ten scripts, surely Varga's way of teaching bored children to count. [344] Tamsin lay on her stomach with a lens and a notebook and began to note what the dust concealed. [363] The paint was worn through in only four squares, as if generations of small feet had landed there and nowhere else. [384] The scuff marks showed the order they were struck, three and seven going up, nine and one coming back. [403] Varga had taught a game that was also a route. [413]

On the deck plans, rib thirty-seven met the keel beside storeroom ninety-one, marked empty. [427] It was also, she realized with a laugh that startled the post-boat's bellman below, exactly where the starlings landed every night. [448] For weeks she had watched their murmurations pour across the sky like spilled ink and collapse onto the aft rigging, always the same spot, and she had written warm? in her notebook without knowing why. [483] Birds know where a house keeps its heat. [491]

Storeroom ninety-one held mops, a cracked bucket, and a back wall that rang hollow when she knocked. [508] Behind the panel, a narrow ladder climbed into dark, and at the top was a door stenciled in eleven languages. [528] She read them aloud, one after another, the way Varga must have taught the children, and felt less alone with each. [549] Lantern room. [551]

Inside, rows of glass cells lined the walls, and every one of them was full. [566] Forty years of sunlight hung there, amber and patient, humming faintly, a whole lost summer that had never been spent. [586] The hull collectors had kept drinking the sun all those years; only the lamps had been disconnected. [603]

Tamsin found the master valve, hesitated, and turned it. [612]

Every porthole of the Marguerite woke at once. [620] Below, the marsh-cutters dropped their poles, and the brass horn filled with voices asking what on earth she had done. [640] Around the ship, the evening murmuration broke and reformed, a hundred thousand birds turning gold as they wheeled through the light, as if the whole sky had come to the party. [671]

Tamsin sat on the hopscotch board, alone and surrounded, and opened her notebook. [684] She drew the ladder, the door, the cells, the route a child could hop. [698] Then she folded down the corner of the page, the way Varga had, and wrote to Master Holloway: The world is not finished. [721] It is only dog-eared, waiting for someone curious enough to open it where it was marked. [737]
