In Tallow Reach, the houses hung like seedpods from the upper arms of the mangroves, and every one of them swayed. [21]
Rope bridges creaked between them, and far below, the tidal flats breathed in and out twice a day. [39]
The villagers had a name for the single held minute when the water neither rose nor fell: the turning of the tide's thought. [62]
In that minute the fog thickened, the birds went silent, and old people swore they could hear the forest deciding something. [83]
Wren Asholt could smell it deciding. [89]
Her nose was the sharpest in three generations of Asholt herbalists; she could name a fever from across a bridge, tell ripe salt-plum from rotten by a single breath, and catch rain an hour before it fell. [126]
It was a gift that made the swaying unbearable. [135]
Every gust carried a new flood of scent through her window, cedar smoke, bat musk, brine, lamp oil, and she lived dizzy with it, always leaning toward the next thing. [165]
She was ambitious in the way dizzy people often are, hungry for something that would finally hold still. [183]
That spring, the thing was a seat on the Canopy Council, which had stood empty of an herbalist since her grandmother Oda died. [206]
The council would grant it to whoever brought the village something new. [218]
Wren meant to bring them a bloom no one in the canopy had ever grown. [233]
She had traded a winter's worth of cough syrup to a southern boatman for it: a single corpse flower seed, heavy as a river stone and wrapped in waxed cloth. [263]
Everyone knew what the flower would smell like when it opened. [274]
Her mother laughed until she cried. [280]
Wren planted it anyway, in a pot of mangrove mulch, and fed it every tonic she knew. [297]
Each night she journalized its progress the way Asholts always had, on the inside of wrapping paper. [314]
It was a family custom older than the bridges: every remedy left their house folded in brown paper, and on the hidden inner face they doodled something, a leaf, a joke, a warning, so that whoever unwrapped a poultice also unwrapped a small private picture. [359]
Wren's drawings that spring were all jagged lines: pot, soil, nothing, nothing, nothing. [372]
The seed sat in the swaying house like a held grudge. [383]
In early summer, hunting for paper, she opened her grandmother's old trunk and found a bundle of wrappers Oda had never given away. [406]
Their insides were covered in faded pencil. [413]
A heavy seed, drawn many times. [419]
A pot tied to a branch, left to swing. [428]
Beneath every sketch ran the same wavy line, like water under a floor, and along it, in tiny letters: plant at the tide's thought, then stop pushing. [455]
On the last wrapper, Oda had drawn a small woman sitting cross-legged in a swinging house, eyes closed, nose tilted up, smiling. [477]
Wren realized she had never once seen her grandmother sway-sick. [487]
So she waited for the turning. [493]
When the fog came and the birds hushed, she replanted the seed, hung the pot from the eave on a length of twine, and sat down beneath it. [521]
She did not reach for the next scent. [529]
She let them arrive, brine, smoke, wet bark, and pass, the way the tide below arrived and passed, and under all of them she found something she had never bothered to notice: a slow, green, steady smell, the forest itself, running beneath the noise. [573]
The house swayed, and for the first time she swayed with it. [585]
She sat like that every tide's thought after, all summer and through the wet season. [600]
Her doodles softened into long, looping lines. [607]
The seed split without her noticing, and the shoot climbed toward the light as if it had always intended to. [627]
It took four years to bloom, and by then the council seat had gone to a rope-maker with a clever knot. [648]
Wren found she did not mind. [654]
When the great purple spathe finally opened, the stench rolled across Tallow Reach like a dead whale, and the whole village fled to the far bridges holding their noses. [683]
Wren, who could smell a fever across a bridge, stayed. [693]
She breathed it in, all of it, and let it pass, and beneath the rot she found the same green current, patient and unbothered, carrying everything. [719]
That night she wrapped a sleeping draught for a restless child across the bridge. [733]
On the inside of the paper she drew a small girl in a swinging house, eyes closed, and under her, one long wavy line. [757]
Then, because the tide was turning, she folded it shut and let the house rock her quiet. [774]
