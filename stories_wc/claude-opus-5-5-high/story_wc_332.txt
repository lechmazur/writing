On Hollis Skerry, the library opened at two in the morning and closed when the gulls woke. [17] Ines Marr had kept those odd hours for eleven years, shelving tide tables and drowned men's diaries for fishermen who could not sleep and widows who would not. [45] The whole archipelago whispered at night, and everyone knew why: dreams slipped loose from sleepers and blew between the islands, snagging in the reeds, murmuring in voices that belonged to no one awake. [78] Most people let their runaways go. [84] Ines could not. [87]

For six weeks after her mother's funeral, she had dreamed the same thing every night: her mother in the green skiff, oars shipped, holding up a brush and laughing at a joke Ines never heard. [122] Then one night the skiff was empty, and after that there was no dream at all, only a draft where it had been. [145] Now, walking home along the causeway before dawn, she sometimes heard her mother's laugh in the sedge, three islands off, going farther. [167]

The library's oldest ledger, a salt-stiff folio in the locked case, said a runaway dream could be recalled only in the hush of improbable beginnings, the minute before sunrise when nothing has happened yet and anything still might. [205] It needed a shape to come home to, the ledger said, and the shape had to be written in the dreamer's own longing. [228]

Ines had only one thing of her mother's that felt like a sentence: the tin watercolor palette, twelve pans worn into uneven craters. [251] Her mother had painted nothing but the sea for forty years, yet the cobalt was barely touched while the rose and the ochre were scraped to bare metal. [279] It made no sense for a painter of water. [288] It made sense, Ines realized one sleepless shift, for a cipher. [299] Her mother had labeled each pan in a cramped hand, not with pigment names but with numbers, and the numbers matched the shelf marks of the library's own catalogue. [328] Rose was 910.4, travel accounts of the mainland. [336] Ochre was 306.87, the letters of mothers to children who had gone away. [349] The cobalt, local history, was almost new. [356] Her mother had not been painting the sea at all; she had been painting the distance to the daughter who left for twelve years and came back to take a job where she would never have to meet anyone in daylight. [397]

Ines did not paint with brushes. [403] She painted with light, the way her mother had once taught her on the jetty: a camera on a long exposure, a pocket torch swung through darkness, lines that existed only in the photograph. [437] So on a clear morning she closed the library early, carried the palette and her tripod to the eastern point, and wet every pan with seawater until the old colors bloomed. [468] The whispers were loud there, a crowd of strangers' dreams hissing in the grass. [482] She opened the shutter as the horizon paled. [490] Holding the palette beside the lens, she tilted it until the first sun struck the wet pans and flung rose and ochre into the frame, and with her torch wrapped in cobalt-washed paper she traced her mother's unspoken sentence across the dark water: the long road out, the letter never sent, and then, slowly, a line of blue that curved back toward shore. [553]

The whispering stopped. [556] In the silence she heard, close and unmistakable, the creak of oars in their locks. [571] She did not turn around. [576] The ledger had warned that a recalled dream must be let into the picture, not looked at. [593] When the shutter closed, the sun was fully up, and the point was only grass and gulls. [610]

She developed the film that night in the library's back room, between visits from insomniacs. [625] The print showed the eastern water on fire with rose and gold, and the blue line curving home, and in the middle of it, where the light was brightest, a green skiff with no one in it, oars shipped, waiting. [665] For a moment the emptiness hurt worse than the dream's leaving. [676] Then she understood what the brush in the dream had been pointing at all those nights: the empty seat was not an absence but an invitation, kept open all along. [705]

She catalogued the print under 306.87 and slid it between the unsent letters. [718] The next morning, for the first time in eleven years, Ines left the library door open past sunrise, and sat on the step with the palette open on her knee, letting the daylight vitalize what the dark had kept. [758] That night the dream came home: the green skiff, empty and full of morning, and Ines climbed in and took the oars. [780]
