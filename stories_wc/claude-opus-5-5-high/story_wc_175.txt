Oriel Vance arrived at the grotto the way she always did, with a bedroll, a spool of hemp cord, and no intention of staying past the thaw. [27] The cave opened on the mountain's eastern face, high enough that the clouds lay below it like a second floor, and inside it everything was green: ferns dripping from the ceiling, moss thick as carpet, a spring that ticked into a stone basin. [70] Her grandmother had called them rooted travelers, the women of their line, who planted themselves for one season in a place and then pulled up clean. [96] Oriel preferred plainer words, but she kept the habit. [105]

This autumn was different, and everyone below the clouds knew why. [116] A single monarch butterfly, tagged and tracked and wept over on every screen in the valley, was flying south, the last of its kind anyone could find. [143] Its route crossed the pass above the grotto. [151] The ministry had sent a young man named Teo with a camera and a satellite phone to record the moment it passed. [173]

He found Oriel on the ledge outside the cave mouth, lashing birch poles into a tall triangular frame. [191] "Is that for the butterfly?" he asked. [198]

"It's for whoever reads it," she said. [205]

Her family had built these frames for two hundred years, one each migration, hung with dyed cloth the color of milkweed blossom, a signal that said water here, shelter here, you are expected. [238] Butterflies could not read, she told him before he could say it; but they could see pink against gray rock from a mile off, and they came. [265] The meaning and the mechanism were the same thing, looked at from two sides. [279]

Then she lifted the old cord from its peg inside the cave. [291] It was knotted along its whole length, a knot for every year the frame had gone up, and her grandmother had tied them to a counting song, one knot on each fourth beat, so the cord held both a tally and a tune. [334] Near the end the spacing changed. [340] The knots crowded together, then stopped mid-phrase, where her grandmother's hands had failed three winters ago. [356] Oriel had never finished the verse. [362]

Woven into the last knot was a moth wing, brown and dusted with silver, fragile as ash. [379]

"What's that one for?" Teo asked. [385]

"The moths," Oriel said. [389]

Every spring and fall, clouds of them had crossed this pass at night, millions, feeding on the same flowers, sleeping in the same grotto. [413] Nobody had tagged them or named them on television. [422] They had thinned and vanished without anyone below the clouds noticing, and her grandmother, who had noticed, spent her last season knotting their wings into the count so the cord would remember what the records did not. [459]

Teo lowered his camera. [463]

They waited through two days of wind. [470] On the third morning the sun came up under them, turning the cloud floor rose and the wet ferns faintly pink, and the whole grotto held so still that Oriel could hear the spring counting its drops. [507] The monarch came over the pass alone, orange as a struck match, and dropped onto the dyed cloth as if it had been told. [531] It opened and closed its wings, slowly, in the cold. [541] Teo filmed without breathing. [545]

Oriel took up the cord and began the song where her grandmother had stopped, her voice low, one knot on each fourth beat. [568] The verse wanted a closing line, a final knot to seal the tally, and she could feel her fingers reaching for it. [590]

She stopped. [592]

A closed cord meant a finished history, and she did not know that this one was finished. [609] Somewhere there might be a moth nobody had counted, a monarch nobody had found. [623] She tied the knot off-beat instead, a hitch that would hold but could be untied, and left the song hanging in the pink air. [647]

The monarch lifted, circled the frame once, and went south into the cloud. [660]

That evening Teo asked what he should put in his report. [671]

"Put the moths in," Oriel said. [677] "Put in that someone was keeping count." [684]

Weeks later, at a rose-colored dusk, a single brown moth settled on the faded cloth, and Oriel sat beside it until dark without moving, letting the silence keep the beat. [714]

In spring she took the frame apart pole by pole, as her line always had, and carried the cord down the mountain with its open end tucked against her palm. [744]
