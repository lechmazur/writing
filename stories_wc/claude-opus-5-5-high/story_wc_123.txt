Ines Varga had been followed home from the barometer tower, and she knew it the way she knew a pressure drop: by a tightening behind the ears. [27] She did not turn around. [32] She let herself through the iron gate, crossed the courtyard where the fig tree held the last copper light, and sat on the bench to unlace her boots as if nothing in the Weir Quarter had changed since morning. [71] Nothing had, officially. [74]

Three years ago the fever had come up the river, and the Quarter's council had chained its own bridges shut. [94] The Crown physicians had declared the fever finished eighteen months later, but the chains stayed, and the council explained why every week in the parish bulletin: the roads were flooded, the storms were worsening, travel was unsafe. [131] Outsiders called it the rogue zone. [137] Ines called it home, and she called the storms nothing at all until she had measured them. [154]

For twenty-two years she had climbed the tower at six, noon, and six, and written pressure, wind, rainfall, and cloud in a ledger no one ever asked to see. [183] She liked that no one asked. [189] A true number written in an empty room, she believed, was the only kind that could be trusted, because it had no audience to please. [214] Solitude was not her loneliness; it was her discipline. [223]

A soft clatter came from the roof of the well-house. [233] A grey pigeon had landed there, ragged from wind, a brass tube strapped to one leg. [249] Carrier birds were forbidden in the Quarter; the council had wrung most of them in the first winter. [267] Ines was curious, but she was curious the way she was about clouds, which is to say she wanted evidence before opinion. [289] She did not lunge. [293] She rose and moved sideways along the courtyard wall, one careful step at a time, keeping the gate in the corner of her eye and the bird in the center of it, until she could lift it from the tiles without startling it. [336]

The message tube was warm from its body. [344] Inside was a curl of onionskin covered in columns of figures. [355] She recognized the format before she recognized anything else: date, hour, barometer, gust, rainfall. [369] Her ledger's format. [372] Her tower. [374]

Reading down, the numbers looked plausible, the kind of gales that would make any traveler stay home. [391] So she did what she did with a confusing sky and read across instead, row by row, laying each line beside the memory of her own pages. [418] The pressures were hers. [422] The dates were hers. [426] But every rainfall figure had been doubled, and every gust had gained ten knots, and a storm she had recorded as a mild October squall now tore roofs from the fish market. [458] At the bottom, in a stranger's hand, someone had written: Is this true? [471] Below that, in a different ink: Coastal Office, Harwick. [480]

The gate hinge whined. [484]

Ines did not need to look to intuit who stood there; she had seen the ink stains on his cuffs a hundred times across the council table. [511] Pell, the copying clerk, collected her ledger every quarter to make the bulletin's fair copy. [526] He was nineteen and had followed her from the tower with the gait of someone rehearsing a speech. [544]

"They told me to add to it," he said. [553] "My mother kept her loft on your well-house before the fever," he said. [566] "I hid one bird when they killed the others, and I sent Harwick the bulletin." [581] "This is what came home." [586]

Ines looked at him the way she looked at a sky that had not yet decided. [602] She could march to the council; she could burn the paper; she could do nothing, and keep her ledger clean in its drawer while the chains rusted on the bridges. [632] The straight way ended at the council, and the council owned the river. [645]

Instead she went inside, fetched her ledger, and returned to the bench, where she copied her true figures onto the back of the onionskin in the smallest hand she owned. [675] Pell held the lamp and did not speak. [683] When she finished she signed it, dated it, and rolled it back into the tube. [698]

"A record is only honest if it is honest when no one is watching," she said, handing him the bird. [718] "So is a sender." [722] "Release it after I have gone inside." [729]

She climbed the stairs without looking back, lit no candle, and sat by the window. [744] Below, the fig leaves stirred; the air had the faint metallic weight that meant weather before morning. [761] After a long while she heard wings. [768] She opened the ledger and wrote, in the column for remarks: Pressure falling, rain by midnight, one bird aloft, eastbound. [788]
