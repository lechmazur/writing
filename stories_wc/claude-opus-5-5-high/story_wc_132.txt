The third storm of the season took down the trellis at two in the morning, and Marta Heale heard it go the way she heard most things now, through the wall of her sleep, like a door closing in another house. [41] She did not get up. [46] Thirty-one years of running Heale Nursery had taught her that nothing in a garden could be saved in the dark. [66]

By morning the rain had thinned to a drip, and the old cedar trellis lay folded across the path, its lattice furred green with moss, the clematis still threaded through it like a net pulled from the sea. [104] Her grandmother had built that trellis. [110] Marta had repaired it every spring, measuring each replacement slat against the originals, tying every stem with soft twine at the angle she had recorded in her notebooks. [138] There were eleven of those notebooks now, shelved above the potting bench, full of how tendrils found wire, how beans spiraled clockwise, how a morning glory would turn its whole body toward a stake it could not yet touch. [177] She had started them to study plant behaviors, but somewhere along the way they had become a record of her own insistence. [199]

She knelt, and her knees told her about it. [208] Her hands told her more. [213] The arthritis had moved into her knuckles over the winter, and now she could not close her fingers around a hammer without the bones grinding like wet gravel. [241] She would reconstruct it anyway, she decided, slat by slat, exactly as it had been. [256]

Then she saw the leaf. [261]

It was a linden leaf, heart-shaped and toothed, blown in from the great tree across the road, and it had snagged on the rose canes beside the path. [289] Sometime in the night, after the trellis fell, the clematis had reached for it with the bent stalk of one of its own leaves and wrapped the linden's stem twice, tight as a ring, and already a new shoot was leaning toward the roses. [333]

Marta sat back on her heels. [339] In thirty-one years she had never once written down what a vine did when no one told it where to go. [360] She had never let it. [365]

She fetched a blank notebook, the twelfth, and wrote the date and a single line beneath it: Support lost; subject has found its own. [389] She looked at the words for a long time. [398] Then she went inside and called her nephew. [406]

Daniel lived four miles away and had offered, twice since the winter, to help with the heavy work, and twice she had told him the nursery ran fine the way it ran. [438] She was not a woman who asked for things directly; she came at them sideways, the way a vine comes at a wall, but she always meant what she arrived at. [469]

"The clematis has made a decision," she told him, when he picked up. [482] "It seems to want the roses, and I am curious whether it would like a second pair of hands, since mine have become a bit of a disappointment to it." [512]

There was a pause on the line. [519] "I'll bring posts," Daniel said. [524]

He came that afternoon, between squalls, with a truckload of rough locust posts and a coil of galvanized wire. [543] Marta had drawn a diagram of the old trellis, every slat to scale, and she held it out to him and then, with some effort, folded it back into her pocket. [574] "Put them where the vine is going," she said. [583] "Not where it was." [587]

So they did it backward from how she had done everything. [598] They lifted the mossy cedar lattice off the path and leaned it against the roses rather than restoring it, and they set the locust posts along the line the new shoot was leaning, following the plant instead of leading it. [638] Daniel strung the wire loosely, and when she reached to tighten it, she made herself stop. [654] It was a reconstruction, she supposed, though nothing like the one she had planned; her grandmother's slats were still in it, only arranged by a different intelligence. [681]

The storms kept coming through September. [687] After each one Marta walked out to the mossy trellis while it was still dripping and wrote down what had moved, what had held, what had reached for something new. [717] Daniel started coming by on Thursdays without being asked, and she started leaving the gate unlatched without saying so. [736]

The linden leaf browned and curled and finally crumbled, but the clematis kept its grip on the empty place where the stem had been, a small coil around nothing, holding the shape of what had helped it change. [774] Marta left it there, and in the twelfth notebook she wrote: Still climbing. [787]
