After the tide pools emptied, the town of Saltmere began to walk in its sleep. [15] It happened every ninth year, when the sea drew back from the red canyon mouth and left the rock basins dry as offering bowls. [39] Grandmothers and fishermen and children rose from their beds with open, unseeing eyes and drifted up the stone stairs into the labyrinth their ancestors had carved into the canyon walls four centuries before. [72] The precedent was old and simple: bar the doors, tie the sleepers to their bedposts, and wait for the water to return. [94] The labyrinth was a ruin, the elders said, a place where the old priests had gone mad, and no good came from following the dreaming feet. [120]

Nell Varrow, who mended nets on the wharf and hummed verses to the knots, had never trusted a rule that required rope around a child's ankle. [146] Her brother Tam had been tied like that nine years ago, and he had screamed himself raw against the cord until his heart gave out before morning. [173] Since then she had woven every net with a gap in its weave, a small door, she called it, for anything that needed to leave. [198]

This year she carried something else in her apron: a copper illustration plate she had pulled from the silt beneath the pilings when the water fled. [224] It had once printed a page of some forgotten storybook, and though green with age, its engraving still showed a woman standing at the labyrinth's heart, a needle raised, a thread spilling behind her through the corridors like a river. [264] Around the border, in letters reversed for the press, ran a single line: Where the dreamers cross, the true way is sewn. [286] Nell read it three times in a shard of mirror and then did what no one in Saltmere had done in four hundred years. [310] She unlocked the doors. [314]

On the first night, she let the sleepers go and followed with a spool of pale linen thread and a sailmaker's needle. [336] They wandered in separate lines, old Marta turning left at the carved heron, the Pell twins climbing a stair that ended in sky, the baker pressing his palms to a wall of rust-colored stone. [370] Where two paths crossed, Nell drove an iron pin into a crack and stitched the thread around it, tight as a hem. [392] Where three crossed, she sang to the knot. [400]

Each dawn, the sleepers returned to their beds unharmed, and each dusk the elders shouted at her across the wharf. [420] She answered them in rhyme, which made them angrier. [429] "Tie a dreamer, break his heart; follow him, and learn his art," she told them. [444]

By the sixth night, the pale threads had become a pattern, and the pattern had become a single route that wound past every dead end and false stair toward the center of the canyon. [478] No single sleeper walked it whole; each knew only a fragment, as if the town had been dreaming the same map in pieces for generations. [503]

On the seventh night, Nell walked the sewn route herself, awake, her hand trailing the thread. [519] The corridors narrowed and then opened into a round chamber where no one had stood since the priests were driven out. [540] Wild orchids grew there, violet and trembling, fed by a seep of fresh water that dripped from the red stone like slow tears. [563] Their scent was soft and heavy, and their petals were the color of bruises healing. [578] On the walls, carved in the same hand as her copper plate, were hundreds of names, and beneath each a small chiseled seam, as though the stone itself had been embroidered. [609]

Nell understood then that the labyrinth had never been a ruin. [620] It had been a garden of mourning, a place the old town brought its grief, and the sleepwalking was only grief remembering its way home. [645] The precedent of rope had broken that road in half. [655]

She knelt, took out her needle, and with its point scratched a new name into the stone: Tam Varrow. [674] Beneath it she stitched the last of her linen through an old iron ring, binding the whole route to the heart. [695]

When the tide returned three days later, the elders came up the stairs, awake and angry, and followed her thread. [715] They reached the chamber in silence. [721] Old Marta found her husband's name and wept among the orchids, and the others began, one by one, to kneel. [741]

Nell sat on the wharf that evening, weaving a net with a wider door than ever, and hummed a verse to the returning water. [765] "Nine years till the pools run dry," she sang, "and then we walk with those gone by." [782]
