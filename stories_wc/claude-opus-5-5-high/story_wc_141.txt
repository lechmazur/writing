The observatory on Garrow Cliff had been abandoned for eleven years, which, Odile Marsh noted cheerfully, was about how long it took a dome to learn to leak in every direction at once. [33] She set her recorder on the brass rail and announced the episode title to the rain. [49] "Nonsense, Explained, number forty: The Singing Compass of Garrow." [58] The village version went like this: old Tobias Marsh, astronomer and failed luthier, had built a cello that told lost people which way to go, and it still hummed up here for anyone desperate enough to climb. [95] Odile had busted thirty-nine myths, including a weeping statue that turned out to be a gutter and a ghost that turned out to be a goat. [121] This one should have been easy, except Tobias had been her grandfather, and the last thing she had said to him was that his riddle was childish. [148] It was painted above the telescope mount in his cramped capitals: WHAT COMPASS DOES EVERYONE CARRY THAT NO ONE CAN KEEP? [169] He had asked it at every birthday, and she had given him a new wrong answer every year, and he had laughed every time, and she had hated that he never told her. [202] She meant to solve it tonight, publicly, with a microphone running, so that it would finally be only a puzzle. [222]

The cello stood against the eyepiece stand, unvarnished, stringless, its scroll never carved, a body waiting for a voice. [241] On its top, balanced where a bridge should have been, lay a scallop shell painted with a tiny star chart, one red arrow slashed across the constellations. [268] Beside it sat his logbook, splayed open like a sleeping bird. [279] "Exhibit A," she told the recorder, "one painted seashell on one unfinished cello, very spooky, somebody call the bishop." [298]

She skimmed the log, found only weather and star positions, and shut it. [311] The pages whispered as they closed, a long papery exhale, and inside that whisper the cello answered. [328] It was a low hum, resonant as a held breath, rising from the hollow body, and the shell shivered and turned on the spruce until its red arrow pointed at the stool where Tobias used to sit. [365] Then the pages settled, the hum died, and the shell went still. [377]

Odile laughed, because laughing was her method. [384] "Helmholtz resonance," she said into the microphone. [391] "Air pushed across the f-holes, the body rings, the shell walks on the vibration like a coin on a washing machine." [412] Myth busted, she should have said. [418]

Instead she opened the book and closed it again. [427] In the second hum she heard, or built, his voice, the way she always rebuilt old conversations in the shower, in traffic, at three in the morning, giving herself better lines. [458] In this version of their last birthday she did not call the riddle childish. [472] She asked for a hint, and he said, "It points while you hold it and vanishes when you check," and the shell swung toward the window, toward the sea. [501] Silence. [502]

She closed the book a third time, then a tenth, retelling that afternoon to the humming cello until the paint began to flake from the shell's ridges, each whisper wearing the little compass thinner. [536] In one retelling she stayed for cake. [543] In another she apologized. [547] In the worst one, the true one, she slammed the door, and the arrow pointed straight at it, and she found she was crying and grinning at the same time, which seemed about right for a woman arguing with a violin's fat uncle in the rain. [593]

The arrow was not pointing at places, she realized; it was following the conversation, turning wherever the talking went. [612] Every compass she had ever debunked promised a fixed north, and this one offered only the direction of the next thing said, good for exactly as long as a page takes to close. [645] She looked up at the riddle and finally had it. [655]

"A conversation," she told the recorder, her voice cracking cheerfully. [665] "Everyone carries one, it points you somewhere while it lasts, and nobody gets to keep it, not even if they replay it for eleven years." [690]

She closed the logbook one last time. [697] The hum came thin and brief, and the shell, nearly bare now, turned toward the stairs, toward the village, toward her mother's lit kitchen window far below, a woman Odile had not phoned since the funeral. [733] "Myth status," she said, pocketing the shell, "inconclusive, but the directions are excellent." [746] Then she switched off the recorder, left the gloom to the dome, and went down the cliff to say something she would not have to reimagine. [772]
