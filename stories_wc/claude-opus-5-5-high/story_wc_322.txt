The gallery closed at six, and by seven Ines Vale was alone with the lies. [15] That was how the director phrased it, fondly: the Hallam Optical Gallery sold honest lies, each one explained on a brass plaque beside it. [39] The Ames room where children grew into giants had a diagram of its slanted floor. [54] The staircase that climbed forever came with a paragraph on Penrose. [65] Rational enchantment, the director called it: you know exactly how the trick works, and your eyes believe it anyway. [84]

Ines had been hired to build the final room. [93] Her business cards said environmental designer, but the critics who reviewed her installations used a different phrase, and she had stopped correcting them: fever dream architect. [119] She built spaces the way a hot, sick mind builds them at three in the morning, where the ceiling is both too near and impossibly far, where a pattern on the wallpaper begins, very politely, to breathe. [156] Realistically imaginary, one reviewer wrote, and Ines had liked that, because every one of her rooms began with something real. [176]

This one began with a spool of sinew thread. [185]

Her mother had been a saddler in a town without horses, repairing boots and belts and the occasional drum, and she had sewn everything with dried deer tendon split into strands so fine they looked like hair. [222] When Ines was six and burning with scarlet fever, her mother had sat beside the bed stitching, and the sound of that thread pulling through leather was the only solid thing in a week of melting walls. [259]

Or so Ines remembered. [263] Her mother, who now lived in a care home and called her by her aunt's name, remembered a different week, if she remembered it at all. [289] Lately Ines had begun to doubt her own version too. [299] Each time she reached for it, a detail had shifted: the lamp was on the left, then the right; her mother hummed, then did not; the hands holding the awl grew older every time she looked. [335] She understood the mechanism perfectly; memory was rebuilt on each recall, a room redrawn from its own diagram. [353] Knowing did not make the floor less slanted. [361]

So she built the room by weaving patterns. [369] Two lattices of sinew thread, strung wall to wall on steel frames, one behind the other, each a slightly different grid copied from the stitching on her mother's old tool roll. [400] Where the grids overlapped, moire bloomed, the ghost pattern that exists in neither layer and only between them. [418] The plaque she drafted explained this in two tidy sentences. [428]

The plaque did not explain why she wanted it. [437] She wanted to discover the texture of connection, not the story of it, which kept changing, but the grain, the resistance, the thing her fingers had known before her mind started editing. [469]

On the last night she tied off the final knot and stepped back into the center of the room. [488] She moved her head an inch, and the moire began to erupt. [500] It did not flicker the way the test panels had. [510] It rose out of the lattices in slow dark waves, rings opening inside rings, and within them, for the length of a breath, she saw a lamp on the left and a hand pulling thread, steady, unhurried, patient as a clock. [551] She moved again and the lamp stood on the right, and the hand was her own. [567]

She felt no fear, which frightened her slightly. [575] The room was doing exactly what she had engineered it to do, and still it felt like being watched by someone kind. [597]

On Sunday she signed her mother out of the care home and brought her to the gallery before opening. [616] The old woman walked past the giants and the endless stairs without interest. [629] In the final room she did not look at the waves at all. [642] She reached out and took one strand of the lattice between her thumb and forefinger and rolled it, testing, the way she used to test a spool before buying. [671]

"Deer," she said. [674] Then, not looking at her daughter, as if reporting the weather: "You were so hot that week I kept sewing just so you would hear something that wasn't you." [703]

Ines did not ask which week, or whether the lamp had been on the left. [718] She put her own fingers beside her mother's on the same strand, and felt it: dry, ridged, faintly warm, pulled taut between two frames that did not touch. [746] Around them the pattern that belonged to neither layer went on quietly rising, and for once she let it. [765]
