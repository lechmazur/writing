Ever since luck became measurable, the estate had kept a brass gauge by the maze gate, and every morning Oriel Venn pressed her thumb to it and watched the needle barely stir. [32] Point two, it read, the same as a cracked teacup. [42] She was seventy-nine, a withered vine dresser with knuckles like old graft scars, and she had tended the hedged grape maze for fifty-one years. [66]

The new steward, Mr. Hask, believed in numbers. [74] He had hired Pell, a lanky boy whose thumb sent the needle slamming to nine, and announced that fortune grew fruit the way sun did. [99] To be fair, he said, there would be a trial: Pell would dress the east half of the maze, Oriel the west, and at harvest the better half would keep its gardener. [131]

Oriel was wisely pragmatic, and she knew how the trial would end. [143] Hask would stand at the gate, read the gauges, and see whatever the gauges told him to see. [161] Luck, measured or not, would never be credited to her. [171]

So on the first night, with a lantern and a pocketknife, she untied the red and white ribbons Hask had knotted at the two entrances and swapped them. [199] Hask never walked the maze; he disliked getting lost, and he trusted ribbons as much as needles. [216] In his ledger, her paths would now belong to Pell. [226]

Then she began to work, and she began to celebrate. [236] Oriel had always commemorated small victories elaborately. [243] When a stubborn cane finally budded, she rang the tin bell on her belt three times, recited the vine's name aloud, and pressed a marble into the soil at its foot. [274] The marbles came from her collection, a cigar box of glass her late husband had cracked in a skillet and plunged into snowmelt, so that each one held frozen lightning inside. [305] Before burying one, she would polish it against her apron until the cracks caught the sun, because a victory, she said, deserved to shine even underground. [331]

Pell heard the bell across the hedges and laughed at first. [342] Then mildew silvered his leaves, and his luck, which spared him every splinter and wasp, did nothing for the vines. [362] By midsummer he was slipping through the hedge gaps to watch her thin clusters and tie tendrils, and she let him, because pragmatism had no use for pride. [390]

Her own body was going the other way from the vines. [401] As the grapes swelled dark and heavy, her grip loosened; as the maze bloomed, she withered, and some mornings she needed Pell's arm to reach the gate. [428]

At harvest, Hask stood between the ribbons with his ledger. [438] The red half, Pell's half by his record, sagged with fruit. [449] The white half was gray with mildew. [456]

"Nine versus point two," he said, satisfied. [463] "Fortune shows." [465]

Oriel had planned to let him finish, to let the lie ripen until he had praised her work in writing. [485] But a deception left standing only teaches the wrong lesson, so she mended it instead. [500]

"I switched your ribbons," she said. [506] "The red paths are mine." [511]

Hask's face tightened toward dismissal, toward fraud, toward the needle. [521] Before he could speak, she knelt at the nearest vine, dug two fingers into the loam, and drew out a marble. [542] She wiped it on her apron and held it up, and the cracks inside flared like veins of light. [561]

"There is one under every vine in the red half," she said. [573] "Dig anywhere." [575]

Pell dug, and found glass, and dug again. [583] Then he stood, dirt to the wrists, and said quietly that the white half was his, and that no marble would be found there. [607]

Hask looked from the ledger to the gauge to the heavy clusters beneath the red ribbon, and for once the numbers had nothing to say. [632]

"Luck measures what happens to a man," Oriel said. [641] "A garden measures what he does." [647]

She was kept on, though her hands would not manage another season, and everyone knew it. [663] So that evening she did what she always did with a small victory. [676] She rang the tin bell three times, spoke Pell's name aloud as if he were a vine, and pressed the last cracked marble from the cigar box into his palm. [706]

"Polish it," she told him, "and bury it where you first get something right." [720]

Behind them the maze leaned heavy with fruit, and the gardener it knew best walked slowly home through its turns as the first leaves fell. [745]
