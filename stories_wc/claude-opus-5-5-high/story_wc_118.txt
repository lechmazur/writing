Odile Parr was the kind of historian the old universities would have quietly declined to hire, because she took notes with her palm flat against plaster. [26] Walls, she believed, were the last honest listeners. [34] Nine years had passed since the last human dream was dreamt, somewhere unrecorded, and since then every sleeper on earth woke to the same clean grey nothing. [61] Without dreams, people forgot how to invent, and without invention they began, slowly, to forget each other. [78] Odile had decided that if no one could make new stories, she would collect the old ones the walls had heard, before the walls came down too. [105]

The reclaimed silo library on the Harrow plain was her ninth building that year. [119] Someone, decades ago, had scrubbed the grain dust from its concrete throat and bolted shelves in a spiral that climbed toward a rusted cap, so that reading there felt like standing inside a bell that had finished ringing. [157] Evening light came through the rivet holes in bronze needles. [167] The silence had a color, and it was that one. [177]

She knew within an hour that something was wrong with the room. [189] The spiral of shelves did not turn around the silo's center. [200] Its axis sat a full stride east, as if the builders had been circling something invisible. [216] When Odile pressed her ear to the curve of the wall, the hum she usually felt as a diffuse murmur pooled instead toward that empty spot on the floor, the way water finds a drain. [251]

At the base of the silo, behind a panel of stacked atlases, she found a fireplace no library would need. [271] Its grate held a nest of diaries, their spines charred, their pages curled like dry leaves. [287] Someone had tried to burn them and lost courage halfway. [297] Odile sat cross-legged on the cold hearth and began to probe through them with tweezers and patience, reading only the unburned margins, the half-sentences that survived. [323]

They belonged to a librarian named Teo Lusk, the silo's last keeper. [335] "...my granddaughter still dreams, the only one left in the county, and she dreams of the dragons on the..." [354] Another page: "...fragment my mother carried from the abbey sale, six hundred years old, and the dragons do not circle the middle..." [376] Another: "...rebuilt the shelves around the place she sleeps, so the walls would hold whatever she..." [392] And, darkest at the edges: "...doctors want her for study, want to open the dream like a watch, better they think it is gone..." [416] So he had burned the evidence of her, and could not finish. [428]

Odile found the tapestry wedged in the flue, wrapped in oilcloth, a palm-sized fragment of faded wool. [445] Three dragons, rust and gold, chased each other's tails around a pale point that sat stubbornly off-center, as though the medieval weaver had known the world did not spin where it claimed to. [478] The displaced axis of the dragons matched the displaced axis of the shelves exactly. [492] Teo had built his library around a sleeping child, and the child had dreamt around a weaving. [509]

Odile crossed the floor, holding the fragment against her chest, and stepped onto the empty point. [525] The concrete gathered around her like a held breath. [534] Then, faint as grain settling, the walls gave back what they had kept: a girl's sleepy voice, years old, telling her grandfather the last dream anyone ever had. [562]

Dragons, she said, who were tired of guarding the center of things. [574] They moved over one step, just one, so that everyone who had been standing in their shadow could see a new sky. [596] The voice laughed, then yawned, then stopped. [603]

Odile stood a long time in the bronze quiet. [612] She did not write the dream down at once. [621] She was sensitively perceptive enough to understand why Teo had lost his nerve at the fire: some stories, once collected, belong to the collector, and some belong to whoever needs them next. [653] Then she took out her notebook and wrote it plainly, and underneath she drew the three dragons one step off their ancient center. [676]

Nobody could dream anymore. [680] But perhaps, she thought, someone could read a dream and borrow its direction. [693] She would carry it to the next wall, and the next, and let the plaster listen. [709] Behind her, the silo hummed, one step east of center, exactly where it belonged. [723]
