Neve Harrow fished for sound in the garden her father had planted where the railway died. [16] Rows of copper reeds stood in the marsh there, and every noise that touched them grew: a heron's croak became a green coil, a child's laugh a bell-shaped leaf that rang when the wind turned it. [52] The village called it the sound wave garden, and avoided it after dark. [65] Neve did not avoid things. [70] She was the calmest fisher on the flats, the one who stood waist-deep while storms screamed and simply adjusted her line. [91] People mistook this for coldness. [96] The truth was stranger: Neve could not feel an emotion until she had transliterated it, turned it letter by letter into a hum, and let the reeds grow it where she could see its shape. [131] Her grief for her father was a tall grey frond by the gate. [144] Her love for her brother Pim was a small, stubborn blossom that whistled. [157]

Pim had not slept in nineteen nights. [164] No one in the village had. [170] It was the season when patterns break themselves, which came once in a grandmother's lifetime: tides forgot their hours, geese flew in circles, and the constellations went sleepwalking. [198] Each night the Heron and the Plough and the Seven Sisters shuffled loose from their places and wandered, eyes shut, across the sky, and beneath their drifting light nobody could close their own eyes. [232]

Neve's grandmother had told her why. [238] Long ago the goddess Oru had sung the stars into their shapes with a lullaby, then gone away into the deep, and only the echo of her song still circled the world, following the stars wherever they strayed. [276] Catch the echo, the old woman had said, and you can sing them home. [290]

So on the twentieth night Neve walked into the garden with her rod and her father's rail spike. [308] He had pulled it from the last sleeper of the dead line, and it was the only thing she owned that made no sound at all. [334] Struck, dropped, dragged across stone, the old iron swallowed every note. [345] She tied it to her line as a hook. [354]

Overhead the Heron stumbled west, then east, then west again, and Neve followed it in a zigzag through the rows, stepping only where its pale light fell. [381] The reeds shivered. [384] Then the echo came. [388]

It was not gentle. [392] It came low across the marsh like a she-wolf crooning to her cubs, a lullaby with teeth in it, hushing and howling at once, and every reed it touched erupted into wild growth. [425] Neve cast toward it and sang, trying to lure it with its own tune. [439] Her voice hit the copper and bloomed into thorny coils that tangled her line. [453] The echo veered away. [457] Every sound she made became another thicket between them. [466]

She stood still, breathing, and did what she always did: she transliterated. [478] Fear, letter by letter. [482] Pim's red eyes, letter by letter. [488] But this time she did not hum the result. [497] An echo, she reasoned, only enters a space that is empty and shaped like itself. [512] So she opened her throat and voiced silence. [520] Her mouth made every shape of the lullaby, every rise and snarl and hush, with no breath behind it, a song carved out of nothing, as soundless as the iron in her hand. [553]

The garden went quiet around her for the first time in its life. [566] The echo turned. [569] It circled once, sniffing at the hollow she had made, then poured into it, and the spike on her line went heavy as a salmon. [594] She reeled slowly, the way her father had taught her, never jerking, never hurrying, until the iron came up out of the dark humming for the first time since it had been forged. [627]

She carried it home against her chest. [634] Pim sat awake by the cold stove, shaking. [642] Neve laid the spike in his lap and let it sing, and she sang with it, low and rough, a lullaby with teeth, the kind that tells the dark it may come close but may not bite. [679] Through the window she watched the Heron stop mid-stride, blink, and step back into its place. [695] The Plough followed, then the Sisters, settling like hens. [704] Pim's eyes closed. [707]

Neve kept singing long after he slept, and somewhere in the middle of a verse she noticed her face was wet. [728] She had not transliterated anything. [733] The feeling had simply arrived, whole and wordless, into the hollow the silence had left behind. [749] Outside, the reeds stood still, and for once she let them. [760]
