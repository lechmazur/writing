Ilse Varrow had not wept in eleven years, and she intended to keep the record intact as she ducked beneath the waterfall. [22] The water fell in a hundred shifting colors, violet splintering into amber, green bleeding into rose, as though the cliff were grinding light through an old prism. [49] She came through soaked, her auburn braid heavy against her spine, and stood where her teacher had first brought her as a girl. [72] The orchard had not changed. [77] Apple trees older than any kingdom leaned together in the blue hour before dawn, their bark furrowed like script, their leaves silvered with a frost that formed nowhere else on earth. [108] The air smelled of cold stone and new green, ancient and just-born at once. [122]

Ilse set down her travel athanor, a squat brass furnace the size of a lantern, and opened her notebook. [141] Forty pages of calculation filled it: temperatures, humidities, the angles of the moon. [154] Three times she had attempted the final transformation of the Work, and three times the stone in her crucible had cracked into gray ash at the last instant. [182] Her numbers were perfect. [186] Something else was missing. [190]

Pressed between the last two pages lay the thing no colleague knew she kept: a dried apple blossom from this orchard, and a scrap of paper in Master Odo's shaking hand. [221] Reason tells you when, it read. [227] Wonder tells you now. [231] Learn to hear both. [235] She had called it an old man's riddle at his funeral, loudly, so no one would see her pocket the blossom from his coffin. [259]

Now she walked to the oldest tree, whose trunk had split and healed so many times it looked braided, and found what she had come for. [285] On the tip of a single leaf hung a frozen dew crystal, clear as a held breath, faceted in six perfect planes. [307] Odo had taught her that it formed once a year and lasted only until sunrise, and that the Work could not be finished without it. [332] Her earlier attempts had used crystals carried home in cold boxes; perhaps that was the error, or perhaps it was something she could not write down. [358]

With silver tongs she lifted the crystal into the crucible and lit the athanor. [372] Then she waited for the starlings. [378]

They came as the sky paled, thousands rising from the orchard in a single breath of wings. [395] Odo had read timing through the patterns in a flock of starlings, and Ilse had spent years trying to reduce the murmuration to mathematics. [419] Each bird, she had proven, watched only its seven nearest neighbors; there was no leader, no plan, only rule upon rule producing something that looked like a single living thought. [449] She could calculate the rules. [454] She had never been able to predict the shape. [463]

The flock twisted above the waterfall, catching its colors on their wings so that for a moment the birds flashed like thrown jewels. [486] Ilse's hand moved toward her pencil out of habit. [495] She stopped it. [498] The thermometer said the heat was correct, and she let that knowledge rest in her like a floor beneath her feet, and then she simply watched. [524]

The birds folded, spilled, gathered again. [530] She felt the rhythm of it before she understood it, a swelling like a phrase of music approaching its resolution. [550] The flock drew itself into the shape of a blossom, five dark petals turning over the orchard, and she thought, without deciding to think it: now. [576]

She opened the vent and flamed the crucible white-blue. [585]

The crystal did not melt. [590] It brightened, drinking the fire the way the waterfall drank light, and beneath it the dull stone of the Work began to blush toward red. [615] Every rule she knew said to use the tongs. [624] Instead, trembling, Ilse reached in with her bare fingertip and touched the crystal, because Odo had once done exactly that and laughed, and she had thought him reckless. [652] It was neither hot nor cold. [658] It felt like the first morning of the world. [667]

The stone flushed a deep, living crimson and did not crack. [678] Overhead the starlings scattered toward the sunrise, their pattern dissolving back into a thousand separate lives. [694]

Ilse picked up her pencil at last and wrote the temperature, the hour, the humidity. [709] Beneath the numbers, beside the pressed blossom, she added a line she would never show anyone: I heard both. [728] The record of eleven years ended quietly there, among the ancient trees, and she let it. [744]
