Wren Tolley whistled while she dug, which the vicar called irreverent and the dead, as far as she could tell, never minded. [22] She was the blithe sort of cemetery gardener who pruned yews in a yellow hat and told the angels on the monuments when their noses had gone green. [50] But on the night after the chess clock ran down, she did not whistle at all. [66]

For eleven years she had played Abel Marsh every Thursday inside his silo, the old grain tower past the cemetery wall that he had painted cobalt so the stars would have something to argue with. [101] Its roof had rusted open decades ago, and they played beneath a ragged circle of sky. [117] Abel had been sexton before her, and he never lost on time. [129] Yet last Thursday, with a winning position and nine minutes left, he had folded his hands and watched his flag fall. [150] Then he had pressed an antique sovereign case into her palm, a little silver thing worn smooth as a river stone, and said, "Don't open it till I'm settled." [179]

He was settled by Monday. [184]

Wren had dug the grave herself, and now, beneath the cobalt curve of the silo, she thumbed the spring. [203] Inside lay no coins, only a coil of red wool and a slip of paper that read, in his cramped hand, Read the border. [227]

The tapestry hung where it always had, on the curved wall behind his chair, a woven view of the cemetery she knew better than her own face. [254] She had always assumed the border was simply fraying, a fringe of loose ends Abel was too sentimental to trim. [274] By lantern light she saw they were knotted, tenderly organized in clusters, the way her mother had once counted stitches. [294] Wren wanted to know why a man who never lost had chosen to lose, and she suspected the answer was sewn here, so she knelt and began to untangle them. [324]

It took most of the night, the darkness thick and soft around her as the nap of old curtains. [343] The first threads were easy: blue meant the north field, green the yews, a single knot for each row. [362] Then came the grey threads, dozens of them, all pointing toward the low meadow by the brook where nothing grew but clover and no stone had ever stood. [390] Each grey strand carried two clusters of knots, and when she read them the way Abel read chess notation, file and rank, they became row and grave. [417] A third cluster, stitched with tiny glass beads, spelled initials and a single year: 1919. [432]

The influenza dead. [435]

Everyone in the village knew the paupers had gone into the meadow, but no one had charted where, and the parish register gave only a sum: forty-one burials, paid in sovereigns to the sexton of the day. [472] Abel's grandfather. [474] Wren looked at the empty case in her lap and understood what it had once held. [490]

She said the first initials aloud, E.P., and the silo gave them back to her wrong. [506] The echo came from low down, from the floor, from somewhere the curved steel should not have sent it, and it sounded less like her voice than like someone answering to a name. [539] She said the next, and the next. [546] Each returned from a different direction, soft as velvet dragged across a coffin lid, uncharted echoes finding their bearings in the dark. [568] Wren was not frightened; she had spent too many evenings among the dead to fear their manners. [585] But her throat ached, because she saw now that Abel had spent eleven years of Thursdays weaving between moves, mapping a debt his family had never paid. [612]

The last thread was the red one from the sovereign case, and it had no place yet in the border. [632] Tied to it, so small she nearly missed it, was a paper tag: Your move. [647]

Wren sat back on her heels and laughed, a wet, startled sound the silo kept to itself. [664] So that was the shape of his intentions: not a resignation but a handover, a game left unfinished so she would have to keep playing. [689] He had let his clock run down because his own time was nearly gone, and a man who kept perfect score could not bear to leave forty-one names unmarked. [718]

At dawn she walked down to the low meadow with a spade, a bundle of stakes, and the tapestry rolled beneath her arm. [741] She tied a length of red wool to the first stake, drove it in where E.P. lay, and, for the first time in a week, began to whistle. [769]
