Ilse came up from the drowned marshes with nothing but a knife, a coil of cord, and an armful of reeds gone silver with salt. [25]
The orchard at the crater's edge took her in the way glass takes in light: fully, and without holding on. [45]
Its trees were crystal, grown from seeds the old engineers had left behind, and at dusk the shimmering crater lit them from below until every branch hummed with color. [74]
Nobody here spoke the languages her grandmother had murmured in her sleep. [86]
Those were gone, even the murmur of them, and people now talked in a thin shared tongue of trade words and gestures, and in marks. [111]
Marks mattered most. [114]
Every family scratched its sign into the bark-glass of the trees it tended, and the signs were joined by etched lines, cousin to cousin, marriage to marriage, until the whole orchard was a web of kinship you could read with your fingertips. [156]
Ilse had no sign, and no line reached toward her. [166]

She wove what she knew. [171]
Herons of reed, bent and bound, stood among the crystal trunks, and children touched their dry feathers as if reed were the strange, precious thing. [196]
The grafter Oren, who spliced crystal limbs for every household, nodded at her work but never invited her to supper. [216]
A graft only takes, he said once, when the cut is clean and the wood agrees. [232]

Then the tags began appearing. [237]
A small spiral with a hooked tail, painted in phosphor that glowed long after the crater dimmed, showed up on the trunks overnight: on the widow Pell's tree, on the tree of the quarreling Asher brothers, on a sapling nobody claimed. [278]
It belonged to no family. [283]
The council called it vandalism and sent everyone out with lanterns to find the painter. [298]
Ilse went too, because a search was the first thing the orchard had ever asked of all of them at once. [319]

For nine nights they hunted together. [325]
Pell showed her how to read the older etchings; the Asher brothers, forced into the same row, argued about the tag's meaning and then, grudgingly, agreed; a boy named Tobin held Ilse's lantern and asked her the reed word for heron, and she found she still knew it. [373]
It was Ilse who noticed the pattern. [380]
Each tag sat where a kinship line had broken off: a dead husband's sign, an exiled brother's, an orphan's blank sapling. [401]
And every hooked tail pointed the same way, toward the rim. [412]

They followed the tails to the glimmering obsidian arbor that stood alone where the orchard ended and the crater's light began. [433]
No one tended it; its black glass branches were said to belong to the dead. [448]
Beneath it, a paint pot at his knee, knelt Oren. [458]
The arbor was covered in spirals, hundreds of them, each joined by phosphor lines to every other, a second web glowing over the obsidian like frost. [484]
"The lines on the trees only show who is left," he said, not looking up. [499]
"I wanted somewhere for the ones who were cut away." [509]

The council began to shout about disorder and false signs. [519]
Ilse stepped between them before she could think better of it. [530]
"He's grieving out loud," she said, "and you've all spent nine nights grieving with him without knowing it." [548]
She pointed to Pell, who had found her husband's spiral and was weeping openly, and to the Asher brothers, standing shoulder to shoulder at last. [573]
The shouting thinned into silence. [578]

Oren looked at her then, really looked, and held out the paint pot. [591]
"There's a place," he said, "for a sign that hasn't got a line yet." [605]
Ilse did not paint. [609]
Instead she went back for her reeds, and by dawn she had woven a heron into the lowest fork of the arbor, its neck bent toward the orchard, its feet rooted in black glass. [643]
She dedicated it aloud, in the old marsh word her grandmother had used for those who drown, and then in the orchard's thin tongue, to everyone cut away. [671]
Tobin repeated the word after her, stumbling, and Pell repeated it after him. [684]

When dusk came again and the crater light rose, it came up through the reeds and threw a heron's shadow across the nearest trees, and Oren, with a steady hand, etched a line from the arbor to the sapling nobody had claimed, and marked it with her name. [732]
It ached, the way a fresh graft must ache, bright at the cut. [745]
But the wood agreed. [749]
