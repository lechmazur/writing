At the blue hour the orchard always sounded restless, and tonight it sounded wounded. [14] Pell heard the difference from the leeward edge, where the circle of driftwood dipped lowest and the apple trees leaned away from the wind as if listening for something behind them. [45] She was the leeward tide prophet, which meant she read what the sea did after the weather had already passed over it. [67] Tonight the sea had brought them a whale. [75] It was only bones now, a gray cathedral of ribs that had come out of the dusk and struck the windward side hard enough to split three logs of the ring. [106] Now it hung there, wedged, grinding against the lashings with every swell, and seawater sloshed between the roots of the nearest pear trees. [129] Hask, who kept the axes, was already shouting for men to cut it free. [143]

Pell looked up instead. [147] The clouds were moving in long combed strands from the southwest, and at their edges they were fraying into smaller strands, and those into smaller still. [173] She knew that pattern. [177] Old Tamsin, who had held the leeward before her, had taught her to sketch it: draw the main line of a cloud, then split its end in two, then split each of those, again and again, until the page grew a tree of weather. [221] Where the branches crowded, the gusts would come. [229] Tonight they crowded everywhere. [233] A squall was minutes out, twenty at most. [241] If Hask's men chopped the skeleton loose, the ring would open like a mouth and the squall would pour straight into the orchard. [264]

She ran windward with her slate under one arm and Tamsin's xylophone mallet in her fist. [280] The mallet was a stupid thing to carry into a crisis, a rosewood handle and a felt head gone hard with salt, but Tamsin had used it on everything the sea had ever thrown at them. [316] The iron buoy that now rang the dinner hour had once holed the ring. [330] The glass floats that chimed in the cherry branches had arrived as a smothering raft of wreckage. [347] Whatever strikes us, Tamsin used to say, we strike back gently, and listen to what it wants to be. [366]

"Hold the axes," Pell said. [371] Hask glared, but he held them. [377]

She climbed onto the broken logs and struck the first rib. [388] It answered with a low, full note that made the water shiver. [400] She struck the second, the third, the ninth, and on her slate she sketched what she heard the way Tamsin had sketched clouds: a line for each fundamental, then branches for the overtones that split away from it, then smaller branches for the overtones of those. [446] The sketched resonance grew fast under her chalk, restless and lovely, and it told her plainly what the eye could not. [467] Every rib rang clean except the two that were jammed deepest into the ring, and those two rang dull and short, damped by the wood they had driven into. [496] The bone was sound. [500] The spine was whole. [504] The skeleton was not breaking the ring; it had become part of it. [517]

"Don't cut it out," she told Hask. [523] "Lash it in." [526]

They worked with the sky darkening to ink, ropes looped through the gaps between ribs, pear roots wound around vertebrae, the split logs braced against bone. [552] By the time the squall arrived, the breach was a wall. [563] The wind hit it and the ribs began to sing. [573] Pell stood among them with the mallet raised and found she did not need it; the gusts played every note she had drawn, rising where the cloud branches crowded, falling where they thinned. [606] For the first time she could hear the weather coming before she saw it. [620]

The squall passed as fast as it had come. [629] The orchard was soaked, and loud, and whole. [637] Hask came to the new wall and touched a rib as if it might bite. [652] "It needs a name," he said. [658] Tamsin had always named the things she turned, so that the orchard would remember they had been chosen and not merely survived. [680] Pell tapped the mallet on the largest rib, once, lightly, like a blade on a shoulder. [696] "I dub you the Windward Choir," she said, and the bone hummed back at her. [711] Above them the last of the blue hour was fading, the clouds already branching again, and she could hear which way the next wind wanted to go. [738]
