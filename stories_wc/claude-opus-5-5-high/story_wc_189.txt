"Are you going, then?" Marisol asked, and her granddaughter did not answer. [12] The question hung in the grotto like a drip that would not fall. [25] Marisol had kept the chronicle of these tidal pools for forty-one years, ever since old Benedek had pressed a jade cicada into her palm and told her the sea needed a witness who would not get bored. [62] The grotto was older than the village, older than the chapel whose bell no longer rang, a limestone throat the waves had hollowed and then, some centuries back, opened to the sky when part of its roof collapsed. [100] Tonight the stars came down through that ragged mouth and lay in every pool. [114]

She did not look at Ines. [120] That was the first thing Benedek had taught her: you never see a faint thing by staring at it. [139] Astronomers called it averted vision; you looked a little to the side, and the dim star bloomed at the edge of your eye. [162] Pools worked the same way, and so, Marisol had found, did people. [174]

So she watched the pool at their feet instead, the one she had named the Ledger because it kept the most honest accounts. [197] The tide was at slack water, that strange held breath between ebb and flood when nothing flows and everything waits. [217] She had spent four decades trying to learn what time felt like, not how long it lasted but its grain, its weave, whether it ran smooth as oil or caught like wool on a nail. [252] Her notebooks were full of these in-between hours, because the transitions were where she suspected the texture lived. [270]

In the Ledger, a hermit crab had dragged itself halfway out of a periwinkle shell and was hesitating beside a larger whelk, one claw resting on the new doorway. [299] Marisol smiled at it. [303] She knew that posture. [307] It was the most dangerous moment in a crab's life, soft and unarmored between houses, and it was also the only way to grow. [331]

At the edge of her sight, reflected in the still water among the stars, Ines was turning the strap of her satchel around one finger, the way she had at seven when she wanted a second biscuit and was working out how to ask. [375] The satchel was new. [379] So were the boots, which were city boots, poorly suited to wet rock, and which Ines had worn anyway, as if she were already practicing. [404] A letter's corner showed white at the satchel's flap, the university crest pressed into it like a seal on wax. [424]

Marisol did not need the answer spoken; she could foresee it the way she foresaw a spring tide, not from any single sign but from the way small signs leaned together. [455] What she foresaw, too, was what would come after if she let the silence curdle: Ines staying out of guilt, filling notebooks with a borrowed patience, growing old in a shell a size too small. [490]

The pause stretched. [493] A drop fell from the roof at last and rang against the Ledger, and the stars shivered and resettled. [512] Marisol noticed, with the small delight she always felt at a true observation, that this pause did not feel long or short. [534] It felt dense, like wet sand that holds a footprint. [544] Every second of it had room in it for her granddaughter's whole face, for the tick of water, for the crab's trembling claw. [567] So that was the texture, she thought: time was coarsest and richest exactly where it seemed to stop. [585] Forty-one years, and the answer had been waiting in the one silence she had most dreaded. [601]

She reached into her coat and found the jade cicada, worn smooth by decades of her thumb. [618] In the old tombs, Benedek had told her, such cicadas were laid on the tongues of the dead, because the insect lives for years in darkness and then splits its skin and climbs into the light. [654] It was not a charm for staying. [661] It never had been. [665]

"Yes," Ines said at last. [670] "In September." [672]

"I know," Marisol said, and pressed the cicada into her granddaughter's palm, the way it had once been pressed into hers. [693] "Every shore has pools, and every pool needs a witness who won't get bored." [707] "Take the chronicle with you, and write down the in-between hours." [718]

In the Ledger, the hermit crab let go of its old shell and slipped, quick and soft, into the new one. [739] Then the flood began, lifting the stars gently from every pool, and Marisol opened her notebook to write her last entry: slack water held longer than measured. [766]
