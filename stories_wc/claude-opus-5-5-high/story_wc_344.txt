Ilse Varga opened locks only when the moon went red. [10] It was an old trade, older than her grandfather's clocks, and it paid badly between eclipses. [26] When the earth's shadow slid across the moon, iron forgot what it had promised, and a patient woman with a tension wrench could ask a vault to remember something else. [56] She had opened cathedral reliquaries, drowned men's sea chests, and one tiny music box that a widow could not bear to hear. [78] She had never learned to close anything. [85]

Her grandfather had told her so, the winter she was nine, after she picked the back of his longcase clock to see the heart inside. [110] The pendulum stopped and never started again. [117] "Opening is the easy half," he said, not angry, only tired, sweeping loose wheels into a dented tin. [135] "Repair has a rhythm, and you have to listen for it." [146] He died before he could teach her, and the tin came to her in his will: a clockmaker's gear assortment, brass and steel, hundreds of teeth rattling like pebbles in a pocket. [178] She carried it for thirty years the way beachcombers carry driftwood, not for use, but because it had once been part of something that floated. [203]

The letter from the Cloud Library arrived the week of the eclipse. [215] Its librarians had fled the cold decades before, when the great clock in the reading hall froze and time on the mountain froze with it, leaving the stacks rimed and silent above the weather. [249] The clock was sealed behind a lock no one could name. [260] Ilse climbed the switchback stairs through the clouds and came out under the soft hush of a distant morning, a pale sun that never rose higher, snow lying on the lecterns like dust sheets in a shut house. [298]

The reading hall was a hall of mirrors. [306] Ice had glazed every pane and gilt frame until the walls threw her back at herself a hundred times, and each reflection was a little younger: a gray woman, a dark-haired woman, a girl of nine holding a pick. [345] In the center stood the clock, its case wrapped in a lattice of frosted steel. [360]

The lock itself was simple; she felt that at once. [370] What frightened her was the note she found wedged in the library's northeast corner, among torn almanac leaves and index cards, where someone had hidden scraps of prophecy like mouse nests. [401] She pieced them together on a frozen table. [409] "When the red moon stands in the glass, one who only opens will come." [423] Another: "If she unlocks and leaves, the ice keeps her." [433] And, in a hand she knew from thirty years of labeled drawers: "Bring the tin, Ilsie." [449] He had worked here once, she realized, the summer before she broke his clock. [463]

The eclipse came at what should have been noon. [472] Through the high window the moon darkened to rust, and every mirror caught it, hauntingly bright, a hundred red moons burning in the ice like coals in snow. [500] The steel lattice softened under her fingers, and the lock gave with a sigh. [514] Inside, the movement was stripped: bare arbors, empty pivots, the escapement missing its wheel. [528] Her grandfather had carried its wheels home to recut, she understood, and never came back. [543]

She uncapped the tin. [547] The reflections leaned closer, the girl of nine among them, and Ilse felt the old urge to open everything, to pry and look and walk away. [573] The ice along the mirror frames had begun to creep toward her boots. [586] Instead she knelt and listened. [591] She fitted a wheel, turned the arbor, and heard the teeth argue. [603] She tried another, and another, sorting brass by the feel of its pitch, until two wheels met and spoke in a steady, quiet knock, like surf against a hull. [632] That was the rhythm: not forcing, but waiting for the parts to agree. [645] She worked through the whole red hour, the escapement last, and when the shadow slid off the moon she closed the case and turned the key herself. [672]

The pendulum swung. [675] Around her the mirrors wept, ice running from the frames in long bright threads, and the reflections thinned until only one woman looked back, gray and ordinary and done. [704] Outside, the pale sun lifted at last, and somewhere below the clouds a bell remembered the hour. [721]

One wheel was left over in the tin, small and worn smooth as a beach stone. [737] Ilse knew it now: the one from his longcase clock, the one she had spent her life thinking she had lost. [758] She put it in her pocket and started down the stairs, already listening. [771]
