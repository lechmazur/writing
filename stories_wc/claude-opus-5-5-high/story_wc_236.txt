The fog had not lifted from Calle Vedra when Ines carried her grandmother's water clock into Aurelio Sant's repair shop. [20] Behind her the bell over the door gave one thin note and stopped, as if it, too, had decided to whisper. [41] Inside, dozens of clepsydrae dripped on shelves, copper bowls and glass columns keeping time by the fall of water, and the room sounded like a cave learning to count. [70] Once she would have known the shop by its smell: brass polish, wet cedar, the mineral breath of standing water. [90] For eleven years she had blended oils for grieving widows and sleepless students, reading their moods in the sour edge of fear sweat or the sweetness of relief. [118] Then a winter fever took her smell, and with it the map she had drawn of every heart that entered her door. [140] Now she mapped the emotional terrain with her eyes, and her eyes worked best when something stood in the way. [160] People guarded their faces; they forgot to guard their reflections. [170]

"It runs slow," she said, setting the clock on his bench. [181] "Four minutes every hour." [185]

Aurelio did not look up at her; he looked at the clock, a tall glass cylinder gone milky with age, its float rising against painted hour lines. [212] He unscrewed the outflow valve, then went very still. [221] Through the clouded glass his face swam, bent and doubled, and Ines saw his mouth tighten the way a fist does. [242]

"This screw is not a clockmaker's," he said. [250] He held it to the window, where fog pressed like wool against the panes. [264] "Square thread, knurled head, surgical brass." [270] "It is from a midwife's birthing clamp." [277]

"My grandmother was a midwife," Ines said. [284] "She timed labors by this clock." [290]

In the brass of a nearby bowl she watched his reflection flinch. [302]

"Then she timed my wife," he said quietly. [310] "Forty years ago, in fog like this." [317] "My wife died before morning." [322] "My daughter lived." [325] He set the screw down as though it might burn him. [336] "I will machine you a proper one." [343]

Ines felt the old trembling start in her hands, the one that used to come before she uncapped a new oil, and she let it settle into calm. [371] From her coat she took the logbook that had traveled with the clock, its pages ruled in her grandmother's hand: date, name, onset, birth. [395] The entries were brisk, but between the inked lines ran faint pencil, so light most people would have taken it for the grain of the paper. [421] Ines had spent a sleepless month reading it by lamplight, tilting each page until the graphite caught. [438]

"What was her name?" she asked. [444]

"Marisol Sant." [446]

She found it, tilted the book toward the fogged window, and read aloud. [459] "Onset nine at night." [463] "Birth four-ten." [465] She lowered her voice for the pencil. [472] "Valve sheared at midnight." [476] "Used my clamp screw so the water would not stop." [486] "Mother asked to hold the baby before she slept." [495] "Father sang to her off-key, and she laughed." [503] "They named the girl Alba, for the dawn." [511] "I have never seen a man love so hard in so short a night." [525]

Aurelio's hand found the bench edge. [531] "I remembered only the blood, and her going." [539]

"She laughed?" [541]

"At your singing." [544]

Ines had guessed, from the word clockmaker penciled beside the father's name, but guessing was not the same as watching a man's face unlock. [568] Since losing her smell she had learned that ordinary miracles were rarely announced; they hid in margins, waiting for someone patient enough to tilt the page. [594] Her grandmother had not been a saint in a dying woman's room; she had been a tired woman who noticed laughter and wrote it down. [619]

Aurelio picked up the screw again and turned it in the gray light. [632] "It runs slow because the thread is worn," he said. [642] "I can recut it rather than replace it." [650]

"Would you?" [652]

"For both of them," he said. [658]

He worked while the fog thinned, and Ines watched him through the rippled column, his bent image steady now, his mouth loosened into something almost like a song. [686] When he set the screw back and the first true drop fell, she could not smell the wet brass, but she knew exactly what the room felt like. [714]

"Alba has a son now," Aurelio said, not looking up, the way men say important things. [730] "I never told him how his grandmother laughed." [738]

Outside, the fog released the street one doorway at a time, and every clock in the shop kept falling, drop by patient drop, toward the hour. [764]
