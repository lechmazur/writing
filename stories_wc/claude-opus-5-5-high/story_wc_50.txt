The frost came in the second week of blossom, and by morning every orchard in Callow had gone brown at the edges like burned paper. [25] People called what followed the lull, a wounded season with nothing to pick, and they spent it indoors, mending harness and not speaking. [48] Ada Vell, the locksmith, spent it in the marble cavern above the village, trying to fix the thing that had broken the same night as the trees. [75]

The cavern kept voices. [79] Whatever was spoken inside its white chambers returned exactly one year later, drawn out of the stone when the autumn sun struck a brass plate set deep in the far wall. [110] Callow called it the harvest of echoes, and in a good year it mattered less than apples. [127] In this year it was all anyone had left, because last autumn the fever had come through, and the cavern held the final words of eleven people. [154] One of them was her father, who had built the timer. [165]

It sat in a niche near the entrance: a clockwork of springs and cams that, on the right day, rotated a ring of polished mirrors and walked a single beam of light through the cavern to the plate. [203] The frost had cracked its mainspring housing, and the cams had slipped their teeth. [217] Her father had left no drawings. [223] He had trusted his hands, and his hands were in the churchyard. [235] So Ada set out to reverse-engineer it from the only evidence remaining, which was the light itself. [252]

She had never been good at looking straight at things. [262] As a girl she had read books by the words beside the ones she meant to read, and at the bench she opened locks by attending to everything except the pin she was lifting: the faint give of the plug, the tension in her other wrist, the sound of the room. [313] Her father had called her peripherally focused, and meant it kindly. [324]

In the cavern that habit became a method. [332] Each morning, noon, and late afternoon she stood by the mirror ring and did not watch the sun. [350] She watched the edges of the marble, where the reflected light pooled and slid, and she chalked its position on the veined walls with the hour beside it. [378] Studying the reflection angles at different times, she began to see that each mirror had been tilted to a particular day, and the cams were only a calendar written in brass. [409] If she knew where the beam had to land and when, she could cut the teeth backward from the answer. [429] It was, she thought, the same as any lock. [438] You did not force the door; you listened for the shape the key would need. [453]

The widow Harrow brought her bread and asked why she wrote everything down. [466] Ada's ledger was already thick with angles and hours and small sketches of the mirrors. [481]

"Because it's a miracle," Ada said, "and miracles get lost." [491] "My father carried this one in his head, and now it's gone with him." [505] "I want to put the wonder somewhere cool and dry, in the cellar of reason, so someone can take it out later when they need it." [531]

The widow considered this. [535] "Doesn't knowing how it works spoil it?" [542]

"Apples don't taste worse because you know about roots," Ada said, though she was not entirely sure. [559]

She finished the new cams nine days before the equinox, filing each tooth by lamplight and testing it against her chalk marks at dawn. [583] One mirror remained wrong by a finger's width, and she could not find why until she stopped looking at it and noticed, at the edge of her sight, a hairline crack where the frost had shifted the whole marble shelf. [623] The cavern itself had been wounded; she corrected for it and wrote the correction down. [638]

On the equinox the village climbed the hill in silence. [648] Ada wound the mainspring, and the mirrors turned with a sound like a key settling home. [664] The beam crossed the white chambers, touched the plate, and the stone began to speak. [679] Children laughing, a man arguing about sheep, the widow Harrow's husband humming off key. [693] Then her father, tired, amused: "Ada, if this breaks, don't look at it straight." [707]

She laughed and wept at once, and the widow took her hand. [719] Knowing how it worked had not spoiled anything. [727] That night she shelved the ledger in her cellar, beside the preserves from better years, and labeled it for whoever came next. [749]
