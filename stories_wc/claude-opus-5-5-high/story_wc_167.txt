Oren Vasse had glazed the windows of Pearl Bay for thirty-one years, and he still arrived at the breakwater before the gulls. [22] This morning the letter was in his coat, folded twice, its edges already soft from handling. [38] The council would vote at noon to remove the prism atrium and pour concrete for a fuel depot. [56] The letter was signed by Marta Quell, the harbormaster, who had once helped him carry the first pane up the rocks. [77]

The atrium stood where the breakwater bent toward open water, a hexagon of angled glass no taller than a boathouse. [97] At dawn the bay mist crept inside through vents he had cut himself, and the whole structure filled like a lung. [118] In its center lay a shallow pond, lined with broken oyster shell, that the old glaziers had called the reckoning bowl. [139]

Oren sat on the rim and read the letter again. [149] It was polite and brief. [154] It cited costs, storms, the tourist decline, and a need for practical use of the waterfront. [170] But Oren had spent his life looking at light through the thin lead lines that held colored glass together, and he knew that what mattered often lived in the gaps. [200] Marta had written "practical use" twice and underlined neither. [209] She had not written that she agreed. [216] At the bottom, below her signature, she had added a single line in pencil: Show me why, before it changes. [236]

He looked up. [239] Above the bay, the last of the night still hung in thin silver bands, clouds so high they caught sunlight long before the water did. [264] The fishermen called them night-shiners. [269] The old glaziers had called their study the noctilucent reckonings, and Oren's teacher, Hanne Brisk, had kept the records in a ledger of scratched glass plates. [295] Every clear summer dawn for eighty years, someone had sat at this pond, thrown a stone, and watched. [313] The prisms caught the silver light and laid it across the mist, and the rings of the water broke that light into patterns: bands close together meant cold currents coming, wide bands meant fish running near shore, crooked bands meant a storm within the week. [358] Hanne had said it was not magic. [365] It was the upper air talking to the lower water, and the glass was only the translator. [382]

Oren had never told the council any of this. [391] It had seemed too small, too ordinary, a glazier's habit, like tapping a pane to hear if it was sound. [411] Now he understood that the ordinary was exactly what he had failed to defend. [425]

He chose a flat gray stone from the basket by the door, the same basket Hanne had filled. [443] He did not throw it at once. [450] Communing, Hanne had called it, and she had meant that you had to be quiet enough to be answered. [469] He listened to the mist tick against the panes, to a halyard knocking somewhere in the moorings, to his own breath. [490]

Then he let the stone go. [496]

The rings spread, and the light came apart across the shell floor in trembling silver lines. [512] Wide, then wide again, then a sudden narrowing at the edge. [523] He took a clean plate from the shelf and scratched the pattern with his diamond, the way he had done a thousand mornings, and beside it he wrote the date. [553] Then he took down the plates from the last three Junes and laid them side by side. [570] Each wide band sat beside a note in Hanne's hand or his own: mackerel close in, the Pell boat full by six. [592] Each narrowing matched a cold week the fleet had stayed home and lost no one. [607] The atrium had never been decoration. [613] It had been a quiet instrument, and its use had been so practical that nobody noticed it. [630]

He heard boots on the breakwater. [636] Marta stood in the doorway, collar up, holding two cups of coffee. [648]

"I thought you'd be here," she said. [655]

"You wrote that I should be." [661]

She almost smiled. [664] "I wrote very little." [668]

"Enough." [669]

He handed her a stone. [674] The sun had nearly reached the water, and the silver bands overhead were thinning into ordinary blue. [691]

"Quickly," he said, "before everything shifts." [697]

She threw, awkwardly, and the light broke wide across the shells. [708] Oren set the plates in front of her and waited, the way the pond waited, for her to read what lay between the lines. [732]

At noon, she carried the plates to the council in a fish crate lined with newspaper. [748]
