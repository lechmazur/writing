In the month when the old king lay in honey and the new king had not yet crossed the river, the palace of Babylon belonged to no one. [28]
Nidintu the astronomer liked it that way. [35]
She walked its terraces at dusk with a single reed lamp, trimmed short, because oil was dear and nobody now signed the requisitions. [58]
This palace had been rebuilt a hundred times, each dynasty glazing new lions over the old, until the walls were a sediment of blue brick and brass pipework and hanging gardens watered by clockwork. [92]
Now the clocks were winding down. [98]
The priests had fled to wait out the succession in their temples, and the guards had gone home to learn which banner to salute. [122]
Only Nidintu remained, scraping yesterday's calculations off her clay tablets so she could reuse them, eating barley she had hoarded since the king first coughed. [147]

Custom said that between regimes the sky had no sworn watcher, and that someone must seal a covenant with the unknown before the new crown was lifted, or the heavens would come loose. [180]
Nidintu did not believe the heavens were loose-able. [188]
But the priests were gone, and she was thorough. [197]

The rite belonged to the old queen's observatory, a round chamber sealed since her death eleven years before. [215]
Nidintu pried the door and found the domed window shrouded in black felt, and set into the floor beneath it, a single glazed tile divided into sixteen squares. [243]
Each square held a numeral in the old wedge script. [253]
She knelt, lamp guttering, and added them. [260]
Every row made thirty-four. [264]
Every column, every diagonal, the four corners, the four at the center: thirty-four. [277]
A magic square, the kind apprentices made to show off. [287]
But the queen had not been an apprentice, and she had not been a woman who wasted clay. [305]

Nidintu worked the way she always did, through schematic representations, because diagrams cost only charcoal. [320]
She sketched the tile onto the wall, then overlaid it on her chart of the northern sky, sixteen cells laid across sixteen houses of the horizon. [346]
She traced the numbers in order, one to sixteen, and the charcoal line bent into a shape: a crooked path climbing toward a patch of sky where her catalog listed nothing at all. [379]
Then she noticed the second layer. [385]
Read as syllables rather than quantities, the numerals along the diagonal spelled a name: Ishtar-ummi, the queen's daughter, who had died at four days old and whom the king had forbidden anyone to mourn. [419]
The queen had hidden her grief in arithmetic, a devotion encrypted so perfectly that it passed for a schoolroom trick. [439]
Every row, every column, every angle of approach arrived at the same sum, the way every hour of her life had arrived at the same child. [465]

The path pointed at something. [470]
Nidintu reached for the black felt over the window and hesitated, because to uncloak the dome was to look, and to look was to test, and she had seen too many beautiful stories fail the test. [506]
She pulled it down. [510]

The sky above Babylon had never been this dark. [519]
With no king to feast and no priests to burn incense, the city's ten thousand lamps had gone cold, and her own miserly wick was the brightest thing for a mile. [550]
She pinched it out. [554]
And there, at the end of the charcoal path, faint as breath on bronze, burned a small blue star that appeared in no catalog in the world. [581]

It was, she thought, empirically ironic: the queen had charted a star visible only when the palace fell silent, as though she had known her child could be seen only when no king was watching. [616]
Nidintu measured it three times, the way she measured everything, and three times the star held its place. [634]
The stinginess the whole court had mocked was the only instrument fine enough to find it. [650]

The covenant, she decided, did not need priests. [658]
It needed a witness willing to keep a promise to something she could not name or prove would answer. [677]
From her satchel she took her last unscraped tablet, the one she had been saving for a year, and pressed the queen's square into it cell by cell, adding beneath it the star's position and one line in her own hand: Watched, and will be watched. [723]
She set it on the tile, face up, where whoever came next would have to step around it. [741]
Far below, trumpets sounded at the river gate; the new king was coming, and soon the lamps would be lit again. [762]
Nidintu sat in darkness beside the child's name, in harmony with the one light nobody else could afford to see, and did not relight her wick. [788]
