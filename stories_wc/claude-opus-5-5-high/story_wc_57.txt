The fog came in off the harbor the way a held breath leaves the body, slow and without complaint. [19] By the time Wren Hallow reached the Gilt Pier, it had swallowed the breakwater, the bell buoy, and most of the town behind her. [43] Snow lay on the planks in a thin, unbroken skin. [53] Beneath it, where her boots scuffed, the old wood showed its famous color, a deep honey gold that came from a century of amber resin rubbed in by hands no one remembered. [85]

Maren Dole was waiting at the end, beside her father's rope. [96] It lay coiled on an upturned crate, forty fathoms of tarred hemp thick with knots, and she regarded it the way one regards a debt. [121]

"The harbor board wants the pier cleared by spring," she said. [132] "They're pulling up the gold boards to sell to some hotel. [143] Dad said you'd want to see the rope before I burned it." [155]

Wren set down her satchel, which clicked softly with glass vials of seed: beach pea, sea kale, a dozen grasses gathered from dunes that no longer existed. [182] She had spent forty years collecting them, and in that time had come to believe that a seed was simply a question a plant asked of a future it would never see. [214] Knots, she suspected, were the same. [220]

"Your father's knots belong to the weavers," she said. [229]

Maren laughed, a small sound the fog ate immediately. [238] "The secret weavers are a bedtime story." [245]

"So is most of history, until someone reads it properly." [255]

Wren knelt in the snow and took the first fathom into her lap. [268] The story went like this: long ago, when the harbor families spun their own line from sea-flax, a handful of netmakers learned to hide instructions in their work, so that no drowning or plague or tax collector could take the craft from them. [311] The sea-flax had died out a century ago, and with it the rope, and with the rope the reason anyone remembered. [332] But old men still tied the old knots out of habit, passing them hand to hand without understanding, the way a song survives its language. [357]

She read slowly. [360] A clove hitch meant a direction; a doubled overhand meant a count; a sheepshank, strangely, meant stop and wait. [379] She had worked out that grammar over six winters from scraps in museum drawers, and now it unspooled beneath her fingers like a voice she almost recognized. [406] The fog thickened until the pier's gold was the only color left in the world. [421] Maren stamped her feet and said nothing, and Wren was grateful. [432]

"Seventh piling from the end," Wren said at last. [441] "Seaward side, under the resin." [446]

They lay on their stomachs in the snow, and Maren held the lantern while Wren scraped at the amber crust with a pocketknife. [469] Beneath it, set into a hollow bored in the wood, was a gray, knobbed tube as long as a finger. [489]

"Fulgurite," Wren breathed. [492] "Lightning glass." [494] "When a bolt strikes sand, it fuses a hollow root straight down." [506]

The weavers had chosen it well: nothing sealed tighter, nothing rotted less, and no thief would look twice at a lump of burnt sand. [530] Both ends were stopped with beeswax gone hard as bone. [540] The glass was thin as eggshell, and the cold had made it brittle. [553]

"Just break it," Maren whispered. [558]

Wren did not answer. [562] She cupped the tube in her bare palms and breathed on the wax, again and again, warming it by degrees, the way she had once waited three years for a desert lily to decide it was ready to open. [601] The sheepshank had said wait, and she was infinitely patient, having learned that patience was not the absence of wanting but a way of listening to it. [628] Somewhere out in the fog the bell buoy rang once and went quiet, and the water breathed against the pilings like something asleep. [651]

At last the wax softened, and with the tip of a quill she began to coax the seeds out, one at a time, onto the snow-dusted gold. [678] They were small, dark, and glossy, forty-one of them, and whether they still lived no one could yet say. [697]

"Sea-flax," Maren said, and her voice had changed. [705]

"If they grow, someone will have to learn to spin it," Wren said. [718] "And tie the knots, and read them." [725]

Maren looked at the rope on the crate for a long time. [737] Then she knelt beside the old woman, took the rope's frayed end into her own cold hands, and asked what the first knot meant. [761] The fog closed over them both, and the harbor kept its hush, as if listening too. [777]
