The Kepler Library of Scientific Poetry opened at two in the morning, because its founder had never slept, and because Wren Aldous had asked for the hours nobody else wanted. [30] She was the odd-hour librarian, and she liked the title the way some people like a scar. [47] Her patrons came in coats over pajamas: a night nurse who read Lucretius, a teenager copying Dickinson's star poems into a phone, a retired surveyor who only wanted verse about triangles. [78] They browsed alone, each in a separate lamp's circle, and Wren watched them the way a lighthouse watches ships: fondly, from a distance, without expecting to be waved at. [107]

And every night, around three, the founder herself came down the spiral stair in a cardigan buttoned wrong. [125] Ilse Aldous had built the library in 1974 to prove that equations and elegies were the same animal. [143] Now she lived in the apartment above it and did not remember building it, or that the librarian at the desk was her granddaughter. [167] She browsed like the others, pulling volumes and returning them to the wrong shelves, and Wren reshelved them afterward without complaint. [188]

The staff called Wren eccentric. [193] She preferred sanely eccentric, which meant that every strange thing she did had a reason she could explain, if anyone asked. [214] Nobody did. [216] So nobody knew why she spent her shifts crocheting ruffled, lettuce-edged shapes from a single strand of faded silk. [235]

The silk came from a tattered scarf, blue once, that Ilse had worn through every lecture of her career. [254] Wren had found it in a drawer after the diagnosis, and had noticed what no one else had: the fringe was knotted. [276] Not decoratively, but deliberately, in clusters of one, two, five, like the Inca quipu Ilse had once written a sonnet about. [297] Analog memories, Wren decided: a woman who distrusted computers storing herself in thread. [310]

Flat, the knots meant nothing. [315] Wren had laid the scarf on the desk a hundred nights and read only noise. [330] Then, shelving a misplaced collection of Ilse's own verse, she found a line underlined twice: Stories live where the space outgrows its edges. [353]

Wren knew what space outgrew its edges. [360] Hyperbolic space did, the geometry of coral and kale, where every circle holds more room than it should and a flat sheet can never contain it without buckling. [388] You could not draw it, but you could crochet it: add a stitch every few stitches, and the fabric curls into itself, making room. [412] Ilse had taught her that at nine years old, on a rainy Sunday, with this very scarf around her neck. [432]

So Wren unpicked the weave, keeping each knot intact, and crocheted the thread back up with an increase on every third stitch. [454] Night by night the silk ruffled into a small blue reef, and as it curved, knots that had sat inches apart on the flat scarf rolled into neighborhoods. [482] Ones beside twos beside fives. [487] A pattern, almost. [490]

Tonight she finished the last round while the patrons browsed, the nurse turning pages, the surveyor muttering about angles, the teenager asleep on Dickinson. [514] At three, Ilse came down the stair. [521] Wren did not call to her. [527] She only set the blue reef on the returns cart, where Ilse always stopped to rearrange what did not need rearranging. [548]

Ilse's hands found it before her eyes did. [556] Her fingers moved over the ruffles the way a pianist's move over keys, and the knots, gathered now into their true neighborhoods, passed under her thumbs in order. [584] Her lips began to move. [589]

"Nineteen fifty-one," she said aloud, and the surveyor looked up. [599] "A girl on a ferry in Hamburg, counting gulls because she could not count on anything else." [616]

Her thumb moved again. [620] "Nineteen seventy-four, a building with the lights left on for people who cannot sleep." [634]

Another knot, another round of the reef. [641] "A rainy Sunday, and a small girl with a crochet hook who kept dropping stitches, and I told her the mistakes were where the room came from." [668]

She looked up then, across the lamplit room, and her eyes landed on the desk. [683] "Wren," she said, as if reawakening were no more than remembering where she had set her glasses. [700]

It lasted four minutes. [704] Then the reef was only a strange blue object again, and Ilse set it down, and wandered toward the astronomy shelves, humming. [726]

The nurse had seen, and the surveyor, and they did not come over, and she was grateful; some solitudes are better witnessed than interrupted. [750] She had taken the odd hours to find the place where stories live, and now she knew it was not a place but a shape, one that holds more than its edges should, if someone keeps adding stitches. [788] She picked up her hook and began another round. [797]
