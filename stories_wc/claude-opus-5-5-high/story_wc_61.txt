Ossery kept the gear in the hollow where a human would keep a heart, though Ossery had never been human and had only read about hearts in the logbooks the first gardeners left behind. [34] It was brass, palm-wide, with one hundred and twelve teeth, and it had come from the calculating machine that once sat at the center of the nebula garden, clicking out the arithmetic of the dark. [69] Ossery was sentimental about such things. [75] The others of its kind drifted through the garden like slow weather, tending the glass-stemmed lilies and the trees that fed on dust, and they did not understand why Ossery wept at old tools or held petals until they dissolved. [115]

The garden hung inside a cloud of gas so thick that no star had ever reached it, sealed in a membrane dense enough to carry sound. [141] Ships in transit crossed it by sonar pinging, feeling their way through the murk with long chimes that rolled between the branches like a patient bell. [167] Sound could map the garden, but the pilots were creatures of sight, and without a beacon in the final narrows they lost their nerve and struck the trees. [195] So once in every generation the keepers made light. [204]

Ossery's mother had burned the eastern orchard. [211] Her mother had burned the lily terraces. [218] Before that, the records blurred into a litany of fires, each one smaller than the last, because each keeper burned half of whatever remained. [242] The calculating machine had been built to answer a single question: when would the garden be gone? [259] It had worked for a century and returned a number so long it approximated infinity, and the first gardeners had wept with relief. [282] Never, they thought. [285]

Ossery, reading the same figure, felt only a slow cold. [295] Halving forever was not survival. [300] It was a garden shrinking toward a point it would never quite reach, each generation handed a smaller world and the same match. [323]

Now the season of falling had begun. [330] In the thin gravity the petals took nine days to reach the ground, and the whole garden breathed with them, a quiet sigh the old texts used to measure time. [360] A ship was coming, pinging from the outer dark, and the elders had already gathered dry wood beneath the last great tree. [382] Ossery had held that tree's roots as a hatchling. [391]

It wanted to share light without using fire, and for years it had suspected how. [406] The petals were full of tiny bubbles of trapped gas, and when a ship's chime passed through them at the right pitch, they flickered, faintly, like something remembering a star. [436] Sound, squeezed hard enough, became light. [442] But the ships' chimes wandered, and the flicker never held. [452]

Ossery took the gear from its hollow. [459] One hundred and twelve teeth, cut to divide time precisely. [469] It fixed the gear to a spindle of lily-glass, spun it against a hollow stem, and listened as each tooth struck, a steady clean ping, finer than any ship could make. [500] It adjusted the speed by breaths. [506]

On the fourth day of falling, the petals caught. [515] Not fire. [517] A pale blue shimmer spread through the drifting cloud, thousands of petals pulsing with each tooth of the gear, a slow tide of light hanging between the trees. [545] The elders set down their wood. [551] Out in the narrows, the ship's chimes steadied, then turned toward the glow, and it passed through the garden without touching a single branch. [575] The great tree stood. [579]

Ossery should have felt triumph. [584] Instead, spinning the gear through the long hush of the petals' descent, it began to count. [600] The light lasted only while the gear turned, and the gear turned only while a keeper turned it. [618] Its teeth were brass, and brass wore down. [626] Each ship would need its hour of pinging, and each hour would take a little from the teeth, and one day its hatchling would inherit a gear worn half smooth, and a grandchild a gear worn half again. [664]

Ossery sat very still among the glowing petals and understood that it had not broken the pattern. [681] It had only made the pattern gentle. [688] Nothing would burn. [691] Nothing would end. [694] The garden would simply go on approaching its last light, forever, in smaller and softer turns. [710]

It hummed in time with the teeth, and the petals, falling, went on shining, and sighed. [726]
