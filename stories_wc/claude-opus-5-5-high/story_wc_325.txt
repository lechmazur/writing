Ines Carrow hated caves the way some sailors hate water, which is to say intimately and with respect. [18] Narrow places made her lungs forget their size. [26] Still, she was the best tracker of air in the county, a spelunker who could feel a draft through three meters of rubble and follow it like a thread. [55]

The Porthkell Cable Station had been built into the cliff in 1924, a research annex where engineers studied how signals weakened along undersea cables stretched toward Newfoundland. [82] In 1931 a spring tide breached the lower gallery and drowned six linemen. [95] The company sealed the tunnel, settled quietly with nobody, and removed the page of the station log that listed who had been on shift. [119] Without names there were no widows, and without widows there was no liability. [132]

Ines had one corner of that page. [139] The station clerk, a lapsed seminarian named Aubrey Penhale, had illuminated the margins of his log like a monastery codex, and when the company men came for the page he tore away a corner and pressed it into her great-grandmother's palm. [180] It held half a looping capital and one fleck of gold leaf, bright as a struck match. [197] Penhale swore he had hidden the rest of the page inside the station before the seal went on. [215] No one else alive knew the six had existed, and Ines meant to stand with them anyway. [232]

The only way in now was the old cable trench, and only between tidal changes, in the forty minutes of slack low water when the sea drew back and held its breath. [264] Ines went in on her belly. [270] Outside, breakers detonated against the headland; inside, the sound arrived as pressure without noise, a crashing hush that pushed on her eardrums like a hand. [295] The ceiling dropped until it hung a hand's width above her spine. [307] She counted breaths and watched the cable. [314]

The armored line was buried under silt, invisible, but it ran straight to the cold deep of the Atlantic, and it carried that cold with it. [340] Where it lay, the damp air changed state above it, her breath thickening to fog, vapor beading into a line of dew across the mud like a seam stitched in glass. [371] That was how she tracked invisible things: not by seeing them, but by watching what they did to the air. [391] She followed the dew line through two collapses and a squeeze that made her vision spot white, and then the trench opened into the instrument room. [417]

Galvanometers stood in rows under skins of salt. [425] The cable terminated in a brass junction box, and on its lid someone had scratched six crosses. [442] Ines pried it open. [446] Inside, folded in oilcloth and dry as a prayer, lay the missing page. [459] Penhale's margins bloomed with vines and kingfishers, and down the left edge ran six names, each capital burnished in gold. [479] The last capital was torn in half. [486] Ines laid her corner against it, and the looping letter closed, the gold fleck settling into the bowl of an R like a coal returned to a hearth. [514] Robert Carrow, lineman, aged twenty-two. [519]

She had meant to photograph the page and leave. [528] Instead she said the names aloud, one by one, into the brass box where the line began, as though the cable might still carry them across the seabed to somewhere that would write them down. [563] Thomas Vane, Emlyn Hosking, Jory Tregear, Samuel Ley, Peter Nance, Robert Carrow. [575] The walls took each one and gave nothing back. [584]

Then the fog on the cable thickened, the dew line swelling to rivulets. [597] The tide was turning, pushing warm wet air ahead of it up the trench, and the invisible was becoming water again. [618] Every instinct told her to bolt for open sky. [627] She photographed the page, then wrapped it in her own dry bag and sealed it back inside the box; the names belonged here, where the men had stood, and the pictures would be enough to make the world remember. [666]

The squeeze was worse going out, the water already licking her wrists. [678] She did not count breaths this time. [685] She counted names, six of them, over and over, and found that a narrow place is bearable when you are not alone in it. [709] When she broke into daylight the sea was roaring back over the trench, and behind her, beneath it, the dark kept its hush around six names that were no longer lost. [740]
