Wendell Pike had conducted the Harrow Street Leaf Blower Symphony for nineteen Novembers, and he was conducting still, though no one had pulled a starter cord since the funeral. [29] He stood in the storm culvert beneath the street, the long brick tunnel where the neighbors used to rehearse because it doubled every note and tripled every mistake. [57] On a ledge above the waterline sat the rehearsal hourglass, empty on top, full on the bottom. [74] He had flipped it for the finale that October night, raised his baton, and felt his heart close like a fist just as the final grain slipped through. [102] Nothing had been flipped since. [107] Now it was always afterward, and afterward was cold. [116]

Above him, through the grate and up the drainpipe and into the vents of number fourteen, his wife and son lived in a silence he could taste. [143] Marguerite, who had once laughed so hard at the Blower Symphony that she spilled mulled cider on the mayor, no longer laughed at anything. [167] Theo, fourteen, no longer did anything but sit on his bed turning a black plastic pull cord handle over in his fingers. [189] It had been in Wendell's hand when they found him, snapped from his own blower, because he had yanked the cord with his last strength as if the engine could restart him. [221]

Neither of them spoke of that night. [228] Wendell knew why, the way the dead know things, by listening to what rots beneath words. [244] Theo had refused to come to rehearsal, had called the symphony embarrassing, had stayed home, and believed that if he had been there he could have done something. [272] Marguerite had told Wendell, that same evening, that she was tired of indulging a grown man's noise. [289] Two guilts sat at one table and passed the salt. [299]

Tending the unspoken was the only work left to him, and he went at it mournfully eager, like a man shoveling snow off a grave. [324] He had no voice for speech anymore, only for song, and only half of that. [339] So each night he stood at the mouth of the drain and hummed the lullabies he had half-sung to Theo as a baby, never knowing the second verses, filling the gaps with nonsense. [372] The tunnel took each note and bent it, and the wind that moaned up the pipes carried the tune into the house flat and wavering, a half-step wrong. [400]

The first week, Marguerite locked the vents. [407] The second, Theo slept with the lights on. [415] Wendell kept singing. [418]

He learned that song alone was not enough, so he knelt in the shallow water where the last autumn's leaves had drifted and macerated them between his palms, oak and maple softened into a dark, spiced pulp, the way he used to crush mint for Marguerite's tea. [465] The wind took the smell upward with the tune. [474] Leaf rot and two-stroke oil and a lullaby missing its words: it was November in the Pike house again, whether they wanted it or not. [499]

On the ninth night, Theo came down the ladder into the culvert with a flashlight and the pull cord handle clenched white in his fist. [524] His father's blower still lay where the paramedics had left it, rusting against the wall. [539]

"I should have been here," Theo said to the dark, and the tunnel said it back to him seven times, softer each time, until it was only breath. [567]

Wendell could not answer with words. [573] He sang instead, the one about the cradle and the bough, and when he reached the part he had never known, he sang what he used to sing: "and the bough goes bonk, and the baby goes honk." [611]

Theo went very still. [615]

Then, with shaking hands, he knotted the handle onto the frayed cord, braced his foot on the housing, and pulled. [635] The engine coughed once, a wet, ridiculous blat, and the tunnel doubled it, tripled it, until the whole culvert rang like a brass band falling down stairs. [662]

Wendell raised his baton. [666]

The blat came back off-key, and Theo laughed, a startled bark he tried to swallow and could not. [684] The tunnel took that too, and multiplied it, and the wind lifted it up the drainpipe into the kitchen where Marguerite stood at the sink. [709] She heard her son laughing in a dozen voices, and one of them, underneath, was her husband's. [726] She dropped a plate, and then, God help her, she laughed too. [738]

Below, the hourglass sat spent on its ledge. [746] Wendell did not flip it. [751] He stayed in the afterward, where he belonged, and kept time. [762]
