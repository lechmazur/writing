Ines Calder had inspected craters for the Geological Survey for eleven years, and she had never once stood inside one at night without thinking of her father. [27] He had been the nightwatch at the old observatory on Cinder Peak, a man who walked the crater rim with a lantern and a brass hourglass until the glass cracked in the winter of 1958. [62] Most of the sand ran out into the snow. [71] One grain stayed wedged in the brass, and he gave it to her in a stoppered vial, saying that was the only second he had ever managed to keep. [100] Now the vial hung from a cord around her neck, and a waning moon leaned over the western rim, bright enough to cast her shadow across the cinders. [128] Somewhere past that moon, two men in a spidery lander were sleeping through their final hours before the descent. [147] By afternoon there would be bootprints in the lunar dust, and the face she had studied all her life would carry a human mark forever. [172] She had come to photograph the last untouched moon. [181] It was not a sensible errand, and she knew it. [191] Her supervisor had laughed when she asked for the weekend, and the ranger at the trailhead had warned her the observatory road closed at dusk and would reopen for demolition crews at seven. [224] The old dome was coming down; the new telescopes lived on higher mountains now, pointed by computers. [241] But Ines had always been curious on purpose, the way her father taught her, asking each thing what it remembered. [261] A crater remembered its impact, or its eruption. [269] The moon remembered four billion years of silence. [277] She wanted one frame of it before the silence ended. [287] The trouble was time. [291] Her wristwatch had stopped in the cold around midnight, and the exposures she needed were long, measured in minutes, timed against the moon's slow slide toward the rim where it would set just as the sky paled. [328] So she did what her father had done after the hourglass broke. [340] From her field kit she took the iodine clock: two bottles of clear solution, a rack of test tubes, a dropper. [361] Mixed in the right ratio, the liquids stayed clear for a fixed span and then turned ink-blue all at once, like a thought arriving. [385] He had used them for timed messages, mixing a tube and leaving it by the logbook so the day crew would know, by the color, how long ago he had passed. [416] Ines mixed a row of her own, each weaker than the last, and wrote on masking tape what each should tell her: open, close, open again, stay. [443] The chemistry would remember what her numb mind could not. [453] The cold was the real opponent. [459] Wind came over the crater lip in long, patient pushes, and the cinders ticked against the tripod legs. [477] She wrapped the camera in her coat and sat with her back to the shuttered dome, counting her own breaths, determined to outlast the night the way her father had outlasted forty winters of it. [512] The first tube went blue, and she opened the shutter. [522] The second went blue, and she closed it, and advanced the film with stiff fingers. [537] Between them she lay on the cinders and looked up, asking the moon, as she asked every crater, what it would remember of this night. [562] Toward morning the wind died. [567] It happened so suddenly that she sat up, alarmed, and heard nothing at all: no bird, no engine on the distant highway, not even the tick of cinders. [595] The east had gone the color of weak tea, and the whole mountain seemed to be holding its breath, waiting for the light to speak first. [621] Her father had called it the dawning hush, the one minute of every night he loved best, when the dark was finished and the day had not yet begun. [650] The third tube bloomed. [654] In that hush she made her last exposure, the moon sinking gold-white into the crater rim, its gray seas unbroken, its dust unprinted, exactly as every human being before her had seen it. [687] When the shutter closed, the rim swallowed the last bright sliver, and the first bird began. [703] Ines looked at the fourth tube, the one marked stay, still clear in the rack. [718] She had meant it as a joke, a way to keep herself from leaving early. [733] Now she unstoppered her father's vial and tipped the single grain of hourglass sand into the tube, a second he had kept, given back to the morning. [760] She set it on the dome's doorstep, where the day crew had always found his, and walked down through the brightening cinders to meet the demolition trucks. [787]
