The meter behind Idris Vale's left ear clicked twice as he stepped into the entomologist's greenhouse, which meant he had just been billed for hoping. [25] He smiled anyway, because smiling was still free. [33] Inside, the air was wet enough to drink, and Dr. Hesper Okafor's beetles ticked against their jars like small impatient clocks. [54] Moths slept upside down along the glass roof. [62] Six members of the Tendril Society sat on overturned seed crates between the orchid benches, their own meters glowing a cautious amber. [84]

Ever since the Cognition Levy passed, every citizen paid by the thought, with surcharges for anger, longing, and anything the auditors classed as sedition. [108] Words were worse. [111] A spoken complaint was a thought declared twice, and taxed accordingly. [122] So the Society did not speak its language; it grew it. [133]

Idris had been a professor of dead alphabets before the university sold his chair to the Revenue Office. [151] Now he was a charming rogue scholar with muddy cuffs, a forged volunteer badge for eleven community gardens, and a gourd rattle that had belonged to his grandmother. [179] He shook it once, and the dry seeds inside whispered like rain on tin. [193]

"Bad news first," said Hesper, who never believed in any other order. [205] "The Revenue drones photographed the Mill Street plots yesterday." [214] "They are running our bean rows through a pattern engine." [224]

"Then they will find beans," Idris said. [231]

"They will find you, Idris." [236]

He loved that she spoke plainly; he could not afford to. [247] Every sentence he uttered was built the same way, candidly cryptic, entirely true and impossible to bill, because the meters taxed intent and his intent always lived one layer down. [277] "Then we should give them something worth reading," he said, and only Hesper noticed he was not joking. [294]

For two years they had joined community gardens across the city as ordinary volunteers, planting marigolds and squash in message patterns only members could read. [319] A curve of chard meant meet tonight. [326] Three sunflowers leaning west meant someone had been fined into silence. [337] The auditors saw vegetables, and vegetables carried no intent. [346] But the pattern engine would learn, and Idris had a final sentence to plant before it did. [363]

He opened the rattle and poured all but one of its seeds into Hesper's palm: pale, flat, freckled gourd seeds from a vine his grandmother had bred in a country that no longer existed. [396] "Her gourds listen," he said. [401] "Their tendrils curl toward this exact rattle pitch." [409] "You can warp a whole vine into any shape you like, if you are patient and a little musical." [428]

Hesper studied the seeds the way she studied larvae, for what they would become. [442] "And what shape do you want?" [448]

"A glyph that means this thought is mine." [456]

Hesper sprouted the seeds under her heat lamps, and within a week the Society was carrying seedlings out of the greenhouse in coffee cans, joining the dawn shifts at all eleven gardens. [488] Each night they returned to shake rattles Idris had tuned to match his grandmother's, and each morning the tendrils had warped a little further, coiling into strokes, hooks, and one long defiant bar. [521]

On the ninth morning, everything converged. [527]

The drones arrived at Mill Street just as the glyph finished closing. [539] Commuters stopped on the sidewalk, read the vines, and thought the word before they could help it. [556] Eleven gardens, eleven crowds, thousands of meters flashing red with sedition surcharges at the same instant. [572] The Revenue servers, built to bill one heresy at a time, choked. [584] Meters froze across the city, then went dark. [592]

Back in the greenhouse, alarms shrieked, the auditors kicked in the door, and Hesper, with perfect scientific calm, opened every jar. [613] Beetles boiled into the air; the moths woke and poured down from the roof like a torn curtain; an auditor slipped on spilled potting soil and took an orchid bench with him. [645]

Idris stood in the middle of it, rattle in hand, and felt his own meter, dead now, go cold against his skull. [667] He could have thought anything. [672] Instead he kept very still. [677] One seed remained in the rattle; he had held it back on purpose. [690] Defiance, he had learned, was not a thing you spent all at once and called victory. [706] It was a spark you stood guard over, sheltered from the wind, ready to plant again when they rebuilt the meters, as they surely would. [731]

An auditor seized his collar and demanded to know what the vines meant. [744]

"Squash," said Idris Vale, smiling, and he shook the rattle once, so the last seed whispered to him that it was still there. [767]
