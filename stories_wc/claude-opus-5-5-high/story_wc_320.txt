Ines Calder kept her tools in a canvas roll and her grandmother's star in her breast pocket, because the wind in Tallwire took anything that was not buttoned down. [29] The city hung over the Varrow Gorge on forty-one cables, a lattice of dead radio towers bolted together by people who had needed somewhere high to listen. [56] Most of them had since left. [62] The broadcasts that built the place had ended decades ago, and an ended city is cheap to rent. [80]

Ines was the last licensed celestial signal interpreter on the municipal roll, which meant she climbed the towers when the sky started talking and wrote down what it said. [109] Tonight the sky would talk loudly. [115] The moon was at perigee, swollen and close enough that its pull tightened every cable in the city like a fiddle string, and at perigee the old dishes caught things. [145] Star chatter mostly, and the long drowsy hum the old-timers called tidewhisper. [157] And, for three perigees running, something else from the top of Tower Nine. [170]

She climbed it with a thermos of barley tea, a spool of copper, and a wrench she had named after her ex-husband so she could tighten him with a clear conscience. [201] At the platform she found it where she had left it, lodged in the feedhorn of the great rusted dish: a shard of hush. [225] It was about the length of her thumb and looked like nothing, which was the problem. [241] It was an absence with edges. [247] Sound went into it and did not come out, and the air around it prickled, serenely electric, the way a room feels a second before someone you love says your name. [278]

The council wanted it pried loose and dropped into the gorge. [289] Interference, they said. [292] A dead spot eating the signal. [298] For two perigees Ines had agreed with them, and for two perigees she had failed, because you cannot filter silence out of silence. [321]

Her grandmother would have laughed at her. [328] Delphine Calder had been a radio actress in the last years of broadcasting, a voice so famous they had set her name in a star on a boulevard in Hollywood, and when the boulevard cracked in the long quake her star had cracked with it. [373] She had carried one point of it north: pink terrazzo, a brass rim, and half an engraved microphone. [391] "Everybody thinks the pause is where the show stops," she used to say. [404] "It's where the audience leans in." [410] Ines had taken that as a kind of creed: she went looking for the beginning in every ending, in dead towers and dead marriages and the last flicker of a star that had burned out before anyone on Earth was born. [451]

So tonight she sat down on the grating, poured her tea, and started discarding assumptions one at a time, the way she would strip corroded wire. [477] Assume the hush is broken. [482] Discard it; nothing broken is that calm. [489] Assume it is blocking the signal. [495] Discard that too; the tidewhisper always arrived strongest in the seconds right after the hush swallowed it, as if the silence were a breath taken before speech. [522] Assume, finally, that the signal was ever meant for the dish. [533]

She looked at the moon, enormous and patient above the gorge, and then at the fragment she had drawn from her pocket. [555] Terrazzo and brass: a thing made to be walked over, made to hold a name in public, made to be remembered without making a sound. [580] The hush was not a flaw in the receiver. [589] It was a receiver, and it had no way to speak. [600]

She wound copper around the star's brass rim, clipped the lead to the feedhorn, and set the fragment against the shard like a tuning fork against a table. [628] Then she began to tune, turning the fragment a hair at a time, listening not for sound but for the shape of the quiet changing. [653] At the eleventh degree the brass warmed. [660] The hush did not break. [665] It opened. [667]

Out of it, clear as a studio at midnight, came a woman's voice she had not heard in nineteen years, reading the opening line of a serial that had gone off the air before Ines was born: "Good evening, listeners, and welcome back." [710] Every broadcast Tallwire had ever sent had gone outward, and some of it had been waiting at the edge of the quiet for someone to lean in. [737]

Tomorrow she would tell the council to leave the shard exactly where it was. [751] Ines uncapped her pen, steadied her notebook against the wind, and wrote down the first word. [767]
