The water had reached the third row of the stalls by the time Ines Farrow waded back into the pit, her cello case held above her head like an offering. [30] She had hung the lanterns herself, six of them along the brass rail, and their flames stuttered in the draft from the broken stage doors. [55] For twenty-two years she had played from this trench, invisible, reading other people's notes. [69] Lately, though, something had changed. [74] Lately she woke with melodies already moving in her fingers, phrases no composer had written, and she had begun to suspect they were not entirely her own. [101] The inspiration had started the week the river came through the foundations. [113] It had started with the program. [119]

She had found it floating against her chair on the first night, an antique opera program from November 1911, the premiere of Aurelio Vance's The Drowned Bell. [146] Every musician in the city knew the story: the river had risen during the final act, the audience had fled, and the principal cellist, Margit Held, had never been found. [176] The opera's last chord had never been played. [184]

Now the program lay open on her music stand, swollen and soft as bread. [198] The water had done something strange to it. [206] Ink from the cast list had bled into the margins, and in the blank spaces between the printed names, the stains had bloomed into images. [231] A hand holding a bow. [236] A spiral staircase descending into waves. [242] And along the bottom of each page, faint brown marks spaced like notes on a staff that had not been printed there. [264] Each night the images shifted a little, as if the damp were still writing. [278]

Ines tuned in the lantern light and waited. [286] She had learned that the ghost did not speak. [295] Margit answered in other ways: a ripple crossing the flooded pit when no wind moved, a lantern dimming at the end of a phrase, a single page of the program turning on its own. [329] Their conversation had no words, only questions asked with the bow and answered with the house itself. [346]

For six nights Ines had played the marks as written, reading the stains as pitches, and every time the lanterns guttered in something like disappointment. [371] The notes made no melody. [376] They sounded like a sentence with every other word missing. [386]

Tonight she leaned closer and saw what she had missed. [396] The marks did not sit on the lines of the imagined staff. [408] They sat between them, in the gaps, and they were not notes at all but rests, placed exactly where The Drowned Bell's final chorale drew breath. [434] Margit had not written a melody. [440] She had written the silences, the in-between spaces where a cellist waits and counts and listens to everyone else. [459]

Ines understood then. [462] To finish the opera, she would have to play where no one was meant to play. [478] She would have to disrupt the score. [485]

It went against everything the pit had taught her. [494] A pit musician blended; she did not interrupt. [502] But she set her bow on the C string and began the chorale from memory, and each time the written music paused, she filled the pause instead, low and steady, turning every rest into a voice. [538]

The flooded house responded. [542] Ripples ran outward from her chair in rings that met and crossed. [554] The lanterns, one by one, stopped flickering and burned clean. [564] Somewhere beneath the water, a second cello seemed to hum along, so quiet she felt it more in her sternum than her ears. [587]

Then she reached the end, the place where the premiere had broken off. [600] The final chord waited, unstruck for more than a century, and the hush around it was so complete that Ines could hear the lantern oil tick in its reservoirs. [629] She held her bow above the strings. [636] She did not know whether she was supposed to play it or leave it. [650]

A page of the program turned. [656] On the back cover, where the water had pooled longest, a new image had surfaced: two bows touching the same string. [676]

Ines drew the chord. [680]

For one breath the hall rang as if it were full, a thousand dry seats, a chandelier blazing, a woman in black silk beside her with her eyes closed. [709] Then only water, and six steady flames. [716]

When Ines looked down, the program's pages had faded to clean cream, every stain gone. [731] She tucked it inside her case anyway. [738] In the morning she would begin writing her own music in its empty margins, and she would leave room in every measure for someone to answer. [764]
