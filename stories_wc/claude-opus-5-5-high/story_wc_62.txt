Maren Voss carved names for a living, and she did it slowly, because a name cut too fast would chip where the grief was deepest. [25] When the trawler Constance went down in September with eleven men aboard, the harbor council asked her for one stone to hold them all. [49] She said yes, and then she could not begin. [58] A single grief she understood; she had shaped hundreds. [67] But eleven griefs pressed into one granite block had no shape she could find, and she would not cut until she found it. [90]

So she went, as she always went when stuck, to the ancient greenhouse behind the ruined manor, a ribcage of iron and clouded glass older than any record, where her grandmother Oda had taught her to hold a chisel. [129] It was dusk, and the monarchs had begun arriving. [138] Every September they poured down the coast and roosted in the greenhouse's broken eaves, thousands of them folding shut at once, and the sound they made settling was like a long exhale from something enormous. [173] Maren sat on the potting bench, closed her eyes, and let her breathing fall into that rhythm until the world narrowed to a single point, the way Oda had drilled into her. [205] Her meditation was not gentle; it was fierce, a clenched stillness, a fist of attention. [220]

When she opened her eyes, Oda was sitting across from her, dead eight months and entirely unbothered by it. [239] The ghost was translucent as the glass, butterflies visible through her shoulders. [251]

"You brought the ribbon," Oda said. [257]

Maren had not known she had, but there it was in her coat pocket: the woven ribbon roll, fat as a fist, that Oda had kept on the dinner table for fifty years. [290] In the Voss house, supper ended with a custom older than anyone's memory: each person traded a favorite poem with someone across the table, reciting it aloud, and Oda wove a band of colored thread into the ribbon for each exchange. [331] Blue for sea poems, black for elegies, gold for anything that made her laugh. [345]

"Retrace it," Oda said. [349] "With your fingers, not your eyes." [355]

Maren unspooled the first yard and ran her thumb along it, feeling the threads rise and sink like lettering under a rubbing. [377] Here was the thick black knot from the winter her grandfather drowned; here the dense stretch after, where the whole family had traded elegies for a month, the bands crowding tight, then loosening, then blooming into gold. [414]

"One grief is a knot," said Oda. [421] "You want to know what many look like." [429] "Then feed it more." [433]

So across the weeks of the migration, while monarchs came and rested and went on south, Maren did the only thing her family knew. [457] She carried a long table into the greenhouse and invited the families of the Constance to dinner at dusk. [476] They came warily, then in numbers. [482] The cook's widow traded a hymn for the mate's son's limerick. [493] The captain's mother recited Tennyson with the rain loud on the glass, and a deckhand's sister answered with a rap verse her brother had written in his phone. [521] Maren wove a band for every trade, and each night Oda stood behind her chair and made her retrace the new stretch in the dark. [546]

At first the ribbon was all black, snarled, eleven threads fighting for the same space. [561] By the third week, colors twined. [567] A blue ran beside a gold beside a black, and because each poem was traded, not merely spoken, no band lay alone; every thread crossed another family's thread. [595]

On the last night of the migration, Maren sat fiercely still while the final roost lifted from the eaves in one soft orange thunder and spiraled up into the dark. [625] She was holding the ribbon, and she felt it before she saw it. [638] Grief shared was not a block. [644] It was a braid, a spiral, each strand held up by the others, rising the way the butterflies rose, no single wing carrying the weight. [669]

"There," said Oda, and for the first time she sounded tired. [680] "Now you do not need me to hold the chisel." [690]

When Maren looked up, there was only glass and the last pale wings. [703]

She carved the Constance stone all winter: a granite column wound with eleven braided bands, and cut into every band, a line of poem traded across a table. [731] She cut slowly, so nothing would chip. [738] In September the monarchs came back and rested on it. [748]
