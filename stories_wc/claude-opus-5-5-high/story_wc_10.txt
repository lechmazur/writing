The winterglass conservatory had been built to hold heat, but after noise became impossible it held something stranger. [18] Frost grew on the inside of its panes in feathers, and wherever a warm body stood near, the feathers thinned into a ghost of that body, bright at the heart and blurred at the edges. [53]

Ilse Varga had learned to read those ghosts the way her father once read piano strings. [69] A clenched jaw melted the frost in two hard crescents. [79] Joy spread outward from the sternum like spilled tea. [88] Grief, she had decided years ago, was cold: it left hands and faces sharp and unmelted, a person reduced to a dark outline. [111]

She was a capsize survivor, one of eleven pulled from the Danube the morning the air went still. [129] She had been underwater when it happened, so the last sound she ever heard was the river filling her ears, and when she surfaced into a world that no longer carried anything, she thought for a whole minute that she had died. [171] Nobody screamed on the bank. [176] Mouths opened and nothing came out, and the gulls wheeled overhead like pages turning in a closed book. [194]

Now she kept her father's tuning fork case on the potting bench beside the orchids. [209] It was walnut, lined with green felt gone bald at the corners, and it held thirteen steel forks, one for every semitone from A to A. [235] The air refused them, but bone did not. [243] Struck and pressed to a jaw or collarbone, a fork still hummed through the body, a private pitch no one else could share. [266] These were the unshaken marvels of the new world: the orchids that bloomed without birdsong, the frost that wrote whoever stood near it, the steel that kept its note when the sky would not. [300]

Ilse wanted to translate silence into music, and the conservatory was her instrument. [313] Visitors came on Sundays and stood among the ferns while she photographed their frost-ghosts, charted the warm and cold of them, and assigned each temperature a fork. [340] Then she seated them and pressed the sequence against their skin, one note at a time, a melody built from their own heat. [363] Warmth went high, near the top A. [370] Cold went low. [373] People wept, or laughed with their shoulders, and left. [382]

On the first Sunday of February a woman came alone. [392] Ilse knew her before she took off her hat: Kata Farkas, who had been piloting the ferry. [409] The inquiry had cleared her; the barge's horn had simply stopped existing halfway through its blast. [425] Clearing had not helped. [429] Kata stood before the frost with her arms folded, and Ilse, who had not forgiven her, raised the camera anyway. [449]

The ghost that bloomed on the glass was wrong. [458] It should have been dark, a cold outline of guilt. [468] Instead the throat burned white, the melt so fierce it ran in rivulets, and the hands, pressed flat to the ribs, glowed like coals banked for the night. [496] Kata was silently expressive in the way of people who had spent years unable to shout, her whole grief pushed to the surface of her skin. [522]

Ilse looked at the photograph for a long time. [531] Then she walked to the pane nearest the bench and stood where she always avoided standing, and let the frost take her. [553] Her own throat burned the same white. [560]

Every chart she had made was upside down. [568] Grief was not cold; it was the hottest thing in a body, the part still thrashing toward the surface. [587] She had scored the mourners low and dull because she wanted grief to be something that could go numb. [606]

She opened the tuning fork case and began to rectify the score, lifting forks from their felt beds and reordering them, grief moved to the high bright A. [634] Kata watched, not understanding, until Ilse gestured her to a chair. [645] Ilse struck the top A against her knee and pressed its stem to Kata's collarbone. [660] Kata's eyes widened. [663] Then, before the hum could fade, Ilse leaned in until her own collarbone met the steel beside Kata's, and the note ran through both of them at once. [691]

It was not a private pitch after all. [699] Around them the frost on the winterglass thinned into two bright shapes leaning together, and for the length of one note the silence was music. [724]
