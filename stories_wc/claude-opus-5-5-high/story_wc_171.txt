The terrace had been old before the war began, and the war was two hundred and nine years old. [19] Its flagstones were split by thyme and lizards, and its balustrade had lost so many pillars that it looked like a mouth with half its teeth. [45] Still, on midsummer night, when the red moon and the white one crossed paths above the valley, it was the only place where the Aunor and the Kessite could stand without shooting. [77]

Mira Tollen arrived an hour before the delegations, carrying the ebony clipboard against her chest like a shield she did not trust. [99] It had been carved from the last black tree of the river forest, and every negotiator since the first had carried it up these steps. [124] Its back was scored with thirty-one notches, one for each treaty that had been signed here and broken before the next harvest. [146] Mira was the thirty-second negotiator, and she had decided that she would not add a notch. [162]

The old treaties had failed, she believed, because they were beautiful. [173] They promised eternal brotherhood and the healing of ancestral wounds, and they said nothing about who would repair the irrigation gates or which village kept the ferry on alternate days. [203] Her draft was forty pages of gates and ferries, grazing rights and grain weights, and it was clipped to the ebony board beneath a single line of ceremony, because the ritual required one. [236] She wanted her ideals nailed to things that could rust, so that people would notice when they needed oiling. [255]

The delegations came up the eastern and western stairs as the moons began their slow approach, each party exactly nine people, each unarmed by custom. [280] The Kessite elder, a woman named Hasra with burn scars climbing her neck, read the draft by lamplight while the red moon edged toward the white. [306] Mira worked the way she always did, operatively gentle, a surgeon's softness that never stopped cutting. [322] She conceded the ferry, clarified the weights, let the Aunor general win an argument about fence posts that she had planted for him to win. [347] By the time the moons touched, every clause stood. [356]

Then Hasra set down the pages and said, "There is a price the paper does not name." [373]

The oldest rite of the terrace, older than either people, held that peace was bought by the one who had done the worst harm present. [398] That person broke the ebony board across the altar stone, and then belonged to the other side for the length of a life, working their fields and eating at their tables. [429] No treaty had ever honored it, because no one on the terrace had ever confessed. [444]

The Aunor general looked at his boots. [451] The Kessite captains looked at the moons. [458] Mira looked at Hasra's scars and remembered the shell battery at Vell Crossing, twenty years ago, and the coordinates she had read aloud as a nineteen-year-old spotter, and the market town that had burned for two days. [495] She had become a negotiator because of that town, and she had told herself that forty pages of ferries were payment enough. [517] But she had spent her career insisting that ideals meant nothing until someone carried them, and now the ritual was asking whether she meant it. [542]

It took her until the red moon had swallowed half the white to muster her voice. [558]

"Vell Crossing," she said. [562] "I called the guns." [566]

Hasra did not smile, but something in her face unclenched, as if she had been holding a weight for a very long time and had finally found someone to hand it to. [598]

Hasra and the general signed, and Mira witnessed, then unclipped the pages and handed a copy to each. [616] She laid the empty board across the altar stone, its thirty-one notches facing the sky. [631] The ebony was harder than she expected, and it did not break cleanly. [644] It split along the oldest notch in a long ragged seam, and she cut her palm on the edge, and a thin line of blood ran into the grain. [673] She gave one half to Hasra and kept the other, because a price should be carried where it can be seen. [694]

Above them the moons began to part, slow as old dancers, the red one trailing a little behind. [712] Mira walked down the western stairs with the Kessite, toward fields she had once set burning, and did not look back at the broken terrace, which was lovely precisely where it was cracked. [745] In the morning, she thought, someone would have to oil the irrigation gates, and she knew exactly who. [763]
