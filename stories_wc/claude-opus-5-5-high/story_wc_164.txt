Nell Brine was a recollectionist made mostly of sea-foam, which meant she had perhaps one good season left before she fizzed away entirely. [23] She did not mind much. [28] "Foam is supposed to go," she liked to tell the stalagmites, "that is the whole charm of it." [46] Her workshop sat in the belly of the Hollow Bell, a geode so large that whole villages of cave-folk had been born and buried inside its violet walls. [74] Light entered through one high fracture and broke across the crystals into slow, oily rainbows, so that everything in the cave looked faintly like the inside of a soap bubble. [104] Nell found this appropriate. [108]

The cave-folk came to her before memory faded, carrying the feelings they could no longer name. [124] Nell transliterated emotions: she would press a damp palm to someone's wrist, feel the ache there, and write it out in the old tide-script, curl by curl, onto slate. [153] A grandmother's grief became three looping waves and a dot. [163] A boy's first love became a spiral that would not close. [174] Once written, a feeling could be reread long after the memory behind it had gone soft. [190]

But lately the feelings arrived with missing pieces. [198] Old Tamsin could feel the joy of a walk she used to take, but not where it had gone; the spiral of it trailed off into blank stone. [226] And when Nell went looking, the passage Tamsin described was simply not there, sealed by amethyst as smooth as if it had never been cut. [251] It was not the only one. [257] Paths were disappearing all over the Hollow Bell, and nobody could say why. [270] Worse, Nell's own path to the sea, the crooked tunnel she had crawled up as a newborn froth, had begun to narrow. [292]

"Well," she said brightly to no one, "that is a mystery with a deadline." [306]

She needed to see what she could not see, so she took down the crater glass. [322] It was a green, bubbled lens, fused from desert sand the night a star fell, and anything viewed through it showed the heat of what had once happened there. [351] Impact leaves an echo, the old recollectionists said. [359] Through the glass, the sealed passages glowed faintly, like breath on a cold window. [373] But the glow was fading, and she could not tell what it meant. [386]

The answer came with the godwit. [392] Every autumn a single bar-tailed godwit flew through the high fracture on its way south, resting one night on the crystal ledges, and every autumn it dropped a scatter of seeds carried from somewhere green. [427] Nell had always swept them up. [433] This year she watched where they fell. [440] Through the crater glass, the seeds were tiny coals, and they did not land at random. [456] They rolled, as if drawn by warmth, and gathered at the mouths of the sealed passages, thickest where the old glow still burned. [479]

Nell sat down on a cold ledge and allowed herself to conjecture. [491] A path, she guessed, was not only stone; it was the feeling of the people who walked it, pressed into the rock like heat into glass. [517] When the last person forgot how a path had felt, the geode, which hated a hollow, simply healed over it. [537] The hole in Tamsin's joy and the wall in the tunnel were the same wound. [552] And the seeds, which had come from far away and remembered warm places, could find what was still warm enough to save. [574]

It was only a guess. [579] She tested it anyway. [583]

She brought Tamsin to the brightest cluster, held her wrist, and read the feeling aloud in tide-script, curl by curl, filling in the missing turns with what the seeds' glow showed her: a ledge, a pool, a sister laughing. [622] Tamsin gasped, then wept, then laughed, and the amethyst in front of them cracked like river ice. [639] The seeds sprouted in the crack, pale green threads holding it open. [651]

Over the following weeks Nell reopened eleven passages. [659] She did not reopen her own. [665] There were no seeds at its mouth, and she understood why: nobody but Nell had ever walked it, and foam holds heat poorly. [688]

So on the last warm night of the season she wrote the feeling of it onto a slate, three bright curls for the smell of salt, and tucked the slate beneath the godwit's ledge. [722] Maybe next autumn someone would read it, and remember a sea they had never seen, and a seed would roll their way. [744]

"Paths go," she told the stalagmites cheerfully, already a little thinner. [755] "The trick is leaving a crumb." [761]
