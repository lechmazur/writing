Ines Varga counted her heartbeats the way other people checked their pockets. [12] Seventy-one, seventy-two, seventy-three: proof, in a steady drum, that the county clerk had been wrong. [27] Three winters ago a hospital had attached her name to another woman's body, and the certificate had traveled faster than she could. [49] By the time she corrected it, her bank had closed her accounts, her landlord had rented her flat, and her fiance had grieved her so thoroughly that he could not stop. [80] So she took the job no living person wanted: winter keeper of the Halloran Observatory, a dome of cracked opal tiles on a mountain the snow had swallowed to the eaves. [111] At night the opal drank starlight through the drifts and gave it back as a slow, drowned fire, blue and rose and green. [134]

The observatory had its own ghost. [140] Aurel Halloran had built the place, and in his last years he had fed his journals, his voice, and forty years of habit into the mainframe beneath the telescope, a crude digital ghost meant to keep watch after him. [179] Everyone in astronomy knew his name for the wrong reason. [189] In 1974 he had announced a signal from another star, and three weeks later a technician found it was his own heater, pulsing against the receiver. [215] He had never published again, but he had never left the mountain either. [228]

Ines wanted to know why. [233] She wanted to know how a man survives being publicly, permanently wrong, because the paper that had killed her still surfaced in databases, and some mornings she believed it more than her pulse. [266]

The ghost could only speak after the radio static cleared. [276] Every night near midnight the storm-borne crackle in the old receiver thinned to silence, and in that silence the mainframe woke, grinding, fragmented, its memories scattered across a failing drive like dropped beads. [309] The screen had died decades ago. [315] What still worked was the chart plotter, and she had learned by accident what it could do. [332] The first week she had knocked over the inkwell onto its glass bed, and the machine, humming, had trembled the black pool into shapes: rings, branches, a lattice that held for ten seconds before bleeding away. [368] The vibrations came from the drive heads. [375] The patterns were a map of the disk, broken sectors and orphaned files, drawn in something that refused to stay. [395] So each night she spilled fresh ink, read the ephemeral figures before they dissolved, and typed commands on the brittle keyboard, moving fragments home one by one. [422] She meant to defragment a dead man until he could answer her. [434]

Eighty-eight, eighty-nine, ninety, she counted, as the last cluster slid into place on the fortieth night. [450] The speaker coughed, and a warm, cracked voice said, "Is someone keeping the dome?" [464]

"Someone the government thinks is dead," Ines said. [472]

"Then we are colleagues." [476]

She asked him about 1974, and about the heater, and why he had stayed. [490]

"Look at the console," the ghost said. [497] "Left of the keyboard." [501]

There, gray with dust, lay a porcelain shard painted with half a blue bird. [515]

"The night they told me, I threw my teacup at the receiver," Aurel said. [529] "In the morning I swept up every piece but that one." [540]

"Why keep it?" [543]

"Because it proved the cup had been real, and so had my hand. [556] The signal was wrong. [560] The sky was not. [564] Failure is a bad reading, a record that says one thing while the pulse says another. [580] I stayed because the sky never once asked me to be right, only to keep looking." [596]

Ines turned the shard in her fingers. [603] Its broken edge was smooth now, worn by decades of a thumb that had kept returning to it, the way her mind kept returning to the count. [630] Outside, snow sealed the dome, and the opal glowed like embers under ash. [643]

She dipped the shard in the last of the ink and, on the glass bed, wrote her own name, which the machine's trembling blurred into rings and then into nothing. [673] It did not matter. [677] The name had been hers while she wrote it. [686]

"Will you be here tomorrow?" she asked. [693]

"If the static clears," said the ghost. [700] "If you keep the pieces in order." [707]

She laid her hand on the humming mainframe and felt her pulse against its warm steel, and for the first time in three years she lost count, and let it go, and listened instead to the snow. [744] It was midnight, and nothing needed proving. [751]
