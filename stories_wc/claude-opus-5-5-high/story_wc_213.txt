Ines Varga had tested the storm siren of Saint Oda's every Sunday for eleven years, and she had come to hate the sound of her own job. [27] The sanctuary stood on a knuckle of granite above the clouds, and the wind never stopped combing it. [45] Below, hidden under that white floor, lay the three villages of the Aurel valley, and it was for them that the siren screamed. [68] They no longer looked up when it did. [76]

She knew this because she had asked. [83] Last autumn she had walked down through the fog with a notebook and knocked on doors, a small woman in a patched oilskin, and the answers had been kind and devastating. [114] The siren was weather, the baker said, like wind in the chimney. [126] The children called it the Sunday goose. [133] Only the old cathedral bell made them stop, the shepherd's widow told her, because it rang so rarely that nobody could pretend not to hear it. [159]

The bell had not rung in four years, because its rope had rotted through at the pulley. [176] Ines had spliced it herself on the morning of the solstice, binding the frayed ends with linen and hide glue. [196] "You may ring it after the glue dries," Brother Anselm had said, not unkindly, "and not one minute before." [215] Hide glue in this cold took the whole day, sometimes longer. [226]

So she waited, and while she waited she did what she always did, which was measure. [242] Over the years she had placed small thermometers in twelve corners of the sanctuary: the cloister well, the goat shed, the lee of the apse, the lip of the north terrace where the cloud sea lapped like milk. [280] She tied red wool to the railings and wrote down how it leaned. [293] Nobody had asked her to track the microclimate variations of a mountaintop, but a tester who only pressed a button, she believed, was not really testing anything. [320]

That afternoon the numbers disagreed with each other in a way she had seen only once before. [337] The cloister well was warmer than the apse, which was impossible in December, and the red wool on the north terrace hung perfectly still while the wool ten meters east stood straight out toward the valley. [373] Cold air was sliding off the summit in a thin, fast river, pooling beneath the cloud deck. [390] Nine years ago the same pattern had come before the ice storm that dropped the power lines at Aurel Low and froze two drovers on the pass. [417] She checked each reading twice, then a third time, because curiosity that could not survive its own doubt was only a hunch in a nice coat. [443] The numbers held. [446]

The monks called midnight on the solstice zero hour, the still point where the year stopped falling and began to climb. [467] By long custom, the siren was tested then, a single long wail to mark the turn. [483] Everyone in the valley expected it, which meant everyone in the valley would sleep through it. [499]

At a quarter to twelve Ines stood in the siren house with her hand on the brass switch and listened. [519] The wind had dropped to almost nothing, the deep solstice hush that made the stars seem close enough to bruise. [539] Somewhere far below, a dog barked and was answered. [548] She thought of the baker, the children, the widow. [557] Then she threw the switch to off, locked the siren house, and walked across the frozen cloister to the tower. [577]

Brother Anselm was waiting there with a lantern, as if he had known. [590] "The glue," he said. [594]

She pressed her thumbnail into the splice. [601] It was hard as horn. [606] "It dried an hour ago," she said, "and there is ice coming down the pass tonight, and they have stopped hearing the siren." [629] She showed him the notebook, the columns of numbers, the sketch of the cold river. [644] He read it by lantern light for a long moment. [654] Then he set the lantern down and wrapped his hands around the cathedral bell rope just above hers. [672]

They pulled together. [675] The first stroke went out over the clouds like a stone dropped into still water, low and round and impossible to mistake. [697] The splice held. [700] They rang twelve strokes, and then they waited, and in that waiting Ines heard what she had climbed so many stairs to hear. [723] Beneath the cloud floor, faint as breath, doors were opening. [733] A shutter banged; a man called a name; far off, a flock bell clanked as someone drove the sheep toward the barns. [755]

At twenty past midnight the first pellets of ice began to tick against the tower slates. [771] Ines let go of the rope and stood very still, listening, which was the whole of her job. [789]
