The shell was so large that no one inside it had ever seen its outside. [15] It lay half-sunk in a warm lagoon, a gargantuan spiral of pearl and bone, and the tides breathed through its whorls like a sleeper. [39] Everyone slept but Wren. [43] Wren was a poet, and an insomniac, and she suspected the two were the same illness. [59] The wind that moved through the shell's chambers came out off-key, a sour third that the others had long ago learned to ignore, and it scraped at her all night. [89]

For three hundred years the Upper Whorl and the Lower Whorl had been at war over the shell's single shaft of light. [111] It fell from the apex, down the hollow columella, onto a gray tree that had never flowered, because each side had bolted iron shutters across the shaft to keep the other from its share. [145] The oldest treaty, carved into the tree's roots, promised a blossom armistice: on the morning the tree bloomed, the war would end, and no one could lift a spear beneath its petals. [177] Everyone knew it was a joke written by people who had already closed the shutters. [192]

Wren kept the other relic of that first century in her pillowcase: half a postcard, soft as cloth, printed with a spiral galaxy. [215] A traveler had brought it from somewhere with a sky, and two lovers, one from each whorl, had torn it apart the night the fighting began, each keeping a half. [245] On the back of hers, faded to rust, was one line: Until the arms meet the heart again. [263] Across centuries of longing, the card had been handed down like a family nose. [277]

Wren was too shy to speak at councils, too shy to meet the eyes of the guards at the shutters, but she was not too shy to sing into the dark, because the dark did not look back. [315] Her mother had died before finishing any of the old lullabies, so Wren knew only halves. [331] She sang them anyway, leaning into the draft, letting the off-key wind carry each broken tune down the spiral toward the enemy. [353] She had a private ambition she never said aloud: to photosynthesize pain into beauty, the way the gray tree was meant to do with light. [378]

On the forty-first night, the wind came back up with the other half. [391] A thin voice from the Lower Whorl finished her lullaby about the moon-crab, note for note, just as off-key. [410] Wren sat down hard on the pearl floor and laughed until she cried, then sang the start of another. [429] The voice finished that one too. [435]

For a season they traded halves across the dark, and she learned things from the words he tucked between verses: that the singer's name was Oro, that he also could not sleep, that he also kept half a card with a galaxy on it. [479] His half had the bright core. [485] Hers had the arms. [489]

She did not ask the council for permission, because she could not have gotten the words out. [506] Instead, one night she simply climbed. [512] She was shyly unstoppable, apologizing under her breath to every rung of the columella ladder, murmuring sorry to the sleeping guard as she stepped over his boots. [539] At the gray tree she found a hollow in its trunk the size of a cupped hand, and there she ensconced her half of the galaxy, pressing it into the old wood like a seed. [574] Then she sang the first half of the oldest lullaby, the one both whorls had once shared, and waited. [593]

Oro came up from below, a lanky young man with salt in his eyebrows, singing the ending, and slid his half of the card beside hers. [619] The arms met the heart. [624]

Together, without a word, they unbolted the shutters from both sides of the shaft. [638] It was high tide, and the light that fell came through the thin pearl of the apex and the green water above it, wobbling with the shadows of fish and coral. [669] It was not bright, but it was enough. [677]

By dawn the gray tree had opened a thousand blossoms the color of the inside of a shell, and the reef-shadows swam over them so that every petal seemed to be laughing. [709] The two whorls woke and came down with their spears, and stood beneath the petals, and could not lift them. [729] Nobody knew who to blame, and after a while nobody wanted to. [741]

Wren hid behind the trunk, red to the ears, and wrote the first whole poem of her life on her palm. [762] That night, for the first time in years, she slept, and somewhere below her, humming off-key, so did Oro. [781]
