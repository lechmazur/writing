Magistra Ilse Brandt arrived at the Hallervik Conservatory an hour early, because a philosopher who duels must always be first to the question. [23] The glass atrium stood beside a brook so quiet it seemed to be thinking, and inside, the Orchid Society was preparing a gala with none of the brook's composure. [52] Waiters in white gloves chased two geese between the benches, a cellist tuned against a leaking fountain, and a woman in pearls directed everyone with a trowel. [79] Ilse sat on her blade case and waited for Magister Corvin Lund, who had challenged her to settle their long quarrel over whether a question could ever be finished. [108] She believed it could. [112] A question, she taught, was a road, and roads existed to deliver people somewhere; Corvin believed roads were for walking, forever, admiring the ditches. [136] Twelve years of this quarrel had cost her friendships, a teaching post, and, on one icy morning when a student's blade slipped, her left arm below the elbow. [164] The replacement was brass and walnut, geared finer than a clock, and it clicked softly whenever she was about to be rude. [186]

At a quarter past ten, Corvin's second, a nervous boy with ink on his cuffs, delivered a blue Danish biscuit tin instead of his master. [211] Ilse knew such tins; every grandmother in Jutland kept thread in one, and nobody had ever found a biscuit inside. [231] This one held only a sewing needle card, the old paper kind printed with a mermaid, its needles pulled from their neat rows and restuck at crooked, deliberate angles. [260] "He says he is delayed," the boy said, and fled from a goose. [273]

Ilse turned the card in the green light. [281] In the center of the atrium floor lay a sundial the old head gardener had been painting when he died, its morning half bright with hour lines and a Latin motto, its afternoon half bare stone. [317] Corvin had lectured beside it for a decade. [325] She knelt, laid the card at the gnomon's foot, and let her mechanical fingers lock each needle's tilt with the patience of a surveyor. [349] Every angle matched an hour line, and every hour line ran out to a letter of the motto: Horas non numero nisi serenas. [372] Where the lines crossed into unpainted stone, she supplied the missing letters from memory, because she had hated that motto for years. [394] It counted only the pleasant hours, and she preferred to count them all. [407]

The needles spelled MANE SINE ENSE. [413] Remain, without the sword. [417] It was an overture, hidden where only she would bother to look, and it was also, she realized, a question. [437] Corvin was asking whether twelve years could end in something other than a winner. [451]

He arrived at eleven, hatless, wet from the brook path, while the gala guests assembled on the benches to watch the famous duel they had been promised. [478] The woman with the trowel rang a teacup for silence. [488] Ilse stood, and her arm clicked. [494] "You sent me needles," she said. [500] "I sent you a door," Corvin said. [507] "You may walk through it or stab it." [515]

She drew her blade, and the audience sighed with relief. [525] A diplomat would have refused the fight; a fighter would have refused the door; Ilse had always intended to be both, and fiercely. [548] They fenced across the sundial while the cellist, uncertain of etiquette, began Bach. [561] A goose joined them. [565] Corvin parried like a man asking questions, always circling, and Ilse pressed like a woman answering them, driving him step by step off the painted morning and onto the bare afternoon stone. [597] Her brass wrist turned his blade once, twice, and on the third touch she did not strike him but set her point against the unpainted hour line where the next letter should have been. [631] Old chalk from the gardener's guidelines rose in a pale puff. [642]

"I accept your question," she said, loud enough for the benches, "on the condition that it goes somewhere." [660] Corvin looked down at the blank half of the dial. [670] "Where?" [671] "Here," Ilse said, sheathing her sword. [677] "We finish the afternoon together, every hour, the unpleasant ones included, and we teach whoever comes to watch." [695]

For a long moment the only sound was the brook, patient as ever, and a goose eating an orchid. [714] Then Corvin laughed, bowed with enormous dignity, and borrowed a needle from the tin to scratch the first new line into the stone. [737] The Orchid Society applauded, having understood nothing, which Ilse decided was an excellent place for a school to begin. [756]
