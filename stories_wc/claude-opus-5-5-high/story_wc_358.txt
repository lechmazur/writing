The port of Halvard went silent on a Tuesday, when the chlorine alarm stopped wailing and the last evacuation bus crossed the bridge. [23]
Three days later, Ines Varga walked into the container yard with a respirator on her hip and a roll of orange survey tape in her fist. [49]
She was an accident investigator, and accidents were always quieter afterward. [60]
The stacks rose around her in rust red and faded blue, six containers high, forming corridors so narrow the fog seemed to have been poured into them. [87]
Somewhere in the middle of that labyrinth lay the freighter. [97]
It had come in low over the harbor on final approach to the airstrip beyond the yard, dropped forty meters in a heartbeat, and clipped a stack of tank containers that split and bled pale gas into the streets. [136]
The crew had lived. [140]
The pilots blamed a microburst. [145]
The weather office said there had been no storm cell within ninety kilometers. [158]
Ines did not trust either answer, because both had been given before anyone had looked. [173]
She had spent nineteen years standing in wreckage, explaining with great care what everyone should have seen coming. [191]
Her reports were precise and they were always late. [200]
This time there was a reason to be early: at dusk, a heavy-lift helicopter would fly the same approach to hoist the tank containers out before the tide rose, and nobody had told it what had eaten the freighter. [239]
She began to survey the corridors the old way. [248]
At every intersection she tied a strip of tape to a lashing rod, let it stream, and wrote the bearing, the time, and the angle of flutter in a waterproof notebook. [279]
She was documenting wind patterns between buildings, she told herself, because that was what the stacks had become: a city with no doors, whose streets had been rearranged by crane operators the week before the crash. [315]
The yard manager had called the new layout efficient. [324]
On her sketch it looked like a funnel. [332]
The sea breeze entered from the south through three long alleys, and in each one the tape leaned inward, toward a single point near the yard's heart. [359]
She followed the lean. [363]
Where the alleys met, the containers had been stacked into a hollow square, and the fog inside it did not drift but sank, slow and steady, like water down a drain. [394]
Three streams of air poured over the stacks, met above the square, and, with nowhere else to go, fell through it in a column. [418]
A singular convergence, she wrote, and underlined it twice, because no weather office would ever see a storm that lived inside a parking lot of steel boxes. [445]
On the ground at the center of the square lay everything light the wind had carried in: paper cups, shrink-wrap, a child's glove, and a small embroidered patch with a turbine and wings stitched in gold. [481]
FLIGHT ENGINEER, it read, above the freighter company's crest. [490]
She had interviewed the man who wore it. [498]
He had torn his jacket climbing out of the wreck half a kilometer away, and he had told her, hands still shaking, that the air had simply fallen out from under them. [530]
His patch had come here the way everything did, pulled along the corridors to the one place where the sky pressed down. [552]
It was evidence, and it was also a map. [561]
Then the tape on the nearest rod went slack, trembled, and swung west. [574]
The tide was turning, and the breeze turned with it. [584]
Ines did not wait to understand it completely. [592]
She ran the corridors again, faster, retying tape, reading bearings on the move, redrawing the funnel as it rotated, and in twenty minutes she had found where the column would sit at dusk: directly beneath the helicopter's planned hover point. [632]
She radioed the salvage coordinator from the top of a container ladder, the fog thinning around her into brass-colored light. [652]
"Come in from the north," she said, "and hold forty meters east of the plan." [667]
"On what data?" [670]
"Mine," she said, and read him the bearings, one by one, in the flat, careful voice she had always saved for reports written too late. [695]
At dusk the helicopter came in from the north. [704]
She watched its rotor wash flatten the fog across the stacks, watched the paper cups lift and spin and settle, and saw the aircraft hang steady where the air was only air. [736]
The tank containers rose one by one into the fading light. [747]
Ines sealed the flight engineer's patch in an evidence bag and taped it inside her notebook's cover, where she would see it before she wrote anything else, and before anything else could happen. [780]
