In the canyon of Hollowmere, nothing said aloud was ever finished. [11] The walls caught each word and passed it downstream, cliff to cliff, until the dead of four centuries still murmured in the updrafts. [34] So the living kept a forged hush. [41] They wore felt over their doorways, spoke in the soft-hammered syllables the Wardens taught, and built their homes inside towers of bone, because bone drank sound better than stone. [70]

Wren Sallow painted those towers anyway. [76] She painted them at night, in chalk and rust, with faces that looked suspiciously like the Wardens asleep with their mouths open. [98] When they caught her, she only blinked and asked whether snoring counted as speech. [112] Nobody could decide if she meant it as an insult, which was exactly how she liked it. [129]

Three nights before the ritual, she stopped painting. [137]

Her mother, Orla, had kept the signal fire on the Pale Spire for thirty years. [152] The spire rose from the canyon floor like a needle of old ivory, always wrapped in mist, and its fire warned the lower towers when the cliffs began to shed. [182] One autumn night Orla fell asleep beside it, and the rockfall came unannounced, and eleven people were buried, Wren's father among them. [204]

The law of Hollowmere was clear about what came next. [214] At dawn Orla would climb the spire and speak one sentence of confession, and the spire's smooth throat would send it whole into the canyon, where it would echo forever. [244] Then the nearest kin of the dead would answer with one word: forgiven. [257] That was the price of forgiveness, and everyone knew it. [267] The forgiver gave the dead to the canyon, and would never again say their names aloud. [283] Only the confession would remain, clean and single, the one true thing Hollowmere would remember about Orla Sallow and about the eleven. [305]

Wren did not dispute that it was true. [313] She disputed that it was the only thing true. [322]

So on the first night she climbed into the hollow ribcage of the oldest tower, where sound pooled instead of fleeing, and she invited whoever would come. [349] Four came. [351] She handed each a stub of chalk and told them the rules: no preparing, no polishing, just a line about someone, spoken the moment it arrived. [377] An old ropemaker went first. [382] "Dagny laughed like a kettle and cheated at knucklebones," he whispered, and the ribs held it, and the others giggled into their sleeves. [405]

By the second night there were thirty of them, and the impromptu poetry sessions had grown bolder. [422] People spoke of the eleven: the midwife who hummed off-key, the boy who kept a lizard in his hat, Wren's father, Aldo, mending nets with his tongue between his teeth. [452] And they spoke of Orla, too: how she had carried oil up the spire's nine hundred steps every day, how she had not slept properly in a decade, how she had once talked a jumper back from the edge by describing soup. [494]

A Warden came to stop them on the third night. [504] Wren offered him chalk. [508] He stood a long time, then said, very quietly, that his sister had been the midwife. [524] He stayed. [526]

Near dawn, Wren led them all out into the mist, to the foot of the Pale Spire. [543] She had learned something from years of painting in bad light: mist bends things. [557] It had bent her lamplight into halos, and she suspected the spire's wet ivory curve would do the same to sound, if enough voices struck it at once. [585]

Orla appeared on the high platform, small and gray. [594] "I let the fire die, and eleven died with it," she said. [606] The spire took her sentence and began to send it down, whole and terrible. [620]

Wren lifted her hand, and every voice spoke its line at once. [632]

The confession struck the mist-slick spire alongside them and refracted, splitting like light through a prism, so that what rolled into the canyon was not one sentence but a spectrum: a fire left dark, a kettle laugh, a lizard in a hat, soup described to a stranger at the edge of a cliff. [685] The truth was still in it. [691] It was simply no longer alone. [697]

Then the silence came back, the forged hush, heavier than before, and every face turned to Wren. [714]

She knew the price. [718] After this word she would never speak her father's name again. [729] But the canyon had it now, in a dozen voices not her own, and it would say his name for her, forever, mending nets with his tongue between his teeth. [759]

Wren looked up at her mother through the mist, and grinned the grin the Wardens hated, and said it. [778]

"Forgiven." [779]
