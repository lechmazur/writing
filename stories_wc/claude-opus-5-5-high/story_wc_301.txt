The men's toilets at Callow Junction had not flushed since 1987, but the walls were still talking. [17] Wren Talley stood among the cracked urinals with a headlamp, a clipboard, and her brother's accordion strapped to her back like a second, wheezing spine. [42] Ivy had come in through the broken skylight and hung over the stalls in green curtains, so that reading the graffiti meant parting leaves like a priest opening a tabernacle. [72]

For eleven years she had chronicled the Sallow River for the county archive: its floods, its mills, its forty-one recorded drownings. [93] She had written every one of them down plainly, refusing the soft verbs other historians used. [109] Not "was lost." [112] Drowned. [113]

The forty-first was Danny, and she had written him down too, in the same flat ink, and then she had spent three years on sertraline not feeling the ink at all. [144] Nine weeks ago, with Dr. Osei's reluctant blessing and a tapering chart taped to her fridge, she had stopped. [163] The feeling had come back first as dizziness, then as weather, then as the plain fact she had kept folded away: the night he drowned, Danny had phoned her from the Weir Tavern for a lift, and she had said no and hung up. [207] He had walked home along the towpath instead. [215]

The station had been his place; as a teenager he practised there because the tiled halls made one accordion sound like a choir, squeezing shanties out of it until the bellows split and he mended them with silver gaffer tape rather than pay for leather. [260] Last week, clearing his flat at last, Wren had found the accordion case and, inside it, a note in his cramped capitals: CALLOW GENTS, THIRD STALL, READ THE NUMBERS. [289]

So she had come the way she approached everything, daringly methodical: a crowbar for the padlocked gate, an Ordnance Survey sheet in a plastic sleeve, a pencil tied to the clipboard with string. [322]

The third stall was crowded with decades of ballpoint wisdom. [332] EVERYTHING FLOWS, EVEN THIS. [336] YOU ARE NOT YOUR WORST NIGHT. [342] And among them, in Danny's capitals, six-figure grid references, each paired with a line that alluded rather than explained. [361]

412 087: WHERE THE HERON TAUGHT US PATIENCE. [369]
398 101: WHERE YOU PULLED ME OUT. [376]
405 093: WHERE I LEARNED THE RIVER KEEPS NO LEDGER. [386]

She logged each one, then spent three days walking them. [396] The first was the gravel bar where their father had taken them fishing and the heron had stood motionless for an hour while Danny, aged seven, fidgeted and whispered and finally, miraculously, went still. [430] The second was the mill race where, at fourteen, she had hauled him coughing onto the bank by his hood. [450] She had forgotten that; the pills had sanded it off along with everything else. [464] The third was Harrow Weir, where he would later drown, and where, she remembered now with a jolt like missing a stair, he used to throw pebbles into the white water, one for each thing he could not forgive himself. [504] The river never gave them back. [510] That was the point. [514]

Now, back in the station, she unfolded the map against the tiles and pencilled lines between the three references. [533] The triangle pointed nowhere; she had half hoped for an arrow, a verdict. [546] Then she parted the lowest ivy and found a fourth line, the ink fresher. [560]

401 096: HERE, WREN. [564] WRITE YOURS DOWN AND LEAVE IT. [570]

She checked the sheet twice, because checking was how she stayed upright: the reference was Callow Junction itself, the hundred-metre square beneath her boots. [594] He had known her. [598] He had known she carried every drowning in the county as though she had stood on the bank and watched, and he had left her somewhere to set one down. [628]

Her hand shook. [631] She wrote in the flat ink of the archive, no soft verbs: I SAID NO AND HUNG UP. [649] HE WALKED. [651] THE RIVER TOOK HIM. [655] I DID NOT. [658]

Reading it back, she did not feel absolved, only lighter by the weight of one pebble, which was maybe how it worked: not a verdict but a practice, stone by stone. [689]

Then she unslung the accordion and stepped out onto the platform, where ivy had swallowed the timetable and the rails ran rust-red toward the river. [714] She could not play. [718] She pulled the bellows open anyway, and the taped seams hissed and leaked, and the chord came out cracked and enormous under the old iron roof, the sound of something broken still breathing. [751] She squeezed again, and again, until her arms ached and her face was wet, and the station answered each time like a choir. [774]
