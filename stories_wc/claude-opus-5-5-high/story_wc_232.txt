Hester Vane had sold maps of every coast on earth and had not left the county in forty-one years. [19] Her shop smelled of rag paper and dust, and most days the only voice in it was the clock. [38] In June a padded envelope arrived from her niece Wren, whom she had met twice. [53] Inside was a note saying Maud had died in April, and a small copper block wrapped in tissue. [71] "She said you would know what it was for," Wren had written. [83] Hester did know. [86]

It was a matrix chip from their grandfather's type foundry, the mold into which molten lead was poured to cast a single character. [109] This one held no letter but a manicule, the little pointing hand printers once used to say look here. [128] When they were girls, Grandpa Abel had shown them a county survey he had printed himself, and in the hills above Tanner Creek he had stamped that same hand. [157] Beside it he had inked a caption: Doorway of Decisions. [167] He said it was the place where he chose to stay in the valley instead of going west, and that every person gets one such door. [193] Maud, fourteen and fearless, had hooked her pinky around Hester's and promised they would find it together. [210] Then Maud married a pipeline man and went to Alberta, and the promise sat in Hester like a pin left in a hem. [233]

She pulled the survey from the flat file, where she had never let herself price it. [249] Hester had always been what a customer once called empirically intuitive. [260] She could feel a forgery in her fingertips before she knew why, and then she would go and prove it with a loupe and a watermark catalogue. [287] Now her hunch said the pointing hand on the map was not decoration, and under magnification she saw she was right: its finger aimed along a faint dotted line into a cave mouth marked with a flower. [324]

It had rained for three days, and the radio said Tanner Creek would crest by evening. [340] She went anyway, because it was midsummer and she was sixty-eight and there would not be many more Junes. [359]

The cave was where the map said, its mouth curtained in trumpet vine so thick with orange blossoms that the stone seemed to be flowering. [384] Inside, pale moonflowers climbed toward cracks of daylight, and white mushrooms bloomed in the damp like spilled rice. [402] The passage forked within twenty yards, then forked again, and the dotted line on the survey gave out. [420] Water was already sliding across the floor, cold around her ankles. [431]

Then she noticed the ants. [436] A black column of them streamed along the wall, each carrying an egg or a crumb, all moving the same way. [457] The rising creek was driving them home to some dry gallery, and ants, she reasoned, would know high ground better than any surveyor. [480] She tested it anyway, setting a crumb of her sandwich at the mouth of a low tunnel; the column flowed past it and up the other way. [507] She followed them home, stooping, her flashlight wobbling, the water loud behind her. [520]

The ants vanished into a seam above a dry ledge, and there, cut into the rock at eye level, was a pointing hand. [543] It had been carved in relief, raised from the stone, and when she pressed the copper matrix over it the two fitted like a key and a lock. [571] The hand pointed at a loose stone, and behind the stone sat a rusted tobacco tin. [587] Inside were two cards. [591]

The first, in Abel's cramped hand, said: Here I chose to stay, and I have never been sorry. [609] The second was newer, dated 1981, in Maud's looping script. [619] Hettie, I came without you, and I'm ashamed of it. [629] I chose to go. [633] Grandpa's door isn't a place, it's the moment you stop waiting, so stop waiting for me. [649]

Hester sat on the ledge a long time while the creek roared in the passage below. [665] Her flashlight made the copper in her palm glimmer, and she saw what she had missed: a matrix is struck in reverse, so its little hand pointed opposite to the carving, away from the flooded way she had come and up toward a crack of daylight. [711] She climbed after the last of the ants and came out on a hillside white with elderflower, the whole valley spread below her like a map she had never bothered to leave. [743]

That evening she called Wren and asked whether Alberta was pretty in July. [756]
