Ilse had not seen a true star in eleven years, not since the mist came off the northern shoals and settled over Kerrow like a sleeping animal. [27] The village called her the earthbound star watcher, half fond and half pitying, because she still climbed the headland each night with her father's brass sextant and stared into gray. [57] Now she knelt instead in the grotto below that headland, where the sea had bored a serpentine passage through the cliff, coiling inward three times before it opened into a round chamber. [89] Mist drifted along its curves like breath in a throat. [99] At the chamber's heart lay a pool so perfectly still that the fishermen called it the pearl. [116] When storms hammered Kerrow, the whole village crowded into that chamber, because the grotto had never flooded, and because nothing else on the coast was safe. [142] But they crowded in darkness. [147] Fire was forbidden there. [151] A sour gas seeped from the rock, and twelve years ago a lantern had found it. [167] Ilse's husband, Aled, had been carrying that lantern. [175]

She laid out the sea glass on a flat stone: green from old bottles, brown from ale jugs, one rare piece of cobalt that had come ashore the spring her granddaughter Wren was born. [209] The tide had frosted every piece, softening edges but dulling faces. [220] Frosted glass kept light to itself. [226] So she polished them the way her father had taught her to polish a lens, and the way the jeweler in Porthmell polished garnets: first with wet sand on leather, then pumice, then rottenstone, then her own warm palm, turning each piece in slow circles until the fog in it cleared. [277]

As she worked, memories resurfaced the way stones surface from a shifting beach. [290] Her father's voice on the headland: A star is only light that refused to stop traveling. [306] Aled laughing as he tied a glass float to her doorframe so it would catch the morning. [323] Wren, during last winter's storm, whimpering in the black chamber and asking whether the dark had eaten the sky. [342] Ilse had promised her a light that could not burn anyone. [353]

Now she weighed each finished piece in her palm. [362] Heavy glass was thick, and thick glass bent light sharply; thin glass merely tinted it. [377] She weighed, too, what she was attempting: to pass the pale daylight from the grotto's mouth around three bends and down into the pearl, using nothing but what the sea had broken and returned. [411] Aled would have called it foolish and helped anyway. [420]

Through the autumn she wedged polished shards into cracks along the passage, adjusting them by fractions, walking the coils with a bit of white shell to see where each reflection landed. [451] The mist, which she had cursed for over a decade, turned out to be her ally; it caught each beam and made it visible, a ribbon she could follow. [480]

The storm came in the first week of winter. [489] By midafternoon forty people sat pressed along the chamber walls, children in laps, the old sailors muttering. [506] The last gray light thinned at the entrance. [514] Wren found Ilse's hand. [518]

Then the wind shifted, and a soft warm zephyr from the south tore the clouds open. [534] The low sun struck the grotto's mouth. [541] Light met the first shard of green glass and leapt, met brown and leapt again, coiling through the passage along the path Ilse had built, each piece reflectively vibrant, handing the beam onward like a lantern passed between careful hands. [581] The drifting mist glowed amber, then green, then gold, blooming around the curves like petals opening in the breeze. [600]

The last shard was the cobalt one, set above the pool. [611] It caught the beam and dropped it into the pearl, and the still water held it without a tremor. [630] Every shard along the passage echoed there too, dozens of small lights scattered across the surface in the exact shapes of the Plough, the Hunter, the Swan. [657]

Nobody spoke. [659] Forty frightened people went quiet around a pearl of stillness, their breathing slowing to match the unmoving water. [677] Outside, the waves still pounded, but they seemed very far away. [688]

"Did the dark give the sky back?" Wren whispered. [697]

Ilse looked at the water, at stars she had not seen in eleven years, lit without a single flame. [716] "No, love," she said, as the warm wind stirred her gray hair. [728] "It was only keeping them until we learned where to look." [739]
