The Magpie had been stitched together from eleven dead warships, and Ilse Varga still heard their guns in the walls at night. [22]
She had refused to fire them once, sixty years ago, and had spent a decade in a brig welded from the same steel. [45]
Now she walked its corridors with a sewing kit and a jade bead, the last pacifist on a ship that no longer needed any. [69]

Nobody aboard fought anymore. [73]
After the Accord, the founders had run the Severance, a lattice of light that peeled each passenger's shadow away and folded it into a pocket dimension beneath the engine decks, then locked it. [106]
She had refused that too, but the founders, newly gentle and utterly certain, had run it on her while she slept. [127]

The others grew light. [131]
Without their shadows, joy lifted them literally; a good joke sent children drifting to the ceiling, and weddings were held in harnesses. [153]
But the lightness had a cost no one measured until the cradles emptied. [166]
Tomas Reyes had been born forty-one years ago, the last human anyone would ever deliver, and every pregnancy since had simply failed to hold, as if the body needed something heavy to anchor a life. [201]

Ilse believed the missing thing was locked beneath the engines. [211]
The Fold had no door, only a seam, and the seam could not be seen with ordinary eyes. [229]
She had found the method by accident, sorting her dead wife's photographs. [241]

When Ilse held the photograph toward the deck plates, the shimmer bent, pointing. [293]
Tears, she reasoned, were grief made physical, and grief belonged to the shadow; these had been shed before the cut, and they still leaned toward where their owners' darkness had gone. [324]

For three years she collected photographs from the old archive, every one wept on before the cut. [341]
Laid in sequence along the corridor floor, their tears drew a luminous line across the steel, a crack in the air no wider than a thread. [367]

Tonight she knelt at the end of that line with her needle. [379]
Threaded through its eye was the bead her grandmother had called Kwan Yin's tear, a drop of green jade said to have fallen from the goddess of mercy as she listened to the cries of the world. [416]
Kwan Yin had refused paradise until every suffering being could enter with her; Ilse had always taken that as permission to be stubborn. [439]

She began to stitch. [443]
Not a closing stitch, but a buttonhole, the kind that makes an opening strong enough to use. [460]
The seam resisted like old scar tissue. [467]
Memories rose with each pass: the gunnery officer she had wanted to strangle, the guard who broke her fingers, a fury she remembered but had not felt in fifty years. [497]
Now she felt it again, and her body grew heavier with each stitch, until her knees ached against the deck. [517]
Good, she thought. [520]
A happy woman would have floated off the seam; only the grieving could stay anchored long enough to finish. [539]

On the final stitch, the jade bead passed through, and the Fold opened like a held breath let go. [558]
Cold poured out, and with it the shadows of four thousand people, pressing against the gap in the shapes of their owners. [580]
At the front stood her own. [586]
It was taller than her, younger, with the fists she had never used. [599]
It wanted what it had always wanted: to strike, to make the world pay. [613]

Ilse did not fight it, and she did not flee. [653]

There would be tempers now, and slammed hatches, and people too heavy to float at their own weddings. [731]
