The clerk who typed her death certificate spelled everything correctly except the part about her being dead. [17] She learned this in a hospital corridor, holding a paper that listed her cause of death as ingestion of rodenticide and her place of death as Container 4471, Berth Nine. [47] The name line on the certificate had been left blank, because no one had found a name on her. [66] She could not supply one either; the poison had scoured her memory as cleanly as a tide scours sand. [85] All she had was the thing they pried from her fist: a paper label, torn from a brown bottle, printed with a skull and the words HARROW CHEMISTS, DOCK STREET, NOT TO BE TAKEN. [119] On the back, in pencil, someone had written: Not for you. [130]

The hospital discharged her into a system that insisted she had already been discharged from life. [146] No bank would open an account for a corpse. [155] No landlord would rent to one. [161] So she went back to the only address she possessed, Berth Nine, where the containers rose forty feet in rust-colored canyons and the cranes moved like slow gray herons overhead. [191]

The labyrinth kept its own calendar. [197] Ships came in on the flood and left on the ebb, and the shifts of lashers and checkers turned with them, so that she learned to count days not in dates but in high waters. [232] At low tide the workers sheltered between the stacks to smoke, and they talked. [246] She listened from the dark above them, folded into the gap between two reefers still humming with cold. [264]

The poison had done something besides erase her; it had changed the way the world reached her. [281] Words arrived with surfaces. [285] A lie felt like wet cardboard. [291] A joke was pumice. [295] But when a man spoke of his daughter's first steps, or a woman described the kitchen she grew up in, the words came smooth and heavy, like rope worn soft by years of hands, and she understood that this was what memory felt like from the outside. [342] She wanted to know it from the inside. [350]

So she collected. [353] Inside Container 4471, which had been condemned for a cracked floor and left to rot at the bottom of a stack, she wrote each overheard fragment on a strip of masking tape and pressed it to the steel. [391] A grandmother's lemon tree. [395] A brother lost off Lisbon. [400] The smell of a ferry in winter. [407] She layered them tide by tide, strip over strip, until the walls grew thick and soft as bark, and she could lie in the dark and run her palms across a thousand borrowed pasts, searching for one that fit her hand. [448] None did. [450] Every story belonged to someone with a name. [458]

Then, on a spring tide, when the water climbed so high it licked the lowest container doors, a crane operator came down from her cab and sat alone on a bollard. [489] She was not talking to anyone. [495] She was talking to the water. [501]

"You were always the careless one," the operator said. [510] "I told you the rats in the old boxes were getting into the grain, and you said you'd deal with it, and I knew you'd leave the poison near your flask, so I wrote you a note on the label. [550] Not for you. [553] Like you were a child." [558] She laughed, and the laugh broke. [564] "They gave us a certificate with no name on it and a box of ash the city swore was you, and three years later I still don't know whose ash I buried." [596]

The words touched her like rope. [602] Not borrowed rope, not someone else's worn handhold, but a knot her own fingers seemed to know. [619]

She climbed down through the gaps she had memorized, tide after tide, and stood at the edge of the floodlight with the label in her open palm. [646] The operator stared at the pencil, at her own handwriting, and then at the face above it. [663]

Neither of them spoke a name. [669] The operator seemed afraid to, as if saying it aloud might send it back into some cabinet to be misfiled forever. [690] Instead she said, "The tide's turning," and held out her hand, rough with steel and cable. [706]

Later, the woman who had no name went back to Container 4471 one last time. [721] She did not tear down the thousand strangers' pasts. [730] She pressed the label into the center of the wall, over a grandmother's lemon tree and under a brother lost off Lisbon, one more layer among the others. [758] Then she ran her palm across it and felt, for the first time, what a memory was like from the inside: not smooth, but ridged and torn and warm, like a hand still holding hers. [793]
