Ines Varga had been hired to keep thieves out of the Lumen Hollow mushroom farm, but the walls were not built to keep anything out. [25] They were chitin mesh, stretched between old mine timbers, permeable so the caverns could breathe and the spores could drift from bed to bed. [49] Seal them, the growers told her, and the whole blue-green glow would suffocate in a week. [65] So for eleven nights she walked the tunnels between midnight and the hour before the echo of morning birdsong came rolling down the ventilation shafts, and she learned the farm the way she learned every building, all at once and entirely. [106] She had always been spatially gifted. [112] Close her eyes in any room and she could still feel its corners, its drafts, the distance from the door to the nearest place to hide. [138] It had made her very good at intrusion detection and very bad at leaving places behind. [154] Her mother's apartment, sold eight months ago, still sat intact inside her skull, every lamp where it had been the night the ambulance came. [178] In her pocket she carried the only thing she had kept: her mother's old laser pointer, the one she had used to trace constellations for her physics students, its rubber button worn smooth and shallow by a thumb that was gone. [219] Ines pressed it sometimes without meaning to, just to feel the small give, the click, the return. [236] Her mother used to say every system has a physics of letting go, a rule for how it releases what it holds, and Ines had never understood it. [264]

On the twelfth night, she pressed the button in Cavern Four and saw the intruder's path. [280] The red beam cut through air thick with drifting spores, and where it crossed, the spores lit like dust in a projector, swirling in a slow river that bent through the mesh wall and back out again. [317] Something warm and human-sized had stirred that current recently. [326] She did not need cameras. [331] She held the whole cavern in her head, felt where the eddy began, and followed it through three permeable barriers to the oldest bed on the farm, where the caps glowed like a banked fire. [366]

A woman knelt there, cutting one small mushroom and wrapping it in damp cloth. [380] Ines knew her. [383] Not her name, but her green scarf, her knitting bag, her habit of humming under her breath on the 5:10 tram, where for months they had sat across from each other every morning, two familiar strangers who had never exchanged a word. [425]

The woman looked up, unsurprised. [430] "My husband planted this bed forty years ago," she said. [440] "He's in the hospice on Varrow Street now. [448] He doesn't know my face anymore, but he knows this light. [459] I bring him one every morning so he has something to hold." [471]

Ines thought of the report she was supposed to file, the steel doors the owner wanted, the caverns sealed tight and slowly going dark. [495] She thought of her mother's lamps, each one still burning in a room that belonged to someone else. [513] Something caught in her chest, quiet and fast as fire running under dry grass. [527]

"Take two," Ines said at last. [533] "One for each hand." [537]

They walked out together through the breathing walls, the spores parting and closing behind them, and climbed the shaft just as the first birds began somewhere above. [564] On the 5:10 tram, Ines sat beside her instead of across. [575] Her name was Petra Halloran. [580]

The report Ines filed that afternoon would revolutionize how the farm guarded itself. [593] No steel, no seals. [597] Low lasers strung across every tunnel, reading the spore currents that flowed through the mesh, so the walls could stay open and still know who passed. [623] Detection, she wrote, not denial. [628] At the bottom she added one standing authorization: P. Halloran, entry before dawn, the old bed, one cap per night. [648]

Three weeks later, Petra's husband died with a glowing cap cupped in his palms. [662] On the tram the next morning, Ines pressed the laser pointer into Petra's hand. [676] "My mother used it to show people where the light was," she said. [689] "You should have something to hold." [695] Petra pressed the worn button, and a thin red line crossed the fogged window toward the paling sky. [713] Ines felt her mother's apartment shift inside her, one lamp going out, then another, not lost, only passing through. [732] Letting go, she understood now, was not a wall coming down but a membrane learning to breathe. [749]
