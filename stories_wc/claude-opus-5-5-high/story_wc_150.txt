The orchard at Sveti Brana grew over the dead, and nobody in the village pretended otherwise. [16] Quince roots slid through the catacomb ceilings like slow gray rivers, cradling skulls, threading ribs, lifting shinbones into the lantern light as if offering them for inspection. [43] Dara Vukic had flown nine thousand kilometers to stand among them, and she found, to her own surprise, that she wanted to say hello. [67]

Her grandfather had left this valley at nineteen, meaning to return in a year. [81] He died in Toronto at eighty-six, still meaning it. [90] At his funeral Dara had stopped believing that anyone ever came back from anywhere, and the loss of that belief sat in her like a swallowed stone. [117] So when the university announced it would excavate the Pilgrims' Tomb beneath his childhood orchard, she volunteered to carry buckets. [137]

The story, as the villagers told it, was simple. [146] Six hundred years ago a column of pilgrims bound for the coast had been caught here by fever. [164] The monks buried them facing the sea and planted trees above them, promising that the journey was only paused. [183] An old woman named Zora, who kept the orchard key, added the part the brochures left out. [200]

"There was a sundial on the tomb," she said. [209] "It told the dead when to get up and keep walking. [220] Soldiers broke it in the war, and after that people stopped bringing flowers." [233] She shrugged, but her hands tightened on her cane. [242] "A clock with no hand is only a stone." [251]

The excavation took the whole of a dry autumn. [260] While the archaeologists mapped the central chamber with measured grids, Dara developed a habit they tolerated with amused suspicion: she wandered. [281] It was not aimless. [285] Each morning she chose a single root at the tunnel mouth and followed it wherever it went, crouching where it dove, reading every niche it passed, asking the bones questions she did not expect them to answer. [322] Where were you going? [326] What did you pack? [330] Who waited for you? [334]

The roots, she learned, knew things the grids did not. [344] They had grown toward water, toward hollow places, toward the scattered pieces of whatever had once stood above. [362]

One quince root led her through a crawlway and into a side chamber where it had split a slab of limestone neatly in two. [386] When she brushed away the dirt, she found carved hour lines radiating from a square socket, and between the lines, names. [407] Hundreds of names, cut small, each beside the hour its owner had died. [420] It was the sundial base, dragged down here by someone who could not bear to leave it in the rubble. [440]

Dara sat back on her heels, lantern trembling, delighted and afraid in equal parts, the way one feels before opening a letter. [462] Near the socket, fresher than the rest, someone had scratched a final name: Petar Vukic, with no hour at all. [482]

Her grandfather. [484] Nineteen and leaving, he had carved himself among the travelers who were only paused. [498]

She thought then of the bronze wedge that had propped open his kitchen door in Toronto for sixty years, green with age, too heavy and too strange for a doorstop, which she had packed in her suitcase because she could not bear to leave it behind. [544] The wedge had a square tongue at its base. [553]

It took the crew three days and a winch to raise the base into the orchard, and Dara spent them rebuilding what the soldiers had broken: mortaring the split slab, resetting it on the tomb's crown, turning it by compass until the carved noon line pointed true. [600] The archaeologists argued about provenance. [605] Zora said nothing and came every day to watch. [614]

On the last morning of the dig, Dara slid the bronze gnomon into its socket. [629] It fit with a soft, final click, like a key. [639] The sun cleared the ridge, and a thin shadow fell across the stone, moving, very slowly, toward the sea. [658]

Below them, Dara imagined, the roots shifted a little in the dark. [670] She did not quite believe the dead would rise and walk. [681] But she believed, now, that some journeys bent rather than ended, that a man could send a piece of himself home by way of a granddaughter's luggage. [708]

Zora knelt, laid a ripe quince beside Petar's name, and then, carefully, as if the stone might refuse her, another beside the others. [731]

"Flowers are for mourning," she said. [737] "Fruit is for travelers." [741]

Dara, curious as ever, asked where she had heard that. [751] The old woman smiled for the first time all autumn. [761] "I just decided," she said, and touched the shadow as it passed. [773]
