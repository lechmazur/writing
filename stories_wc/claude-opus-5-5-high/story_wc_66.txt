Every night after the orchestra's rehearsal, Ines Marr carried her copying satchel up the goat path to the ember alcove. [20]
It was a hollow in the hillside where charcoal burners had once banked their fires, and the stone still held a faint orange heat that the mist could not put out. [51]
Thistles grew thick around its mouth, purple heads bowed with damp, and she had learned to part them with her elbow so they scratched her sleeve instead of her wrist. [81]

For eleven years she had been the Municipal Orchestra's copyist, and she was diligent in the way of people who have nothing else left to keep. [107]
She ruled her staves by hand. [113]
She blotted every flag and beam. [119]
During the story famine, when the Bureau collected novels and ballads and bedtime tales and pulped them in the valley mills, music without words had been the only thing permitted to live, and so she had kept living inside it. [159]

The famine was over now. [164]
The Bureau had said so on posters. [171]
Stories were legal again, licensed and approved, but the pulp dust still drifted over the town on dry days, grey as moth wings, and the people who breathed it had forgotten how to begin anything with once upon a time. [211]
Children asked what happened next and adults stared at them, embarrassed, as if a tooth had gone missing. [229]
The mill workers called themselves dust, half joking. [237]
Ines did not find it funny. [243]

Inside the alcove she set down the shortwave transmitter she had built from a dead man's wireless and a church organ's wire. [265]
When she switched it on, the dial glowed a soft sea green, and in that glow the margins of her viola parts came alive. [289]
She had copied them that afternoon with ordinary black ink, and between the staves, with a second pen, she had written in a mixture of her own devising, crushed phosphor from old clock hands stirred into gum. [326]
By daylight the extra lines were invisible. [333]
By the dial glow they were secretly luminous, a pale green handwriting floating over the notes like breath on cold glass. [354]

They were lyrics, but never whole ones. [361]
She did not broadcast stories; the Bureau's listening vans could find an unlicensed story in a minute. [378]
She broadcast songs with holes in them. [385]
A fishwife lost a key in the river, and the key was. [397]
A boy walked north until the road grew teeth, and then he. [409]
Each verse ended one breath before its meaning, and the melody circled back, waiting. [423]
Her theory, which she had told no one, was that you could not hand a story to dust. [441]
Dust had to be tempted into finishing one itself. [450]
That was the whole of her ambition: to teach dust to tell stories. [463]

She tuned to the gap between the licensed frequencies and began to sing low, the pirate broadcast slipping out through the mist the way smoke leaves a chimney. [491]

Halfway through the second verse, headlights swept the slope below. [501]
A Bureau van, its roof antenna turning like the head of an owl. [514]
Ines did not stop singing. [519]
She unpinned her wool shawl and cloaked the transmitter with it so the dial glow would not show through the thistles, and the luminous lyrics vanished from the page. [548]
She had to sing the rest from memory, in the dark, her knuckles against the warm stone. [565]
The van idled, then moved on, because a song with no ending sounds like nothing at all to people who are only listening for endings. [590]

When the road was empty she lifted a corner of the shawl. [602]
Green light returned, and with it her floating words. [611]
She reached to switch off, then stopped, because the receiving needle had quivered. [624]

Someone was transmitting on her frequency. [630]
It was a crackling, childish voice, probably on a set rigged from a mill radio, and it was singing her tune. [651]
It sang the fishwife, and the river, and then it sang past the hole, a line Ines had never written: and the key was a fish, and the fish opened the door under the water, and the door led home. [691]
It was a clumsy line. [696]
It did not scan. [700]
The child sang it twice, proud, and then the signal went quiet. [712]

Ines sat very still while the thistles ticked with mist against the alcove mouth. [726]
Then she dipped her second pen, and in the margin under the fishwife she wrote the child's line in luminous ink, so that it would glow tomorrow for whoever came next, and she began, very softly, a new verse with a larger hole. [769]
