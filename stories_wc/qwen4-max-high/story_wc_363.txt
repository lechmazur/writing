The silence did not fall; it raveled.  First the thrushes went, then the wind in the cane, then the clack of bamboo against bamboo, each sound drawn loose like a thread pulled from a hem.  Sada heard it going.  She was a loom spinner, wistful the way the last of any craft is wistful, and she kept her frame in the forgotten orchard deep in the bamboo forest because the trees there still remembered apples.  Nine feral rows, grass to the waist, cane crowding the gaps like green scaffolding.  Once the cane had clacked all night like knitting needles; now she worked in a quiet with a grain to it, a quiet coming apart. 

Her daughter had gone over the ridge in the last year that had a name, to the towns where the world still held its weave.  No road crossed that ridge now, and no sound, and no hour; the raveling had unpicked the distance itself, so that a mile was as unreachable as a century.  Sada had one thing to send: the orchard still bore, and she was still under it.  Forty years she had spun for other people's houses; this was the first bolt she had ever woven for one person.  Words would not cross; words were sound, and sound was what the silence ate.  Cloth might. 

The trouble was the pattern.  To weave a place back into the world you must begin where the world has an edge, and Sada had no border left, only middles, only the loose interior of days.  She found one in a rummage sale box bought for a coin at the last fair before the quiet: jigsaw puzzle edges, forty pieces with a straight side each, the rest of the picture lost.  Apple blossom, a strip of blue, a fragment of a ladder.  She laid them along the ninth row and matched them to the trees, and the border came clear: this much sky, this much grass, this exact leaning of the third trunk.  Edges first, then the inside could be filled.  She warped the loom to those edges and began. 

Her shuttle needed weight, so she used the bead she had prised from the oldest tree's wound, tree sap gone to amber, a bee and a petal sealed inside.  It was stoically sparkling.  Everything else in the orchard was surrendering its color to the hush, but the bead held its small gold light and would not let go, and that stubbornness was the whole message: something here refuses to be undone.  She tied it into the selvage. 

The raveling took her work each night.  She would wake to a bare frame and a heap of unspun fiber, the silence having picked out every pass she had made, and she would recommence.  Through the hush of time's unraveling tapestry she recommenced, and recommenced, seasons coming out of order, blossom and fruit and bare branch in a single afternoon.  She learned what the loom was taking for thread.  Not wool.  Her remembering.  Each pass cost her a sound she owned: the creak of the ladder, the bee's hum, her daughter's laugh at six years old, at nine, at the gate.  She paid them into the cloth and the cloth grew bright.  By the time the border closed she could not recall the laugh, only that there had been one, and that it mattered.  When the fiber ran out she kept her hands moving, and the thread came anyway.  She did not name what she was giving, and neither did the trees. 

The bolt crossed the ridge the way only a finished thing can, by being needed on the other side.  In a town where the world still held, a woman opened her door to a length of cloth: nine rows, blossom, a leaning trunk, and a bead of amber with a bee inside, still stoically sparkling.  She put the cloth to her ear and heard an orchard, and a girl laughing in it, and knew the girl.  Deep in the bamboo the loom stood empty in the grass, its warp gone slack, the ninth row waiting.  An apple fell, and nothing anywhere said so.
