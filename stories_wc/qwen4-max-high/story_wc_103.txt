Ilsa Vane designed hedge mazes for people who wanted to be lost politely.  She read gardens with her palms, the way other women read letters.  What she wanted was not lostness but the texture of time, whether it ran grained like oak or napped like velvet, rough one way and smooth the other.  A maze, she had come to believe, is a machine for slowing time, and she had built forty of them, feeling for a grain she could never quite touch. 

Her sister Ada had been retrocognitively trapped since the quake.  Ada sat in the east room and lived one afternoon over and over, the light at four o'clock, the tea going cold, the floor shivering under her.  She could not see Ilsa; she could not see the year. 

The photographs proved it.  Every photograph of Ada was fading, not yellowing as old paper does but thinning, her hands going first, then her face, until each frame held nothing but a curved sliver of gold in her open palm.  The halo fragment.  A quake-shaken crescent from the chapel saint's gilded crown, it had been in Ada's hand when the afternoon closed around her, and it was the only thing in her that had not thinned.  Ilsa understood it as an anchor: the loop held because Ada kept looking at that one bright, unchanging point.  So she would build a place with no returning. 

Between the house and the ruined chapel ran a gorge, and across it Ilsa grew a living bridge garden, pleaching hornbeam and willow over a spine of cable until the span breathed, leafed, and dripped.  She crocheted the plan first, adding stitches faster than each row could hold, so the little wool model ruffled and would not lie flat.  That was the geometry she needed: hyperbolic, negatively curved, every junction offering five turnings where a flat garden allows three.  In such a maze the paths multiply faster than memory can map them, and no corridor ever comes round again.  A mind stuck in a circle cannot close its circle here. 

She worked in a quaking reverie, her fingers unsteady on the ties, the gorge breathing below her like something asleep.  While it grew, the photographs kept fading; the last of Ada's face went out of the wedding picture in a single night, and Ilsa found herself unable to remember whether her sister's chin had a cleft.  She was trading the record for the woman, and she signed the trade. 

On the morning the bridge closed its canopy, Ilsa carried Ada out onto it.  Her sister weighed almost nothing, like a bundle of coats.  Ada's eyes stayed on the gold in her palm, and her lips moved with the same four words she had been saying for years.  Ilsa set her down at the first junction and pried the halo fragment out of her hand.  She threw it into the gorge. 

Ada made a thin sound and went after it, into the green, and at every junction the hedge offered her five ways, and then five more, and then more than she could count.  The ruffled corridors curved away from themselves; her searching could not close.  Ilsa followed at a distance, watching the afternoon disintegrate.  It went the way frost goes off a window: the four o'clock light lost its slant, the cold tea lost its cup, the shivering floor forgot how to be a floor, and Ada's held moment came apart into ordinary air. 

At the far end, where the bridge rooted into the chapel's broken wall, Ada stopped and looked around her, and saw Ilsa, and said her name.  She was older, and hungry, and frightened, and here. 

Ilsa took her sister's wrist, and there it was, the thing she had built all this to learn: time had a nap, like velvet.  Stroke it forward and it was smooth and merciless; stroke it back and it bristled, and would not let you. 

Behind them the maze rustled, still multiplying; far below, the fragment lay in the stream, bright and useless, holding its one instant alone.  At home the photographs would be blank by evening, every one, gold and all.  She would take new ones, and they would fade the ordinary way, from the edges, over years.  Ilsa held on, and let herself be carried the only way the nap allows.
