The rain stopped, and the old viaduct began to glow.  Water ran off the balustrade in threads, and every stone lit from inside, a pale green fire the townspeople called the afterlight.  Vesna climbed the rail and stood on nothing above the flooded street.  She was a defier; gravity had been asking her to come down for eleven years, and she had kept saying no.  A defier cannot sleep in a bed, cannot kiss anyone without drifting, cannot be held.  In her hands she held the clay tablet the dredgers had pulled from the riverbed, older than the viaduct, older than the river's name.  Its runes never sat still.  Read them twice and they rearranged, seeding a new corridor out of the old letters, so that no two readings ever built the same maze.  She let it fall.  Between the drop and the ripple there is a room, and the room is periodically eternal: it opens only when the afterlight burns, once in seven years, and inside it time has no floor.  Vesna stepped off the rail into that room.  Below her the puddle hung unbroken, a held breath of water, and the tablet hung an inch above it, turning.  The reflection in the puddle was not the viaduct.  It was a corridor of wet brick, and then another behind it, and another, branching, the runes writing halls faster than she could walk them.  She had come to conquer the labyrinth of herself, and every visit had taught her only that the maze won by being new.  She went down into her own reflection to retrace the night she had stopped falling.  Eleven years ago her brother had gone off this same rail in the rain, and the water had taken him, and Vesna had refused the ground so hard that the ground had let go of her.  The labyrinth knew that night.  It built it for her again, and again, and each pass the light grew brighter and told her less.  At the first junction stood a Vesna who had landed, soaked and ordinary, holding a dead boy's coat.  At the second stood a Vesna who had never climbed at all.  At the third, one still hanging above the street with her arms out, eleven years older, waiting for someone to tell her it was safe.  Fractured timelines, the tablet's runes called them, in a grammar of cracks: every choice she had not made kept walking around without her.  She retraced until the corridors stopped pretending to be places.  The brick became her habits; the dead ends became the sentences she said to herself before sleep; the generated halls came out of her, seeded from her, new every time and always the same shape.  That was the trouble with a maze that builds itself out of you: you cannot solve it by walking, because every step is another wall.  The afterlight was blinding now and black, a brightness that darkened everything it touched, and by it she finally saw the center.  No monster, no brother, no exit.  Only the tablet, waiting an inch above the water, and her own two feet, which had not touched anything in eleven years.  She understood then what conquering the labyrinth required.  Not a map.  Not another pass, another seven years, another forever spent inside a single second.  She had to stop generating.  She had to let the thing she had been outrunning catch her.  Vesna said yes.  Gravity, patient as stone, took her at once.  The corridors folded like wet paper; the other Vesnas turned their faces up; the runes went out one by one, and the last thing the black light showed her was her brother's coat, sinking, and her own hands empty and finally open.  The tablet struck the puddle.  The ripple came.  It ran outward through the reflected viaduct and broke it into ordinary pieces, and Vesna landed in the street with the water closing over her ankles, cold, and loud, and real.  The glow died out of the stones.  Above her the viaduct was only a bridge, dark and dripping, and the street was only a street, and she was a woman standing in it, holding her weight herself.  She bent and picked the tablet out of the water.  The runes were still, waiting to be read, ready to build her any world she asked for.  In seven years the room would open again, eternal and brief, and she could go back inside.  Vesna put the tablet under her coat and walked home along the ground, one foot after another, letting each step land.
