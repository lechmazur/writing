The Orpheum closed for good at eleven, and June stayed behind to sweep.  Monday the buyers would take the keys, and she had already decided to let her voice go with the building.  She had not sung since her mother died in the spring.  Sound came to June in colors, the way it had come to her grandmother: the broom a dry gray scratch, the exit sign a low green hum.  That was how she saw the ghost, because he was made of nothing else.  An echo rose from the empty stalls, silver and thread-thin, matching no sound she had made.  It was a tenor holding a note he had never finished.  The old stagehands called him the Encore; he died in 1958 with one more song in him, and the house had kept him ever since.  He could not speak, only give back what the room had already said.  He took her broom-scratch and returned it as applause.  Then he gave her the ring of a coin, and June knew that gold sound.  It was in her coat pocket, where it had been for eleven years: a brass token her grandmother had hand-painted with the Wheel of Fortune, for a tenor who could never decide whether to sing one more.  Her grandmother had found it in a floorboard trap and kept it in her paint box, and never said why.  He had lost it the night he died, and with nothing left to choose with, his time had splintered into every night he might have sung.  Now that the house had shut for good, the stage door stood open on hedges.  June stepped through into a maze that had no business existing in an alley, and every turn held a different year.  Left: 1958, gaslight and wet wool.  Right: Monday, diesel and the yellow teeth of machines.  Straight on: her mother, alive, humming while she pinned June's hair.  The smell of her mother's rosin and cold cream rose up, and June nearly turned into it.  But the coin rang gold in every year, the same gold, and she followed the one sound that time could not bend.  The hedges opened on an orchard.  Nine pear trees in a ring, and on every bough hung a song nobody had sung: encores swallowed by closing time, fruit that had never been picked.  He stood under the middle tree, gray as a photograph, one hand out for the coin.  He had not been haunting her; he had been fishing for her with the only bait he had, her own remembered sound.  He wanted his encore, and the price of it was that she would have to open her mouth again.  She put the token in his palm.  The painted wheel turned in his hand, and the years began to intertwine: 1958, her mother's spring, Monday's machines, braiding into a single evening.  Then he gave her back her mother's hum, and this time he left the last bar empty.  An invitation, not a memory.  June sang it.  Her voice came out rust-colored and cracked, then cleared, and the ghost's silver line wound through it until neither of them could have said which was which.  The pears dropped, one after another, and burst into applause.  He bowed all the way down, and pressed the token back into her fingers, its painted wheel worn to bare brass.  He had stopped needing to choose.  The orchard folded shut, and June stood in an ordinary alley that smelled of pears.  Dawn came up gray over the loading dock.  She would hand over the keys on Monday; a song does not stop a bulldozer.  But she walked home humming the finished phrase, and it was hers now, an encore with nowhere left to be spent except forward.  In her kitchen she took out her grandmother's paints and, on the blank reverse of the token, she painted the Fool: a young man at the edge of a cliff, one foot already lifted, a small white dog barking at his heel.  Then she called the bakery on Ninth about the room upstairs they rented for rehearsals.  She did not flip the coin.  She had learned what it was for: not to decide, but to remind you that somebody once stood in an orchard full of unpicked songs and chose to sing one.
