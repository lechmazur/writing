The old rocking chair stood at the mouth of the Vanishing Point Gallery, and Ada pushed it once with her knee before she opened the tin.  Everything she did next had to fit inside the time it took those rockers to settle.  Ada scattered ash for a living; the chair was how she measured a goodbye.  She drew the dead in charcoal, burned the drawings, and carried what was left to the place they had felt safest.  The ash in her tin was Teodor Vrancic, who had felt safest here, in a corridor he built to lie.  The walls leaned inward, the ceiling dropped, the floor tiles shrank, and every painting he hung shared one vanishing point: a small grey door at the far end, painted on plaster.  Walk toward it and it never came closer.  He made it for his wife, so that when her mind began losing its landmarks she would always have one horizon that stayed put.  She rocked in that chair while he painted, facing the door that never arrived.  Safety you could stand inside, he called it.  His son Luka waited at the threshold with the mask in a shoebox.  Porcelain shards, forty of them, wired edge to edge: the cup his mother dropped the morning she stopped knowing the kitchen.  Teodor had wired them into a face.  "He was making a self-portrait," Luka said.  "The last thing he finished."  Ada had read the notebooks; she knew whose face it was.  She knew what the last page said: the corridor does not work, she is afraid of the door, I am afraid of it too.  Luka needed a father who had died certain.  So Ada gave him true things in the order that would let him keep that.  "He worked on it every night," she said, which was true.  "He never called it finished," she said, which was also true, and she let Luka hear triumph in it instead of doubt.  She had come for her own reason, and it was not the fee.  Years ago her mother's fever had broken at four in the morning, and for eleven seconds Ada had believed the world was safe.  Charcoal could hold a face forever; nothing could hold those eleven seconds.  She had tried anyway, portrait after portrait, burning each one, until she understood that ash was the only honest shape for a feeling.  But here, in a hall built to hold one instant of reassurance forever, she thought she might catch it.  The chair creaked, slower now, its arc shortening.  She set the mask on the floorboards facing down the corridor and knelt.  With her thumb she powdered the seams, working charcoal ash into every wire and hairline gap until the forty shards stopped being forty.  In the raking light of the far lamp the cracks went dark and the face came whole.  Luka made a sound she had heard at other bedsides.  "Mama," he said, and for two seconds he was a boy in a kitchen where nothing had broken yet.  She had not told him; the ash and the lamp had.  Ada felt her eleven seconds arrive inside someone else's chest, and that was as close to crystallizing them as she would ever come.  She tipped the tin into the air the rockers were still pushing.  The ash went up and out, a grey breath traveling the length of the lie, narrowing as the walls narrowed, aiming for the little painted door.  It looked, for a moment, like it would get there.  Then it settled on the shrinking tiles, halfway, the way everything settles.  Luka lifted the mask to hang it at the vanishing point, where his father had left a hook.  The powder held in the seams; the two seconds did not.  He told her he would keep the gallery open forever, and she saw the auction tag still wired to the chair's spindle.  Everyone here was surviving on part of the truth, and she thought it was probably the kindest arrangement available.  "It's a good room," she said, and meant it, and did not mention the tag.  At the far end the painted door hung in its lamp, no closer, no farther, promising a room on the other side that nobody had built.  The old chair gave one last knock against the floorboard and stopped.  Ada looked down the hall and felt safe, knowing better, which is the only way anyone ever does.
