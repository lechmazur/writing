The last petal came off the old rosebush on a Thursday, and Mira let it lie on the fire escape boards like a receipt.  Everything after that was subtraction: pots down to the landing, beans pulled, the harbor lock shut for the winter.  She was the building's fire escape gardener, which meant she was also its plumber, its lookout, and its keeper of small light.  Below her, the harbor lay luminous under the moon, every moored hull laid over in white tin, and Mira angled her mirrors to catch it.  Saucers, tray lids, a hubcap, the flat of a soup spoon: she had spent two summers learning to throw moonlight off the water and up into the dark corners of the alley, where her beans sulked and where the boy slept.  His name was Teo, he was eleven, and he had dropped his street key off the pier three weeks ago; the landlord charged nine crowns for another, and his mother had eight.  So he slept behind the coal bins and slipped in behind whoever came home late.  Mira had not hauled him out into the street and made him explain himself; a corner that hides you can also keep you, and she meant to keep his.  She only moved the hubcap two inches, so the light fell across his blanket instead of his face.  Her father had been the harbor lockmaster, and before that a locksmith, and he used to say a lock is only a conversation between teeth.  He left her his files, his blanks, and his bitting gauge, a brass comb whose teeth stood for the nine depths a key could be cut.  The ninth tooth, the deepest, had snapped off years ago; she carried it in her coat pocket and rubbed it smooth with her thumb while she decided things.  Someday she wanted an acre of ground; tonight she had four crates and a landing rated for less than she pretended.  Wistfully pragmatic, she priced the acre every spring and bought seed for the crates every autumn.  Now the rose was finished, the lock was shut, and the boy was still outside, so she took the ninth tooth out and set it back in its gap in the comb.  The street door was her father's last job: an old rim lock with one stubborn pin, cut nine deep, so deep the gauge barely reached.  She called down the fire escape, not loudly, and told Teo that if he could hold a file he could own a door.  He came up the iron ladder into the quivering hush that falls on the harbor at slack water, when the tide has stopped and not yet turned and every reflection holds still, waiting.  She clamped the blank in the vise bolted to the rail, gave him the flat file, and set the gauge beside it with its broken tooth held in place by her thumbnail.  Then she counted for him, the way her father had counted for apprentices, the way the men below were rowing their skiffs home: two pulls and a rest, two pulls and a rest.  He filed with the rhythm of oars in water, and the shavings curled off bright as fish scales, and his shoulders came down from around his ears.  You cannot instill courage by shouting down at a boy in an alley; you give him a count to breathe to and a cut that deepens under his own hands.  They checked the depth against the ninth tooth, and it matched, and Mira let out the breath she had been holding since the petal fell.  Down at the street door the moonlight came in sideways off the water, off the hubcap, off the spoon, and lay along the keyhole like a chalk line.  Teo turned his key, and the deep cut lifted the stubborn pin, and the lock said yes in her father's voice.  He looked back at the alley, at the corner that had kept him, and Mira did not tell him to be grateful or to be brave.  She put the ninth tooth in his palm, closed his fingers over it, and said the rose would bloom again in June and he would be the one to water it.  The gauge would read eight depths now, and eight was enough for this street.  Then she moved the hubcap again, so the light stayed in the empty corner all night, in case anything else needed somewhere to hide.
