The loom had written nine thousand symphonies since breakfast, and nobody could hum one.  That was the age: creativity automated, brilliance everywhere, and a strange dimness underneath it, like a room lit by lamps that cast no warmth.  Mira ranked last among the mages of her guild, and honestly earned it.  She could not conjure fire, only the ghost of fire.  Her whole talent was lingering luminance: whatever had shone kept shining faintly, for a while, after the shining stopped.  The examiners called it a residue, not an art.  The loom made light that never paused, so nothing in the city lingered at all.  Her grandmother had lingered.  Four days after the burial, Mira could still see the lamplight on the old woman's hands, and then one morning she could not, and that was the second death.  That winter the guild summoned its last apprentices: bring a masterwork to the loom, and be measured.  She did not want to make something great; the loom had greatness covered.  She wanted one simple thing that would stay lit after the hand let go.  So she took the keystone from the garden folly.  The folly was a fake ruin her grandmother had paid a mason to build, an arch going nowhere, cracked on purpose, ivy invited.  Its keystone held up nothing but the pretense, and held it perfectly.  Mira strapped it to her back with harness leather and went to climb the spire.  The spire stood above the clouds, icebound, blue as a bruise, and the loom lived at its top.  Its stair had been quarried away for the loom's foundations, so Mira went up by mist walking.  Mist walking is a cheap, disreputable method: you condense the cloud under your boot and step, and the cloud holds.  It accommodates impossibilities, which is why the guild forbade it and why grandmothers loved it.  You must not ask the mist how it bears you.  You must simply be borne.  She climbed all day through white nothing, the stone grinding her spine, her breath freezing into small ghosts that followed her.  At dusk she hauled herself onto the summit gallery, where the loom blazed.  It was beautiful and it hurt to look at, and it gave off no afterglow whatsoever, because it never once stopped.  An old man oiled its gears.  He had been a composer once; he had fed the loom his early work to teach it, and now he could not remember any of it.  'You're the residue girl,' he said.  'I'm the last apprentice,' Mira said, and unstrapped the keystone.  The leather fell away; her shoulders rose like a released bird; she set the stone on the ice.  'That holds up nothing,' the old man said.  'It holds up a garden that was never real,' she said.  'Same trick as the mist.'  She cupped her hands and struck the small spark she had been taught before the loom, the useless one, the one every child learns.  It lit the keystone's cracked face: the mason's chisel marks, a thumbprint in old mortar, the ivy's scar.  Then she closed her hands and put it out.  The loom roared on, brilliant, tireless, and beside it the stone kept glowing.  Dim as a coal under ash.  It glowed because it had been lit, and because hands had made it, and because a woman had loved a fake ruin more than a real palace.  The old man knelt on the ice with his oilcan and stared.  'It isn't doing anything,' he whispered.  'No,' Mira said.  'It's keeping something.'  He put his fingers near the light, not touching, the way you warm hands at a hearth in a house where someone has died.  Something moved in his face.  He hummed four notes, badly, and stopped, astonished, because they were his.  The loom could not have written those four notes; it could have written nine thousand better ones.  No instrument on the loom could measure how long a thing was loved.  Mira left the keystone there, above the clouds, glowing faintly at the machine's foot, a small holy thing that asked for nothing.  They would rank her last again, and she found she did not mind.  She stepped off the gallery onto mist, and did not ask how it held her.  Behind her the old man was still humming, and the stone was still glowing, and all that brilliance seemed, for the first time, dim.
