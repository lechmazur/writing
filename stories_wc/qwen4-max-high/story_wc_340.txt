The elevator in the spire had ten floors to climb, and Wren intended to use all of them.  Below her, the kitchens; above, the lantern room, where no servant had ever been asked to go.  Now one was asked, because the old captain was dead and the lamps still needed trimming.  The cage rose at the speed of a slow hymn, which was the only reason she had time.  In her apron pocket she carried his diary, which she had not been given, and his goggles, which she had.  The household had called him sour for eleven winters, and had said it kindly, the way one speaks of weather.  Wren, who was warmly inquisitive rather than merely curious, had wanted to know what a man did with eleven winters. 

Floor two: the ice began.  Frost furred the cage's brass rails, and the shaft windows went from grey to white to that particular blue that only exists above the clouds, where the light has nothing left to bounce off but snow.  She opened the diary on her knee.  Every page was the same ledger of losses: cargo, crew, the names of the seventeen who went down with the Perseverance, the date, the wind.  Eleven years of the same eleven lines, copied and recopied, in a hand that never improved.  The steward had read a page aloud at the wake, to prove the old man had suffered properly.  Grief, Wren thought, was a very tidy jailer. 

Floor four.  She unfolded the note tied to the goggles' strap, which said, in his cramped hand: for the girl who asks questions, wear them upstairs.  Nobody downstairs had thought it strange that a dead man should leave his flying goggles to a scullery maid; they had thought it senile, which is cheaper.  Wren had thought it a message, and had spent three nights failing to read it. 

Floor six, and the spire's windows were ice an inch thick, milky, full of drowned bubbles.  The low sun came through them scattered, not as a beam but as a soft crowd of light arriving from everywhere at once.  She put on the goggles because her eyes were watering, and the world went amber and strange.  The left lens was crazed with a thousand fine scratches, deliberate as engraving; no captain would have flown with it. 

Floor seven.  Through the scratched glass the scattered light broke again, and the page in her lap grew a second handwriting.  It lay between the ruled lines, pale as frost on a window, written in something that only showed when the light came at it sideways and broken. 

Floor eight; she read fast, the cage shuddering upward.  Under the list of the seventeen dead he had written that the cook had taught the new girl to fold pastry, and that the girl had laughed at him for folding his wrong.  Under the wind and the date he had written that the cloud sea at dawn went the colour of apricots, and that he had stood in it until his feet went numb, and would do it again.  Under the cargo he had written that he had been happy here for eleven years, and could not say it downstairs, where they needed him broken. 

Floor nine.  The eleven repeated lines were not penance but a key, ruling the page so a reader would know where to lay the light.  Wren understood that she was not climbing to a job but to an inheritance, and that the ledger of losses had been a wall with a door in it.  He had hidden his joy beneath his anguish on purpose, so that only someone warm enough to keep asking, and patient enough to look sideways, would ever find it. 

Floor ten; the doors sighed open on the lantern room, all glass and cold, the great lamp waiting to be trimmed.  Below, the cloud sea went to the edge of the world, and it was, exactly as he had said, the colour of apricots.  Wren did not weep; she had a shift to work, and the dead, she felt, would prefer her employed.  She trimmed the lamp, then took the pen from her apron and opened the diary to the first clean page.  In plain ink, for the household, she wrote the wind and the date and the weight of the oil.  Between the lines she would put the other record, in a pale varnish she meant to spend the winter learning to mix.  The goggles she would keep until she found the next one who asked.  Downstairs, in time, they would call her sour, and they would say it kindly, the way one speaks of weather.
