After the cloud bursts, the pond spread wider than the village could see, and the lotus temple stood in water to its throat.  The causeway that had joined shore to sanctuary lay under the lilies in three broken pieces.  Nima had written love letters for eleven years, none of them signed with her name.  Fishermen dictated to her; widowers; a girl who could not spell the word for patience.  She kept the drafts in a tin box, because a ghostwriter is only a keeper of secrets who has learned grammar.  Four hundred letters, and no one alive knew what was in them but her.  She had begun the work hoping to find the thread that connects all things, the single sentence under every confession.  Instead she found four hundred variations on the same tremble: I am here, are you there.  On the fourth morning of the calm she found a thrush's nest on the path, dropped by the wind, its inner coil intact: a spiral of twigs wound tight as a fingerprint.  She held it up to the light and the water threw small colors across it, green and rose and the pale gold of the temple's upper windows.  The bird had not used a straight line anywhere.  That was the thread, then: not a line but a turning, everything coming back around to touch its neighbor.  The temple wanted its causeway rebuilt in stone, and the monks asked her, since she had the best hand in the village, to write the appeal.  She wrote it, and then she wrote something else.  Let the causeway be a nest, she proposed: a spiral of willow and lily stem, wide enough for two people abreast, curling out from the shore and around twice before it reached the temple steps.  The head monk said it was not solemn.  She agreed, and said that anything she made turned out inevitably whimsical, that her hand curled even when she meant it to be plain, and that people walk slowly on a curve, which is the correct speed for arriving.  The district would fund art but not infrastructure, so the papers called it an installation, and the village called it a bridge, and both were true.  The weaving needed filling, the way a nest needs down.  She had four hundred letters and could not use one of them.  A keeper of secrets who spends her hoard is only a gossip with a longer memory.  So she carried the tin box door to door and gave each letter back to the mouth that had spoken it.  Keep it, she said, or fold it into the walk; either way it is yours, and either way you will have to cross the pond beside your neighbors.  Some kept them.  The widower brought his and would not say whose name was inside, and pressed it into the willow himself, and stood a while with his palm flat on the place.  The girl who could not spell patience came with her mother, and they folded one sheet between them into a paper twist the size of a thumb.  It took nine days.  The spiral went out over the lilies on floated bundles of reed, and the village walked on it before it was finished, which is how they learned it would hold.  Nobody could see the letters; they were inside the weave, the way a thrush keeps moss where the eggs lie.  On the tenth day the shore rejoined the temple.  The monks came out to meet the villagers at the second turning, and the light off the pond moved on everyone's faces, one color and then another, and no one spoke above the sound of the water.  Nima had written one letter of her own, finally, addressed to no one, and she wove it into the innermost turning, where the coil begins.  It said that she had spent her life carrying other people's words across a gap, and had found the thread at last, and it was not a sentence but a shape, and everyone was standing on it.  She left the tin box empty in the temple, an offering, and walked back along the curve behind the widower, who was humming.  Above them, a thrush was rebuilding, going around and around.
