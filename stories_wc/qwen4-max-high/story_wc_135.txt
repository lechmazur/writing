The rain stopped at noon, and the ivory harbor went white as a rinsed bone.  Maren carried her glass plates down to the quay before the puddles could dry.  She was the ocean-spray archivist, which the returned exiles took for a joke until they saw her ledgers.  Every morning she caught the sea's breath on glass, let the water leave, and kept the salt.  What mattered was the gaps, the clean shadows where something had stood between the water and the air.  Eleven years of exile had taught her to read absence the way pilots read stars. 

The exile had ended in spring, by a decree nobody in the harbor heard proclaimed.  The boats came home anyway, on rumor, in staggered intervals: two in April, one in June, nine this week.  Each boat gave her fragments, and she wanted what she always wanted, to compile them into meaning.  A shoe.  Half a lullaby.  A list of the drowned, salt-stiff, that stopped mid-name. 

Maren was reactively visionary, which is a clumsy way of saying she could not see until she was struck.  Touch a thing the sea had handled and the vision came at her, whole and unbidden, then faded in nine or ten breaths.  So she had learned to stagger her fragments.  An apprentice handed her one object every eight breaths, and each new shock caught the last picture before it dropped, and the pictures chained.  Through staggered intervals she could hold a minute of seeing, and a minute was enough to navigate an absence. 

The absence that mattered was the recall.  Odric the herald sat on the mounting block with his trumpet across his knees, and had sat there since the first boat.  The instrument had come back from the magistrate's warehouse with its second valve gone.  Without it the recall broke in the middle, a chord with a hole in it, and Odric would not blow a broken word to men still at sea.  Three boats were still out past the rain-line, waiting for a sound they could trust. 

Maren knelt and put her palm flat on the trumpet's empty socket.  The vision came like spray in the face.  She saw a clerk carry the valve to the seawall in a bag of confiscated brass.  She saw the bag tip.  Then the picture thinned, and she said, 'Next,' and the apprentice put a drowned man's shoe in her hand.  The shoe showed her the seawall from below, at low water, and the mud under the third mooring stone.  'Next.'  The half lullaby, sung into her ear by a girl who had learned it on the island, showed her a boy dredging that mud for crabs.  The list of the drowned stopped at 'Ti,' and the absence had a shape, and the shape was a boy called Til. 

She found him on the quay selling crabs, and the valve was in his pocket, green with eleven years of salt.  He had kept it because it was heavy and shone, which is reason enough for a child.  Maren did not scold him.  She traded him a coin and the story of what he had been carrying, so that he would know he had saved something.  To hearten a person, she had learned, you tell them what their hands have done. 

Odric took the valve as if it were hot.  He fitted it, worked it twice, and stood up on the mounting block.  The recall went out over the ivory water, whole, all three notes, the middle one restored.  Maren watched the exiles on the quay straighten, one after another, the way a field stands up after rain.  Out past the rain-line, three sails came about.  Odric lowered the trumpet and wept, and she heartened him the same way she had heartened the boy, by naming what he had refused to do and why. 

Then she went back to her plates.  The evening spray dried on the glass in a shape she had never filed: no shadow of a hull, no clean gap where a thing had been withheld.  She wrote the date, and under it, 'Recall sounded; all absences navigated home.'  It was the first entry in eleven years that meant something on its own, without her to read the gaps.  She left the plate uncatalogued on the sill, where the rain could find it, and went to help the last boats in.
