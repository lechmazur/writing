The first monarch came through the broken pane on the last evening of August, and Maren let it land on her chisel.  She did not move for eleven minutes.  Stillness, in her, was not gentle; it was a grip, a jaw set around a scream.  Thirty-one people had died in the ribbon mill in June, and the town had asked her, the gravestone carver, for a single stone that could hold them all.  Names, she knew, would only make thirty-one holes.  She wanted the shape of the thing itself, the way a river has a shape apart from its water.  The granite stood in the north aisle of the old greenhouse, ordered for a centennial that never happened, milkweed and aster crowding its base.  At dusk the glass went the color of weak tea, and her master came back.  Bram had taught her to cut letters for nine years, and had died in the spring, and now he stood in the condensation the way a reflection stands, wrong-handed and patient.  He said what he always said: retrace before you cut.  She drew the line in chalk and ran her finger backward along it, against the stroke, feeling for the place where the stone wanted to break.  The line was hers, and it was wrong, and both of them knew it.  He asked whose silence she had drawn, and she could not answer, and the question sat in her like a stone in a shoe.  By the second week the butterflies hung in ropes under the benches, and the sound of them opening and closing was a low thunder, the roar of a fire heard through a wall.  She worked in that noise and it steadied her.  In Bram's tool chest she found the last roll of woven ribbon the mill had made, cream with a red selvedge, and she took it home.  Her family had a custom: at dinner each person traded a favorite poem, spoke it aloud, and passed it on like a dish.  Since June the table had been silent, her father chewing with his eyes down.  She set the ribbon on the cloth and said they would trade poems again, and that she would invite the others, house by house, until all thirty-one had been spoken for.  Her father gave her four lines of Blake and wept without covering his face, which was the first honest thing he had done since the funerals.  She tied a knot in the ribbon at every place his voice stopped.  Not the words: the stops.  The widow Halloran chose a psalm and broke in the middle of a line; the boy who had swept the mill's floor chose nothing, and his silence took two feet of ribbon.  Week by week the dinners filled the greenhouse, because the houses were too small for so many, and the butterflies came and went above the soup.  Bram stood in the glass and said nothing, which she understood at last was the whole of his teaching: he had never once told her what to cut.  In October the ropes thinned.  The ribbon was thirty-eight feet of knots and gaps, and she pinned it along the granite and retraced it with her finger, backward, against the stroke, until her hand knew every hesitation by heart.  Then she cut it: one line wandering the length of the stone, swelling at the knots, thinning to nothing where the voices had failed.  It took nine days, and she sat an hour before each session, breathing, holding her stillness like a hammer held above a nail.  The last monarch went south through the broken pane on the evening she finished, and the quiet afterward was louder than the wings had been.  Bram's shape did not appear in the glass.  She wiped the pane with her sleeve and found only her own face, older, steady, and behind it the ferns and the stone.  They came at dusk to look, and no one asked what the line meant.  They put their fingers in the groove and ran them backward, all of them, without being told, finding their own knots in the dark.  Her father brought bread and a poem, and they ate under the empty benches, and the grief had a shape now, and it was long, and it went on, and it held.
