The phoenix rose nine nights ago, and the orchard had already forgotten the fire.  Moonlight here never moved; it hung above the rows like a lamp left burning in a kitchen window, and under it the apple trees bloomed and fruited at once, silver on silver.  Yeva walked the rows with a tin of ash under her arm and a slip of paper folded into her shoe. 

She was the beast whisperer, which meant that all her life she had stood at the edge of the human world and leaned toward the other one.  Wolves told her their grievances; sows let her lift their piglets; once a dying elk had breathed its last thought into her cupped hands.  She had never once been asked to join them. 

Tam had baked in the village below the orchard, and she had loved Yeva for eleven years.  The phoenix's rising burned the bakery flat, and Yeva swept what was left into a tin.  The fire always returns what it takes, but it returns it wrong.  Ash becomes beasts; the orchard fills with them each cycle, dumb and new, wearing no memory of the people they had been.  The beasts told her of a new doe that had come up from the burned village and would not eat.  Every animal in the orchard answered her; the new doe did not. 

Words were the problem.  A returned beast has no room in it for language; speech slides off such a mind like rain off oiled canvas.  But scent goes deeper than any word, down under memory, into the floor of the world where the phoenix keeps its ledgers.  So Yeva had come to conjure. 

She set the tin down between two roots and opened her satchel: cubeb, grains of paradise, black cardamom, one thread of saffron.  Tam had bought them for the shop from a caravan that came once a decade, and the smell of them was the smell of Tam's hands at four in the morning.  Her hands had shaken since the burning.  They went paradoxically steady now, the moment they touched the mortar, as if grief were a thing that could only be carried by the whole body and never by the fingers.  She ground the spices and the moon leaned closer, the way it does when someone is working. 

Then she took the slip of paper from her shoe.  Tam had written the fortunes herself, folding them into the cookies while the dough was warm, and Yeva had opened this one on the morning of the fire.  It said: "Come as you are."  "I will come as something else."  She had thought it was a joke about the shop's new oven.  Now she understood the terms. 

A conjured scent will not keep in a jar or on a human palm; it has to rise out of a living coat, from the blood outward, warm.  Only a beast can carry it, and only a beast can receive it.  That was the price, and she had been refusing it for nine nights.  If she went over, she would lose her words, and words were the whole of her usefulness; she would be one more dumb new animal in the rows, and there would be no one left standing at the edge, leaning.  Worse: the moon takes memory from whatever it changes.  She could go to Tam and arrive as a stranger. 

She rolled the paper in the powder until every word was coated, and she swallowed it, and she swallowed the rest of the spice after it.  Let the moon take her mind; the promise would be in her stomach, in her blood, in the smell coming out of her. 

The conjuring took hold, and it was not gentle, and she did not want it.  Her jaw lengthened, her spine poured itself into a new arrangement, her vocabulary went out like a candle carried outdoors, and she was a doe, small and dun, standing in the same footprints.  She could not remember the word for wife.  She could not remember her own name, or the tin, or why the ash mattered.  But she smelled of cardamom and burnt sugar and four in the morning, and across the rows another doe lifted her head. 

The other doe came slowly, the way you approach a thing you have no reason to trust and no ability to refuse.  She put her nose to Yeva's throat and breathed, and kept breathing, and did not step back.  Beyond the trees the phoenix went on turning its new light, and the moon stayed where it was, and the orchard bloomed and fruited and asked them to explain nothing.  It was enough.
