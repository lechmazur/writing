The chainsaws would start when the frost released the ground, so the orchard had one hour of hush left, and Verity spent it in the sheet music archive, reconnecting a wire.  The movers had severed the induction loop when they shifted the shelves, and a loop is only a promise until both ends meet.  Frost light came through the packing-house windows and lay on ten thousand scores like a held rest.  Verity wrote the county paper's gossip column, which was how she cast things out: name a demon in print and the whole town says it, and the saying pulls it loose.  Nine years ago she had printed the name of the thing riding Ada Bell, and it had come out through Ada's ears, and taken her hearing with it.  That was the arithmetic of her trade: a miracle always leaves the room limping.  The town chewed the name for a year, and a chewed name does not die; it drifted until it found the antique perfume bottle in the archive's curiosity case, blossom attar distilled from these very trees in 1911.  Scent and gossip are cousins: both travel without touching anybody, and both stay in the curtains.  Now the orchard was sold, the scores were bound for the pulper, and when the trees fell the attar would have no living source, and the thing would go looking for something warm.  Ada catalogued second-violin parts forty feet away, deaf, patient, the nearest warm thing.  Verity had come to apologize, nine years late, which is the only punctuality the sorry ever manage.  She could not say it plainly; plain names are handles, and the thing in the bottle would climb into any sentence built straight.  So she talked the way she always talked, slant.  "A gardener who salts a bed to kill one weed," she said into the loop's microphone, "should not be surprised when the roses come up tasting of the sea."  Ada's pen stopped.  The hearing aid in her ear held a little coil, and the loop under the floorboards held a big one, and between them there was nothing but a changing field.  Verity had learned the trick from the archive's own wiring: you do not touch the thing you want to move; you change around it until current rises in it uninvited.  An apology works the same way, if it works at all.  She twisted the last splice, and the amplifier's needle lifted, and Ada touched her ear like a woman checking whether rain had started.  "I salted your bed," Verity said.  "I have carried the shovel ever since."  Ada wrote on a catalog card and held it up: SAY IT STRAIGHT.  Straight speech was the one door Verity had kept shut for nine years.  She opened the case, unstoppered the bottle, and the reading room filled with a hundred springs.  She wrapped the loop's bare ground around her wrist and leaned into the microphone and said the plain thing, her own name first, the way you ground a wire before you touch it: "Verity Crane printed Ada Bell's demon to sell papers, and Ada paid."  The bottle went cold; the blossom smell thinned; the field swung hard, and what had lived in the attar came across without touching anything and settled behind Verity's teeth.  Ada heard it go: one clean tone, the first sound in nine years, gone as soon as it arrived.  Imperfect, all of it: her hearing would not come back, the orchard would fall, the apology had cost more than it repaired.  But Ada crossed the room and put two fingers on Verity's wrist, on the pulse, the way you test a wire for current.  "Slant again," Verity said, because straight speech was spent, and something behind her teeth was listening for handles.  "A bell that's been rung too hard keeps a crack, but it still hangs in the tower."  It was the only advice she had, and slant was the only way she could give it.  Ada laughed without sound and wrote: COME BACK WHEN THE STUMPS ARE IN.  Outside, the frost let go, and the first saw opened its throat, and the hush ended.  Verity pocketed the empty bottle, which was only glass now, worth exactly nothing, and went to write her last column: a retraction, nine years late, printed where the whole town would read it and finally stop saying the other name.  Some debts you cannot pay; you can only stand close enough, and change fast enough, to induce a little warmth in the one you wronged.
