Above the cloud sea, on a stone terrace at the top of the cable line, grew an arbor of old wisteria, and Marta kept her watch beneath it every midnight.  The village below had no road, only the drop zone on the plateau, and the freight plane came at night when the wind slept.  Marta was a pattern seeker: she read the shiver of the leaves, the swing of the anemometer lamp, the nine-second breathing of the ridge, and from those small repetitions she told the pilot whether the air over the terrace would hold a parachute open.  That was the watcher's burden: everyone else slept inside her decision.  Eleven years ago a watcher called a false clear, and the rotor folded her father's canopy over the ridge, and all they recovered from the scree was his parachute buckle, its tongue snapped.  She had rebuilt the lamp shutter around it, using the broken tongue as the catch, so that every signal she sent began with the same press of her thumb.  Tonight the radio had been full of static, a storm somewhere over the sea throwing gravel into the channel, so she worked the old way, lamps and log.  At one o'clock she wrote clear in the log and shuttered green toward the north.  Teo, her apprentice, copied the entry in his own book, proud of the neatness.  Then the static cleared.  It went out all at once, like water draining, and into the silence came the pilot, Halloran, twelve minutes out and asking for the valley temperature.  The valley crew answered: down four degrees in half an hour.  Marta looked up into the arbor.  The wisteria had changed its breathing; the leaves turned every seven seconds now, not nine, and each turn ran a little further along the trellis before it died.  Cold air was pooling on the plateau and would soon spill over the ridge, and a spilling deck builds a rotor that folds a canopy like paper.  She had been attentively inventive all her life, building instruments out of scrap so she could see what nobody else on the mountain could see, and now the thing she saw was that she had been wrong.  "You already logged clear," Teo said.  He meant the medicine in the crate, the fuel Halloran was burning, the company that already called the lamp watch a superstition.  To revise the call was to admit she could not read her own hill.  But trust is not built by being consistent; it is built by being believable when it costs something.  She keyed the microphone.  "Halloran, this is Arbor, revise my call to red, wave off, cold deck spilling over the ridge, rotor building, seven-second period and shortening."  There was a pause of the kind that has weight.  Then Halloran said, "Copy red, we hold north and come back at first light."  "Tell me what you see," he said.  Her hands were already moving.  She did not think the code; she had drilled it blindfolded for eleven winters, thumb on the snapped tongue, three long, two short, and her fingers repeated it the way they remembered tying her father's knots, without asking her permission.  The lamp went red over the terrace, and the aircraft's light swung away north, and then the rotor came.  It came the way things come above the clouds, without sound: the wisteria lifted all at once, every leaf standing on end, the cloud floor below the terrace heaving in a long slow swell that broke against the ridge and rolled back.  Marta watched the empty air fold a canopy that was not there, the same silent fold she had carried for eleven years, and this time nothing fell out of it.  By four the deck had drained, and the arbor breathed at nine again.  She struck out her earlier line, wrote the revision and the reason beside it, and had Teo sign as witness, because a log that hides its corrections is worth nothing to the next watcher.  Then she put his thumb on the buckle and taught him the drill, three long, two short, until his hand knew it in the dark.  At first light Halloran came in over a quiet ridge, and the crate swung down under a white canopy, and the village below the clouds got its medicine, and no one ever heard how close the night had come to repeating itself.  Only the arbor kept the echo, and it kept it without sound.
