The mist at Zephyr Bluff never lifted; it only thinned to silver and thickened again.  Ilse Marek had lived inside that silver for nine months, since the service struck her name from its rolls and called the omission a clerical mercy.  A burned spy learns to be small.  She had been burned for refusing to give up a name; the refusal was the only clean thing she owned.  She mended things instead: the harbor's fog bell, watches, the drystone wall the winter had shouldered apart.  Fixing what was broken was the only work nobody could revoke.  Then came the hinge of the year, the tilt of the earth toward spring.  The snow on the bluff grew granular, layered, patient.  Every Thursday she walked to the flat ledge above the water and pressed letters into the accumulated crust with a spoon handle.  Depth of stroke gave the vowels; spacing gave the rest.  Snow was the safest paper: it held a message a week and then forgave it.  No one had ever answered.  She wrote anyway, because she had come here to find where unused courage waits, and she suspected it waited in her, spoiling like grain in a sealed bin.  On the first Thursday of March a boy came up the switchback path, holding a small brass pillbox in both hands as if it were warm.  "He said you would know the box," the boy said.  She did.  Anselm had carried it through three borders, and he had engraved the lid himself with a wren, because he said a wren was the loudest small thing alive.  Anselm had also signed the order that burned her.  "He died in January," the boy said.  "He told me to bring this before the thaw."  Inside, on the cotton, lay a strip of film no longer than a fingernail paring.  She held it to the grey window light: a grid, an offset, the old key for the snow alphabet, and beneath it four words in Anselm's cramped hand.  "Mend what I broke."  So he had read her Thursday letters, all of them, from the far side of the water, with a glass.  Nine months of silence had been a man listening.  The film held one more line: a name, and the department that had bought him.  The name was not Anselm's.  It belonged to the man who still signed the pay orders, and he had let Anselm carry the blame down into the ground.  The name she had once swallowed had been innocent; this one was not.  The boy watched her with his hands empty.  "Do I take something back?"  Here was the fork she had rehearsed for nine months.  Give the boy everything and he would be stopped at the ferry, and the film would go into a stove, and he would go into the water.  Give him nothing and the broken thing stayed broken, and she stayed a clerical mercy.  She had been trained to be daring; she had survived by being prudently daring, which is a different animal.  She kept the film.  She vouchsafed the boy one syllable of the name, the first, and the number of a locker in the ferry town.  He was to say it to the woman who sold bait, who owed Ilse a winter of mended reels.  One syllable could not hang anybody, but it could open a door.  Then she walked him to the ledge and showed him the spoon, the crust, the depth of stroke.  "Write it here," she said.  "Snow keeps a week and then forgives.  If they take you, you will have carried nothing that lasts."  He pressed the letters badly, and she did not correct him, because badly was safer.  When he had gone down the switchback she went to the bell.  She had brazed its cracked clapper in November and never once swung it, out of some superstition that a mended thing should not be tested.  She swung it now.  The note went out into the mist, flat and silver and enormous, and a boat out in the white answered her, and then another.  Then the silver hush closed over the water again.  So that was where unused courage waited: not in some reserve inside her, but in the mended things she had been afraid to ring.  The thaw would take the ledge in a week.  She would go down to the ferry with the film sewn in her cuff, and mend the rest herself.
