After the severance, the islet kept only one road, and it led up through wet grass to the bonsai terrace.  Rain had just gone over, and the breeze carried its smell, iron and salt, past the pots.  I climbed with a ghost ship's compass wrapped in my dyeing apron.  My bonsai tutor, Master Odile, was wiring a juniper, and she did not look up for a long time.  She spoke slowly, setting each word down before choosing the next, like stones across a stream.  "You brought a needle that points at what nobody said," she said.  "I want the color of unspoken apologies," I told her.  I was a dyer; my father's mourning band still hung in the workshop, white as a held breath.  Six years ago I told him the sea was calm, and he went out, and the severance took the horizon and him with it.  I never said the true thing, which was that I had been angry and wanted him gone.  So I would dye that apology and wear it where words had failed.  Odile breathed in a rhythmically placid way, four counts in, four counts out, and the juniper seemed to breathe with her.  "Then you know what the tides of remembrance do," she said.  I knew: twice a month the sea brought back a day, and we stood in it again, and the islet wore that day like weather.  The compass had come ashore on the last one, lifted from a hull that was mostly light.  Now, in my hands, its needle swung off north and pointed at my chest.  Water came up through the terrace tiles that was not rain.  I smelled his oilskin; I heard the oar knock; the whole islet tilted toward one afternoon.  "It gathers the tide into a single point," Odile said, unhurried, while the water reached my ankles.  "One memory, all of it, at once."  "That is how the ships were lost."  Her slowness was a defense: a tide cannot gather around a person who takes a minute to finish a word.  She reached under her bench and unrolled a lace panel, brown with age, the pattern dense as a chart.  Her grandmother had made it, and her grandmother's grandmother, and the instructions were woven into it: a hole for a breath released, a crossed pair for a thing shared, five picots for the five ways of letting a grief spread thin.  "My eyes are old," she said.  "Read it aloud, and do what it says."  I had read cloth all my life, and the lace spoke.  Hole: breathe out.  Cross: give some of it away.  Five picots: divide, divide, divide, divide, divide.  "Into the juniper," Odile said, and set my hands on the needles.  A bonsai is a grief taught to grow in many directions at once.  I gave the afternoon to a thousand small green points, a little to each, and the water stopped rising.  It did not leave; it diffused, thin as dye in a full vat, until every leaf held one grain of my father's last morning.  Odile's four counts in, four counts out, kept the time for me while I worked.  The needle slowed, then swung, uncertain, toward the tree, toward the sea, toward everything.  "Now say it," she said.  "There is no one to hear."  "The tide hears."  So I told the wet air that I had been angry, that I had wanted the house quiet, that I had lied about the sea.  The breeze took it apart and carried it out over the water in pieces small enough to be borne.  Out past the reef a hull of light came briefly solid, and went.  The compass needle lay still, pointing at nothing, which is north.  I looked for the color I had come for.  It was there for a moment: cerulean, the blue of a thing withheld too long, standing in the water on the tiles.  Then it thinned, the way a dye thins when the cloth is finally dipped, and the sky went to ordinary evening blue.  "It only exists while it is unspoken," Odile said.  "You have made yourself a poorer dyer and a lighter woman."  I left the mourning band white, and I left the compass on her bench.  She will need it, or someone will, when the next tide brings a day nobody has divided yet.  At the bottom of the grass road I stopped and breathed, four counts in, four counts out, and the islet breathed with me.
