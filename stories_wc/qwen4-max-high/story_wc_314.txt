The waterfall had been falling for ten thousand years, and it was still new.  Maren, an alchemist with auburn hair gone half to ash, stood in its spray and let the light break over her: red, green, gold, the colors of the mineral seams shifting with every breath of the sun.  Behind that kaleidoscopic waterfall lay the orchard no map admitted.  She stepped through, as her teacher had once stepped through, into air that smelled of the first morning of the world: wet bark, cold pears, grass still deciding to grow.  In one hour the Work would reach its final transformation, and everything she had calculated for forty-one years would be tested in a single drop.  The drop was not yet a drop.  On a strand of spider silk strung between two pear branches hung a frozen dew crystal, no larger than a wren's egg, holding within it water that had never touched the ground.  Her ledgers called it the fixed volatile; her teacher had called it wonder, and said that wonder could be measured but never handled.  She had spent her life trying to prove him half right.  Every failed furnace had taught her the same lesson: number alone was blind, and feeling alone was a fire without a hearth.  She wanted them married in her hands, the ratio and the hunch, one flame between them.  The crystal had to pass through flame to give up its water, and flame was the one thing she trusted absolutely, because flame did not lie about temperature.  Too long in it and the dew would boil away into ordinary steam; too little and it would stay locked, a stone.  Her tables gave her a range of nine heartbeats, which was another way of saying they gave her nothing.  So she looked up, past the branches, to where the starlings were coming over the falls in their thousands.  Her teacher had found the orchard by following them; she had learned, over decades of ruined mornings, to read them.  She had tried reading them as data, plotting wingbeats against wind, and had got only noise.  The flock was a gesture, not a number, and it had to be read the way a face is read.  Above the water the birds knotted, loosened, poured themselves into a shape like a cupped palm.  Wait, said the palm.  She lit the little lamp she had carried through the water, set the crucible of lead in the grass, trimmed the wick to the width of a hair, and laid the crystal in the iron spoon.  The spoon had been his, its handle cracked, and she had never once written in her ledgers why she still used it.  In the margins, where a reader would expect corrections, she had written the names of the trees: Old Mother, Two Sisters, the one he had planted the spring he died.  Colleagues called her cold, and she let them, because a woman who wept over glassware would not be given a furnace.  The starlings tightened, spun, and opened: a flame shape, wavering, then a hand opening finger by finger.  Now.  She held the spoon in the flame and counted, not with the tables but with the birds, feeling the flock's turn in her own wrists as if the sky were a muscle she belonged to.  Four heartbeats, five, and the crystal began to cloud at its heart.  The tables had given her nine heartbeats of margin; the birds had given her the one heartbeat inside it.  She lifted the spoon out.  Wonder, he had written, can be measured but never handled; she saw now that he had been afraid, and that the fear was the one error in his books.  She took the half-thawed crystal out of the spoon with her bare fingers, which no protocol permitted.  It was colder than anything she had ever held, and warm where she touched it, and that touch of wonder was the last measure, the one no instrument could take.  Above the falls the starlings broke apart into ten thousand separate birds, each one going somewhere, the pattern finished.  The ice let go.  A single drop hung at her fingertip, shaking with all the colors of the waterfall, and beneath it the crucible waited in the wet grass.  She said his name aloud, to the trees, because there was no longer any reason not to.  Then, before the final transformation, in an orchard that smelled as old and as new as the first morning, she let it fall.  Whatever came next, she had already touched it.
