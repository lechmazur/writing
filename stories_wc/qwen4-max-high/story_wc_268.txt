Nen was four feet tall and weighed as much as the bell tower, which was how the old blood announced itself: not in height but in density.  She was the last titan, and she mended her own sandals because there was no one left to do it for her.  Readers who found her called her the librarian, and she never corrected them, though she poured her own tea and swept her own aisle.  The temple library had slipped its century long ago, taking her with it when the purges came.  A rune of drift was cut into the threshold stone, and it kept the whole building sliding sideways through time, the way a boat slides along a current it cannot see.  Only when the constellations shifted did the drift pause, and in that pause the doors opened onto some waking year, and readers wandered in with wet boots and candle stubs.  They stayed an hour, or a lifetime; the pause measured neither.  Nen used the pauses to work.  Her cousin Hesper sat the throne in the world outside, six and a half feet of gilt and borrowed ancestry, and the priests had crowned him because he looked like a titan ought to look, and she did not.  The weighing stones had drifted away with the temple, so the priests judged by silhouette.  He had forged the genealogy leaf that named his line, and she meant to prove it with a library that could not stay in one century long enough to be believed.  So she wrote in margins.  She kept one iron gall ink pellet, gray as a molar, in a tin box, and each pause she ground a shaving of it into rainwater and filled her pen.  Iron gall bites; given centuries it eats clean through the page, so every message she left was already dying, and she wrote knowing her words would one day fall out of the paper as lace.  The messages were cryptic because plain questions get burned.  In a book of tides she wrote: The tall king's grandmother has no shadow; hold her to the candle.  In a herbal she wrote: True ink devours the house it lives in; ask the crown's leaf whether it has begun to eat.  Books went out with the readers, and answers came back in the same margins, in other hands.  Sometimes the answer arrived before she had written the question.  A cartographer in one century asked the crown archivists for the leaf; a glassmaker three centuries later held it to a candle and wrote what he saw.  No shadow, no bite: the letters lay on the vellum like soot on glass, though the leaf claimed four hundred years.  Nen copied the testimony into her record, and beside it laid the true roll from her own shelves, the one that named her, where the old ink had eaten every letter into a window.  Proof, and no court to carry it to.  She could not leave while the rune held: the drift would set her down in a year with no Hesper in it, and no court.  To reach the year that was actually happening, she would have to sunder the rune, and four thousand years would arrive in a single afternoon.  She thought about it for one pause, which is to say for no time at all, and then she knelt on the threshold with her thumb on the cut stone.  The rune broke like a sugar crust.  The library did not crash; it sighed.  Shelves settled, plaster came down like slow snow, and the oldest books let their eaten pages go, so the air filled with paper lace, every letter she and her strangers had written falling loose through the light.  Outside, the constellations finished their shift.  She walked to the capital with the roll under her arm and the pellet in her pocket, and nobody stopped a small woman carrying paper.  In the throne room she set the true roll on the step and asked the priests to bring Hesper's leaf and a candle.  The light came through the old letters and stopped at the new ones.  Hesper looked at the two pages for a long moment, then took off the crown himself, which was the only graceful thing he ever did, and set it on the step beside her evidence.  She took the throne; the step cracked under her, and she never had a cushion built for her feet.  That winter she began a new record with the last shaving of the pellet, knowing the ink would eat its way out of the page in four hundred years, and that someone would have to hold her words to the light.
