The streetlights were still burning at five in the morning, amber against a sky already the color of skimmed milk, and nobody in the sixty houses along the bay had mentioned it in years.  I had come back to empty my grandmother's house, and I noticed.  I had come for the paperwork, but also for a question I have carried since I was nine: why some houses feel alive, and others are only rooms.  That is my affliction: I notice the creep rather than the change, the slow unanimous vote a place takes that a thing has always been so.  The ferry had stopped, then the cannery, then the choir, and the town called the silence that followed peace.  Spring came in regardless, gorse firing the headland yellow, the bay flat as poured glass.  I worked at dawn because the agent wanted photographs and I wanted the house alone, in the hour when the lamps burn on past their purpose, daydreaming, believing themselves necessary.  Three mornings, and then the keys would go into an envelope. 

In the linen drawer I found a shard of orris root, pale as a knuckle, folded into waxed paper.  Nan had given nine years to a perfumery before the bay took her back, and she taught me that orris must dry three years before it will speak, and that when it speaks it says violets, and rain.  It is a fixative: it adds nothing, it holds what is already there and keeps it from leaving.  There was another shard in the hall cupboard, another behind the clock, one in every room, seven in all, each in its paper, each holding its small weather. 

The agent came on Thursday and used the word peaceful four times.  I was thinking about the disclosure form when I said it, without looking up.  "It isn't finished."  He stopped in the doorway.  I heard my own sentence half a second after he did, the way you hear a bell after the strike, and neither of us filled the gap.  He left early, and I sat on the stairs with the shard in my palm and finally understood what I had been noticing all week. 

The other houses along the bay were quiet.  Nan's was hushed.  Quiet is what remains when nothing is being held.  A hush is held on purpose, the way orris holds a violet; each of her seven papers was a shard of hush, a silence with a hand underneath it.  She had buried a husband the bay kept, and she had decided, in her perfumer's way, that nothing else in that house would be allowed to evaporate.  Forty years of a woman deciding, every time she opened a drawer, that this house would keep its breath.  That is why some houses feel alive: someone is still holding them, and the holding does not stop the moment the hand does.  The town had let go, room by room, year by year, and called the letting go peace, and the lamps burned on into daylight because no one remembered they were for the dark. 

On the last morning, while the streetlights daydreamed, I did not bless the house, because I have no words for that and Nan had none either.  I sanctified it the only way I could think of: I moved things.  I turned the kitchen chair to face the window instead of the table.  I set her blue cup upside down on the sill, where no one keeps a cup.  I hung the front door key on the wrong hook.  Then I went from room to room and put six shards of orris where they did not belong: in the coal scuttle, under the stair, on the high shelf a stranger would have to stretch for.  A thing out of place asks to be put right, and the asking is a claim on the next hand that comes through the door.  Let the buyer feel the small wrongness and reach, and in reaching, hold.  Let the house be caught mid-gesture, unfinished, and therefore alive.  I left the door open the width of a hand. 

The seventh shard I kept, and it went warm in my pocket like a coal that has decided not to go out, smelling of violets and rain and the whole held spring.  Behind me the lamps went out along the bay, one after another, and I was the only one who noticed, and the hush came with me, and I did not let it go.
