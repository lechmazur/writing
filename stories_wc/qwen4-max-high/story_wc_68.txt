The sewer on Candle Hill stays dry because the people who built it understood slope.  Below it lies the sunken city, streets of green water, roofs used as stepping stones, a harbor full of masts leaning together like drunks.  I live in the dry part and call myself a philosopher, which annoys everyone.  The tidewalkers call me the Drain, and I have never corrected them, since they are also right. 

Officially the plague ended in spring and the sea came in summer; the arguing began before either was finished.  The sickness is gone, the survivors are back, and two stories floated up with them.  The Bell Wardens, who ring the hours and ration the barley, say the water rose after the sickness was cleansed, as a verdict.  The tidewalkers say the magistrates opened the sea gates while the sick quarter still coughed, drowning forty thousand to spare the rest.  Both stories are certain, and neither has ever been measured. 

I read sediment.  Silt cannot be bribed and wants no moral, which makes it the last honest archive in this city.  In the flooded cistern beneath the mint the layers stack like pages: ash from the burnings, lime from the burial pits, the coarse sand of the flood, then the fine gray patience of everything after.  Odile taught me at the harbor school, and drowned in the room where she kept her instruments.  I have wanted one thing since: to keep her methods alive, because a method is the only inheritance that survives being contradicted. 

The Wardens burned the old tables and named reckoning a plague art.  I obeyed.  I stopped teaching numbers and took a job sweeping the cistern stair, and I described the dirt aloud while I swept, since no decree forbids a man narrating dirt.  Anyone may listen to a sweeper.  Rage gets you hanged; a ruler gets you an audience. 

In the flood sand I found a brass cog with seventeen teeth, stamped with a rate and a maker's mark.  It came from a seafarer's chronometer, the boxed clock that carries a home port's noon across an ocean so a ship can reckon its longitude.  The harbor master kept one in the gatehouse above the mint, and its teeth shear when the case is struck hard.  Gatehouse brass and chain filings ride in that sand with it, so the breaking and the flooding are one event. 

Directly beneath the flood sand lies burial lime a hand thick, still bitter, still burned.  Lime that thick means the pits were working when the water arrived.  The gates opened during the plague, not after it.  The tidewalkers are right, and the people who feed us are lying. 

For three nights I agonized, which for me means lying very still and counting teeth.  Seventeen, seventeen, seventeen.  Hand the cog to the tidewalkers and they will hang the water reeve, who is the last magistrate's son, and who also runs the barley boats.  Hand it to the Wardens and they will melt it, and Odile's arithmetic dies with it.  Keep it and I am only a man with a secret in a drain.  I had wanted a doctrine with my name on it; I discovered I wanted a verdict more than I wanted a method, and the wanting disgusted me. 

So I chose a fourth thing.  I bolted the cog to a slate at the sewer mouth, drew the cistern column beside it to scale, and wrote the depths where anyone can dig.  Under it I cut the harbor school's rule: count, compare, doubt, count again.  No conclusion carved anywhere. 

The Wardens came to ask whose side I was on.  I told them the tide table, and offered them a bucket.  The tidewalkers came to ask why I would not simply say murder.  I said the word is theirs to earn at low water, with a trowel, in front of witnesses who hate them. 

A boy from the barley boats dug the first column on Tuesday and got a hand thickness of lime, which is what I got.  He was disappointed that I did not congratulate him.  I told him to do it again on the other stair, and to write down the number even if it ruins his opinion. 

Two stories still fight above me, and both now must come down here to speak.  Let them fight; the layers will keep, and the counting will outlast whoever wins.  I am not the author of the truth, only the drain that keeps the ruler dry.
