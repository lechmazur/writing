Three years of drought had cracked the city open, and then the rain came for nine days and filled everything the cracks had made.  Marisol Vance investigated preventable deaths for the county, which meant arriving late and measuring how little it would have taken to stop them.  The call was a track walker named Abel Marsh, found behind a sealed cross-passage door under Halsey Street.  The department had already filed it as misadventure, which was the word they used when nobody wanted a second look. 

Water fell through the sidewalk grates in ropes, and the maintenance tunnel threw the sound back so that every drop arrived twice.  Above her a train passed, and the whole tunnel hummed with it, and she waited for the hum to die before she trusted her own ears.  She went along the wall tapping with a wrench, listening to the reverberation change.  At the ninth panel the echo turned hollow, and behind plywood she found brick older than the subway itself.  She unbolted the plywood. 

The room beyond was a cellar twelve feet square, with an iron hook in the ceiling where a rope had once lifted people through a trapdoor.  Abel sat against the far wall with his knees drawn up, like a man resting.  In his lap lay a tin lantern punched with stars, the kind carried on the Underground Railroad.  Abel had documented the cellar off the books, a station where people once hid on their way north.  He had gone down to save a room the transit authority wanted filled with concrete. 

She had been taught never to touch anything bare-handed.  She touched it.  His last two days pushed into her all at once: the dark, the thirst, the counting.  Entangled memories, Dr. Pell called them; he had opened her to them when she was nineteen and had adjudicated her mind ever since.  "You cannot tell yours from theirs," he liked to say.  "That is why you have me."  He had trained her to be watchfully calm, and for eleven years she had mistaken the leash for the training.  He had built a whole career on her certainty that she could not trust herself. 

She set the lantern down and made herself breathe.  Then she did the only thing she trusted: she looked.  In the memory, Abel had scratched tally marks into the mortar, one for every hour, and stopped at forty-one.  She counted the wall.  Forty-one.  In the memory he had crawled to the standpipe and found it capped and dry, shut off for the drought.  She crawled.  The cap was there, wrench-tight, the city seal unbroken.  In the memory he had punched one extra hole into the lantern's largest star with his pocketknife, so the pattern would belong to him and not to a museum.  She held the tin up to her headlamp and counted six points where every other star had five, the sixth still bright with new metal. 

Nothing in her head needed Pell's permission to be true.  A memory could be checked against a wall, a valve, a piece of tin; that was not faith, that was observation, and it belonged to her.  So she let the rest of it in, all forty-one hours, the cracked lips and the waiting and the hope each time the pumps started.  It buckled her; she stayed standing in the cold water and let it buckle her, and then she was still standing. 

The finding wrote itself.  A lock-out tag pulled without a headcount; a valve closed for a drought that ended two days after Abel stopped counting.  Ninety seconds of checking would have kept him alive.  Pell called while she was still underground, and his ringtone reverberated along the pipes, patient and enormous.  She let it ring.  She would tell him tomorrow that entangled memories were evidence, that evidence could be tested, and that she was done being told which of her own hours were hers. 

She signed the report with her name alone.  She set her headlamp inside the tin and closed the little door on it.  Then she logged the lantern as evidence and carried it up the ladder herself, because it had been made for exactly this: someone in the dark, moving toward the free side of a wall.  Rain came down through the grate, and the little punched stars went ahead of her, showing the way.
