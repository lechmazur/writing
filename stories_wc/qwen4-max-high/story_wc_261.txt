The tower held its breath the way the sky had held its breath the night the stone fell.  Ines woke on the cot beneath the bell nobody rang anymore, and the anesthesia let go of her one nerve at a time.  They had sewn her shut again in the night.  Pain came up the way dawn comes up, gray first, then color. 

On the sill stood the frosted hourglass the nurses used to time her doses, its glass clouded so you could see only the shape of what remained.  Her grandmother had said: you read the level, not the grains, the way you read a scar and not the knife.  Ines had spent eleven months trying to learn that reading.  Her mother had died of it in the spring; in autumn the surgeons cut the same thing out of Ines. 

A stone fell out of the sky that night and went into the orchard below the tower, and the village called it a miracle, and it was, only it had been aimed wrong.  The blight went out of the apple trees in an hour, blossom opened in the frost, and her stitches stayed wet and stubborn and ordinary.  The grace meant for her body had landed in the roots instead, and a little of it had spilled into her ears.  That was how she came to hear the ivy.  All night it talked against the stone, not in words but in green pressure, wanting to tell her about the heat that had come down into the ground. 

She had picked her stitches open three times looking for something under them.  The nurses called it noncompliance; her aunt called it grief; Ines called it asking.  You cannot get an answer out of a wound you keep opening, her aunt said, but Ines had never once been able to leave a question closed. 

So she went to the table where the nurses set their cups and did what her grandmother had taught her: she examined the coffee rings.  Rings are the scars a table keeps.  A full circle means a cup set down easy.  A circle with a gap means the cup was lifted before it was empty, and the gap points where the hand went.  Eight rings were closed; the ninth belonged to the nurse who set her cup down half finished the night the sky lit, and its gap faced the orchard. 

Out on the frosted grass she read the apple bark the same way: every scar on every trunk had a gap, and the gaps all faced one place.  The ivy pulled at her sleeve with its small cold hands and said: under, under.  The old ice tunnel ran from the tower cellar out beneath the orchard, and its mouth had caved in years ago.  She turned the hourglass and went down with a trowel. 

She dug on her belly in the dark, and the roots came through the ceiling to meet her, and they were warm.  Above her the sand was going, and she felt her stitches give, one and then another, and she kept digging, because it was the same thing she had done with her fingernails all week, only now it had a direction. 

At the tunnel's end she found the stone, no bigger than a plum, wrapped in white roots grown around it the way a hand grows around a coal it will not drop.  The trees were talking all at once, a hush of them, the way a room goes quiet after something bright crosses the sky.  She could have pulled it out.  She could have carried her miracle up into the light and pressed it against her belly and been closed by morning. 

Instead she put her two hands around the roots and read them.  There was a gap in every ring of them, and the gap was not a wound; it was where the thing had gone in.  Her mother's old scar had had one too, and Ines had never looked at it, only at the story she wanted it to tell.  She lay in the cold tunnel with her opened side and let the trees keep what had been meant for her. 

She climbed up before the sand ran out and let the nurses sew her again, and this time she kept her hands quiet.  Dawn came into the quiet tower, and the orchard breathed out under the frost, and Ines set her cup on the sill and lifted it before it was empty.  The ring it left was a circle with one small gap facing the trees, which is not a wound, which is where the light gets out.
