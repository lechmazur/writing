After the last resource war, the settlement of Slaggate spread across the cracked wasteland on rafts of scrap.  Car doors bridged the fissures, and the wind played the pipe stacks so softly that everyone learned to listen harder.  Nessa called that sound the wild hush, and she made her living inside it.  People brought her distances and asked her to make them bearable.  She never answered in miles.  "The water tower is two songs and one argument away," she would say, or, "Your sister is as far as a shout goes when nobody answers."  Her descriptions were not measurements but intuitions that arrived already dressed, and they were always right.  Her whole quiet ambition was to translate distance into closeness, so that no one in Slaggate had to feel far from anything. 

Her brother Teo had gone east in the spring to find the water the war had drunk.  He sent back one phonograph cylinder, wrapped in a shirt, carried by a scavenger who wanted salt.  On it he sang a marching song badly, the way he always had.  But a man who sings too loudly is hiding a whisper, and Nessa knew his habits.  Under the song, cut shallow into the wax, ran a second text.  She read it the way her mother had taught her: lamp low, needle tracing the grooves, the crank turning slow so the song stayed a murmur. 

Teo had written in two inks, red over blue, so plain sight saw only a smear.  Through amber glass the red vanished and the blue words rose.  Through green glass the blue drowned and a second line surfaced, contradicting the first.  Slaggate called these tinted revelations and trusted them, because nothing true out here ever arrived uncolored.  Under amber Teo had written: I am alive, and I am a road that never moved, one spine and a thousand hands, no water in me.  Under green: Do not send the dog. 

The riddle cost her three nights.  On the fourth she remembered the autumn before the war, when Teo dipped the last leaf of the last orchard tree in candle wax and hung it in the window.  She unwrapped it now, brown and sealed in its cloudy coffin, veins standing up like the fissures of the plain.  One spine, a thousand hands, and she knew the plain's fissures branched exactly so.  It was a map, and the midrib was the only crossing. 

The bloodhound was named Tally, and he had retired from finding bodies.  Nine years of war had taught him one gesture: lie down, muzzle on paws, and the handlers would come with shovels.  Since the peace he slept under the smelter and would take a scent from nobody.  The settlement let him refuse, the way it let all its old instruments rest.  Nessa knelt and held the wax leaf to his nose, because Teo's hands had sealed it, and wax keeps what hands leave.  Alive, she told him, which was a hope she had chosen by choosing her glass. 

The hush broke.  Tally bayed once, and all of Slaggate came out onto its scrap bridges to hear a sound nobody had budgeted for.  They went east along the midrib, three days of cracked clay, his nose low, the leaf warm in her fist.  Wind went through the pipes behind them, and nobody spoke, because a hound working is a kind of prayer. 

At a place she would have called one apology short of home, Tally stopped.  He did not lie down.  He sat, lifted his muzzle, and howled toward a fissure where a man was scraping at seep-water with a spoon.  Teo looked up amber in the evening light, tinted the way every true thing came to her.  "I told you not to send him," he said, hoarse; "he only comes back with graves."  "You wrote that in green," she said, "and I read you in amber."  Then she opened her book and wrote the distance the only way she knew: my brother is one dog's breath away.  Behind them the plain lay hushed and wild, and for the first time since the war it measured nothing.
