"The wheel turned fine," my father said, and then he stopped talking.  That was three hours ago, and the hush he left behind has not broken yet.  My aunt stopped in the middle of a prayer.  The hospice aide stopped writing.  I drove out to the tunnel with the radio on, because I am the kind of woman who leaves. 

Liquidators pay me to appraise dead mills: turbines, looms, brass fittings, whatever the rats left.  I go in alone, without a permit or a helmet, which my father used to call sinning against arithmetic.  I do not believe in ghosts, or in the God my aunt was praying to, or in the candles she lights for men she never met.  I believe in weight, in inventory, in what a thing fetches at auction. 

But for nine years I have been listening to the frantic ones who call the talk-radio psychics after three in the morning.  Madame Verity takes them between ads for mattress warehouses, and she tells them they are carrying an attachment.  I write down what they actually say.  Cold water at the ankles.  A wheel that will not turn.  Somebody counting beads in the dark, and the smell of oranges, which is a smell I know.  Callers who have never met, in towns that have never heard of ours, describing the same shift.  That is not haunting; that is testimony, and nobody is taking it down but me.  Nine years of cross-referencing their dreams gave me a milepost, a smell, and a shift time. 

The mill ran three shifts, and the old train tunnel below it drained through one iron gate into the wheel pit.  In the flood year my father was twenty-two, standing at the gate valve with a lantern and an order from the office.  Open it and the water goes into the mill; keep it shut and the tunnel fills.  Three men were below him splicing a pump line, and he kept it shut, and the water took them.  At the inquiry he said the wheel was seized with rust, and the company wrote it down, and everybody was forgiven.  He lit a candle every year for Teodor, who wound his wife's rosary around the rim for grip and counted it in the dark, waiting for the gate to open. 

I park at the sumac and go in past the trespassing sign, the way I always do.  Sleep sits on me like a coat; I am drowsy and completely awake, the way you are at the exact bottom of the night.  Orange peel, cold ankles, and then, at ten past three, the shift comes on.  No lamps, no faces, just forty men taking up their work, and somewhere ahead of me the slow click of beads being counted.  I do not call it a ghost shift, because naming a thing is how my aunt gets God into a room.  I write it down the way I write down a boiler: shift change, ten past three, forty hands, no lamps. 

Then I find the wheel.  Brass, four spokes, the beads still strung on the rim, glass gone the color of weak tea.  An appraiser learns to hold a thing and let it tell you its weight before you let it tell you its price.  Scrap brass, four pounds, maybe nine dollars.  That is not the weight I came for. 

I put both hands on the rim and turn it, and it turns.  Easy as a door.  Sixty years of rust, and it moves like it was greased last Sunday, and behind me the water starts to go down.  The counting stops.  The shift goes off, all at once, the way a shift does.  I stand in the hush my father made at midnight, holding the thing he swallowed, and it weighs four pounds, and it weighs three men. 

I walk out to the truck, where the radio is still on, and dial the number I have never dialed.  The screener puts me through.  I am not frantic, but I understand now why they all sound that way.  Madame Verity says, "Sweetheart, what are you carrying?"  And I say it for the record, the way nobody said it at the inquiry: the wheel was never seized, my father was twenty-two and frightened, and he chose the mill. 

Madame Verity does not tell me I am carrying an attachment.  She says nothing at all, and the dead air opens up, and I recognize it: the same hush, the one from the hospice room, the one that has been holding me all night.  I do not pray into it; I hold the line and let the silence weigh what I said.
