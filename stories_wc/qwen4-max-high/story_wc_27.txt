The ceasefire was six weeks old, and the rebellion was dying of politeness.  Nobody had signed anything; the orbital guns simply stopped, and our fighters began drifting home to see whether their roofs still held.  I stayed in the repair bay, sorting the wreck.  The bay had been abandoned before the war even started, its cradle arms frozen open around nothing, its roof torn wide enough to show the whole sky.  Ash from the burned lifters drifted in the corners like grey snow.  I catalogued what the fighting left: hull plates, a pilot's chair, my brother's boot.  I was not looking for salvage; I was looking for the shape of a reason.  People called me the stargazer because I slept days and stood nights on the cold deck with my mother's opera glasses pressed to my eyes.  Brass, three-power, older than the colony's theatre, useless for planets and perfect for the wide dark between them.  I went barefoot because boots were currency and because the deck told me things through my soles.  That is how I found the hum.  Nine stones stood through the bay's floor, older than the port, older than the survey that had permitted the port; the builders had poured concrete around them rather than move them.  Every dawn, in the margin of daylight when the sun stayed under the horizon but the sky had begun to grey, the stones let go of the night's cold and sang.  It was below hearing, a pressure in the heels and teeth, thunder you could stand inside without flinching.  It lasted ninety seconds and left the whole bay calm as held breath.  The cradle's induction coils, dead for twenty years, still ringed the stones like fingers around a bell.  I tested it with a scavenged meter: the singing stone drove current through the copper, a thin, steady trickle, gone before the sun cleared.  Not enough to run a lamp.  Enough, maybe, to reignite the beacon.  Our transmitter sat in the wreck pile under a tarp, a thing my brother had died carrying out of the capital, and it had no battery left in the world.  One spark of current would close its relay and let it speak, and every hidden receiver in the valley would answer, and the lull would end on our terms instead of theirs.  I told the council this from the end of their map table, barefoot, because every chair was taken and I could not think how to ask for one.  My voice came out too loud, then too small.  I said the ceasefire was not mercy, it was arithmetic: they were counting us going home.  Commander Idre asked whether I proposed to restart a war with a rock.  I said yes, and did not take it back, and felt my ears burn the whole time.  They gave me nine people and one dawn, mostly to be rid of me.  Outside, Idre caught my sleeve and said his grandmother had called the stones the listening ones, and that he had laughed at her for it.  We spent the day stripping cable from the cradle arms and twisting it into a loop the stones could sing into.  The only clock was a star: when the blue one dropped into the notch of the tallest stone, the hum peaked, and closing the leads early would burn the coil.  Too faint for the naked eye against a brightening sky, but the opera glasses were built for exactly that, for catching a dim singer across a lit house.  At the margin of daylight I lay on my back on the deck, glasses braced on my brow, bare feet flat against the concrete, and called the count.  The star slid into the notch.  The floor took my heels and shook them gently, enormously, and the copper began to drink.  Nothing roared; the guns stayed silent; the whole bay sat in that thunderous calm while Idre held the leads apart and waited for my hand.  I raised it, and he touched them together.  The beacon's relay clicked like a knuckle, and it spoke my brother's call sign into the dark.  Across the valley, one by one, the answers came.  I had spent a year asking the wreckage what my brother's death meant, and it had finally answered: the mess was a circuit, waiting for someone barefoot to close the gap.  By full daylight the hum was gone, and we were loading, quietly, the way you work beside a sleeping child.
