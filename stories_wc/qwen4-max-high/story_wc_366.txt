Marit spoke perhaps forty words a week, and most of them were about weather.  The islanders called her the skipper and let it go at that.  She noticed everything, though.  Summer shoes on the harbormaster's daughter in November.  A new tremor in old Bell's hands when he packed the eggs.  She said nothing, and the next crate held wool socks and a padded corner for the eggs. 

In her oilskin pocket rode a brown envelope, eleven years old.  Inside the flap, in faded ink: Add a line.  Do not sign.  Pass it on.  It went from island to mainland and back, and she was its ferry.  She knew every hand in it, though none had names: the schoolteacher's loops, the widow's slant, the child whose letters grew taller as she grew.  She had never added a line.  Words were cargo she did not know how to land. 

On the wheelhouse shelf stood nine bottles, each holding one small thing.  A coal from her mother's stove.  A button off her father's coat.  She was trying to bottle the feeling of safety, the way other people bottled plums.  The tenth bottle stayed empty.  It belonged to the night the water took her father's boat, and a beam came out of the dark and found her.  She had been nine.  There was nothing left of that night to put in glass.  Only the piece of sea glass she had found afterward, green as a starboard lamp, worn soft as a lozenge.  She could never make herself drop it in. 

Then, on the last crossing of October, the lamp flickered out.  Not a bulb gone.  The whole automated head went dark and stayed dark, and the engineers on the mainland said spring, maybe summer.  One hundred and forty years of light, finished on a Tuesday. 

The islanders would not climb the tower after that.  They heard someone up there: boots on the iron stair, the smell of paraffin, a match struck and struck again.  Elias had kept the lamp for forty years and died in the chair beneath it, and everyone agreed he was still keeping it.  Now the light was out, and the boots would not stop. 

Fog came down that night with the herring boats still outside the sound.  Somebody had to stand between the boats and the rocks.  Marit tied up, took her hand lantern, and climbed. 

The lantern room was cold and enormous, all lens and shadow, the great glass beehive waiting for a flame.  On the floor, at the lens's foot, someone had stacked stones.  Three flat ones, then a leaner set to the left, then one upright.  Her father had taught her that grammar on the slipway: boats out, someone coming home, keep the light.  Ballast stones, carried up one at a time by a man who could not leave his post and could not say what he wanted.  A message with no name on it, waiting sixty years for somebody who could read.  She knew the feeling: a watch nobody had come to relieve. 

She set her lantern in the focus of the lens, and the little flame went out across the water in a long white arm.  Then she rebuilt the stones.  Two flat, one across them: I see you.  One laid pointing down the stair: go home, I have the watch.  In the hollow where the upright stone had stood she left the sea glass, green as a lamp that never needs oil.  The boots went down the stair, unhurried, and did not come back.  She held the flame steady until the last boat found the sound. 

At the landing, Bell asked her who had kept the light.  She said, "The keeper."  Then she took out the envelope and wrote her first line in eleven years: "The light is on, come in."  She did not sign it.  She gave it to the girl with the wool socks, who would give it to someone else. 

The tenth bottle stayed empty, and she stopped trying to fill it.  Safety was not a thing you kept in glass; it was a thing you handed on, in the dark, without your name on it.  On the shelf it caught the harbor lights anyway, and glowed.
