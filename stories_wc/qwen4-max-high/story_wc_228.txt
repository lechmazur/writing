The mirror over his basin cracked in March, when the headland shrugged and a mile of cave shifted beneath the town.  Nothing fell; the glass only held a new line, and Emil began counting his life from it: before, and after.  He painted the quay, which meant he put the same green on the same iron for thirty-one years, and the salt ate it every winter, and every spring he came back. 

Everyone loved him a little and knew him not at all.  He knew whose hands shook before noon, whose boats were mortgaged, which widow took her tea standing at the window.  He mixed his colors to suit their moods and never let anyone walk him home. 

In April the council voted the harbor slate-gray, and the chandlery stopped mixing quay green.  The four tins in his shed were now all of that color there was.  They also gave him a helper: Dalia, nineteen, a surveyor's daughter with a clipboard and no patience for silence. 

The caves ran under the whole town; at low water you could walk from the harbor to the lighthouse without once seeing the sky.  Twice a year Emil went down with a lantern and an unopened tin, and told the yard he was checking the seaward footings.  He was looking for a shell. 

Ana had painted a scallop out of his first bucket of green, the summer they were twenty and hiding from a storm in the cave mouth.  "Now something of yours is mine," she said, and they left it on a ledge to dry, and never found that room again.  The headland was a maze, and the tide had opinions.  She had been dead six years, and he had been searching for six years.  When the mirror cracked and handed him back his face in pieces, he swore to himself, silently, the way you swear to a floor: find it, keep it her green forever, tell no one. 

On the last low tide before the changeover, Dalia followed him down.  He counted galleries out of habit, third left then second left, but the March shrug had rearranged the headland, and the second left was a wrong turn.  It opened.  Fresh water wept from the roof; ferns stood in a shaft of light falling from some crack a hundred feet up; there was moss, and a pool of a color he had never managed to mix.  An oasis inside salt stone, with no salt in it, where nothing weathered.  On a ledge above the water, exactly where she had set it thirty-one years ago, sat the painted shell. 

Dalia did not reach for it.  "You have been looking for that," she said.  "Six years," he said.  Then, because the ferns were listening and the light did not care, he told her all of it: Ana, the bucket, the storm, the cracked mirror, the promise he had never said aloud, not even to the shell.  The vow had two halves, and he was breaking the small one to keep the large. 

It came out wrong and true at once, because the shell had not faded, and that was the trouble.  Nothing in that room had changed since the summer of the storm, and nothing had been renewed either.  He had carried paint down here for six years, and it had never once been needed.  Up on the quay his green died every winter and returned every spring because he carried it back in a bucket.  That was the only forever he had ever actually been given: a forever you had to keep doing.  "Eternity down here," he said, "is only nothing happening." 

He put the shell in his pocket, and left the unopened tin on the ledge.  At the cave mouth he stopped and gave Dalia the recipe: two parts chrome oxide, one part yellow ocher, a thumb of black, linseed, and the order of stirring, which mattered.  Then he took her up the harbor stairs and let her paint the last bollard green, and pressed the shell into the wet paint at its base, where the council's slate would cover it, and the salt would work under the slate and find it. 

"They will paint over it," she said.  "Then we will paint over that," he said.  He said we, and heard himself say it, and did not take it back.  She walked him home.  Above the basin the cracked mirror handed him back in pieces, and he did not mind; he had learned that a thing is kept by being done again.
