The universe hiccuped, and in the skipped beat the temple library came back into the world.  It had fallen out of time so long ago that no calendar claimed it, and its shelves coiled inward through the rock like the whorl of a shell.  Mera was the last reader, which made her the witness at the end of history, a job nobody had applied for.  She had come down through the stacks with a softcover dictionary in her coat, spine cracked, pages swollen from a flood that had happened somewhere else.  She meant to catch the universe at its trick and unpick the seam where the illusion was stitched. 

The hush was not empty.  It rang, the way a struck bell rings after the sound stops, and in that ringing the library asked its question.  Should the beat resume?  Everything that had ever happened was held in the pause, waiting on her verdict, and the only procedure the library knew was spiral arbitration.  The hiccup lasted no time at all, and she would cross it the way she had crossed her life: slowly, on foot, thinking.  A claim had to be spoken once at every turn of the coil, each time more exactly, until it stopped changing.  Whatever survived that narrowing was the truth, and the truth would decide.  The door out stood at the center, so leaving meant going deeper.  She walked out by walking in. 

At the first turn she said, "Let it continue."  The shelves did not argue; they simply waited for the next lap, and she went on down between tablets and scrolls and books that had outlived their languages.  She had been a schoolteacher in a drowned country, and she had kept the dictionary on her desk to settle arguments.  A boy named Idro once asked her what "still" meant, and she had answered wanderingly exact, the way she always answered: circling the word, mentioning the quiet of a room and the persistence of a fever, until she landed on the exact spot where both meanings met. 

At the second turn she said, "Let it be remembered."  That changed under her, so she kept walking.  Memory needed someone to do it, and there would be no one; the pause had already emptied the world of everyone but her. 

At the third turn: "Let it be known."  That failed too, because knowing had always been her private hunger, the reason she had chased every definition down to its roots, wanting to find the one word that did not point at another word.  She had wanted an outside.  An author, a floor, a first cause, something the whole shining spiral could rest on.  There was none, and the dictionary had been telling her so for forty years: every word explained by other words, the circle closed and holding. 

At the fourth turn she stopped arguing with the coil and let it teach her.  The illusion was not that the world existed.  The illusion was that it needed to be justified from somewhere else, that a thing without a floor must fall.  Nothing had ever fallen.  Words held each other up, and so did stars, and so did the boy Idro and the flood and the bell and the hush.  She opened the softcover at the S's, though she did not need to; the pages had gone soft as cloth from her thumb. 

At the fifth turn, at the center, where the door stood and the shelves ended, she said the claim that would not narrow further.  "Still."  Quiet, and also: going on.  She unified them, not by choosing between the two but by admitting they had never been apart.  The library accepted it, because the word did not change when she said it again, or again. 

She went out through the center, which is the only way anyone has ever left anything.  Behind her the hush resounded, enormous and unbroken, the loudest thing she had ever stood inside.  The beat resumed.  History ended, not as a sentence carried out but as a word completing itself.  Nothing fell.  Somewhere in the last of it, a boy was asking, and a teacher was circling the answer, wanderingly exact, and both of them were still.
