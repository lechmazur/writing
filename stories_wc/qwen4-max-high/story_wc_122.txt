The season turned with a faint rustle, and Ada felt it only as a change in the light.  The fever had left the world audibly muted; the music teacher was deaf now, and something in her had gone deaf with the ears.  She could still read a score, still set forty hands to a beat, still watch a child's throat shape a note.  What she could not do was care.  Numbness, the doctor called it, as if it were weather.  Ada wanted one honest feeling back, even a bad one, even grief. 

So she carried a broken phonograph needle up the gorge to the drum maker's cavern.  The needle had snapped the week her hearing went, and she had kept it the way other people keep a lock of hair.  Inside, the cavern breathed wet limestone.  Hides hung in long pale rows, steam curled off the bending forms, and every sound the drum maker made came back to him softened, doubled, and gone.  He called the seam down the middle of the floor a filament of hush.  There the echoes met and cancelled, a thread of quiet spun straight through the noise, thin as a hair and just as strong.  He hung his best hides along it so they would cure without rattling.  Ada stood in it and felt the floor stop trembling under her boots.  It was the first silence that had ever matched her own, and she nearly wept, and did not. 

She laid the broken needle on his bench and wrote her order on his slate.  Mount it in wood, so the shank can touch a drumhead.  Then cut me the children's songs, and run them backward.  The break had left a blunt stub that would never sing again, but it could still shiver.  The drum maker had a wax cylinder machine he used to keep the old tuning chants from dying.  He reversed the mandrel with a crossed belt, the way he reversed a hide to flesh both sides.  Backward recorded songs, he wrote, grinning, are the same songs, only they arrive before they leave. 

Ada had a plainer reason.  Forward, a song struck her palm as one blunt shove.  Backward, every note began out of nothing, swelled, and ended on a click she could count.  Reversal gave the music edges, and edges were something a hand could hold.  They set the needle's block on a cured hide stretched over a shallow shell, and Ada laid both palms flat.  The cylinder turned the wrong way.  Her own school's hymn came apart and reassembled inside her wrists.  She knew the shape of it, the long climb where the altos lifted, and she found that climb running down into her bones, and it did not move her.  Nothing yet. 

So she brought the children.  They came up the gorge on the last warm afternoon, while the leaves let go in the faint rustle that marks the hinge of the year.  There were twenty of them, hands behind backs, afraid of the dark and of the drums.  She sat them along the filament of hush and gave each pair of hands a hide.  Then she started the backward songs.  She had meant to teach them; she had not meant to enthrall them.  Mouths opened; the fidgeting stopped; a boy pressed his cheek to the skin of the drum to get closer.  Their faces did what faces do at the good part of a song.  Ada watched the girl who never sang, the one with the flat voice and the shut face, and saw her eyes go wide and wet. 

Something in Ada's chest tore quietly, like a seam giving.  Grief came first, for the hearing, for the nine flat months, for every lesson she had taught like a woman mailing letters from a dead town.  Then joy came behind it, the way the reversed notes came, swelling out of nothing.  She had wanted one feeling and got two, and they mirrored each other, and the hush in the cavern mirrored the hush in her, and none of it was empty.  The drum maker wiped his hands and said nothing, which was the correct thing to say. 

Ada kept the broken needle.  All winter the children climbed to the cavern, the songs ran backward, and the season turned again with its faint rustle, and she felt the hinge.  She could not hear them.  She could feel them arrive before they left.
