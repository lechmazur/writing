The shrine, cut deep into the glacier, had nine windows, and none of them looked outside.  They were panes of ice polished thin as communion wafers, and behind each one lay the black water where the ferry Vestri had gone down.  Candles stood in niches carved along the walls, and their light went into the ice and came back changed, the way starlight comes back off snow.  Every candle there had been lit from another candle, a chain of borrowed flame reaching back to the night the ship went down.  Nothing in that room made its own light.  Ilse had learned to work by borrowed brightness.  She was the last wreck-diver on the coast, and the only one who came up carrying voices.  She whispered to the drowned through the windows, and they answered in pieces: a syllable, a pressure, the smell of engine oil.  It was Ilse's work to weave those pieces into something a mother could carry home without falling down.  She wove carefully.  She took the terror out and left the love in, the way you lift splinters from a palm.  In eleven years she had never handed over one whole true thing.  That was the ache that kept her diving: somewhere under all that ruined steel there had to be something pure, and she wanted to bring it up intact.  On the candle shelf sat her one keepsake, a child's snow globe from the purser's cabin.  Years of small flames had melted it; the glass slumped like a held breath, the water gone, the tiny white ferry inside fused to a lump of glitter.  She had meant to give it back whole to his daughter, and had made it worse by keeping it warm.  Sund, who owned the line, paid for the candles.  His brass plaque named the purser, Eirik Vold, as the man who dogged the aft hatch and drowned the passengers behind it.  Now Sund wanted the shrine sealed, the grief finished, the ice left alone.  But the dead cannot enter the dreams of people who loved them; grief shuts that window from the inside.  So they go sideways, cameo appearances in the dreams of strangers, a face in the crowd of someone else's night.  The shrine kept a dream ledger for exactly this, and Ilse read it every morning by ice-light.  Comparing dates and drawings, she found that seventeen pilgrims who had never met Eirik Vold had drawn the same door.  In every sketch a man knelt at it with his fingers broken, scraping.  He was not shutting it.  Nailed to the steel above him, in seventeen unrelated hands, hung the same square of paper with the same three words and the same crest.  Ilse went down under the glacier with a lamp and a pry bar.  The Vestri hung in the black like a thought nobody would finish.  At the aft hatch she found the oilskin packet, still nailed, still legible: CARGO BEFORE PASSENGERS, and Sund's signature beneath the crest.  Below it, wedged in the coaming, lay the bones of the hands that had tried to undo the order.  She surfaced with the paper against her skin.  That evening the shrine was full: mothers, Eirik's grown daughter, Sund in his good coat, the candles doing their small honest work.  Ilse did not weave.  She read the seventeen dreams aloud, one after another, until the strangers' drawings, held up together, made a single door in the air.  Then she pressed the oilskin flat against the ninth window, where the ice was thinnest, and let the candlelight come through the paper from behind.  Sund's signature stood out black, and behind it the water moved.  The room made a sound Ilse had spent eleven years protecting people from.  She let it happen.  Into that sound came Eirik's voice, unwoven, straight through the ice: I could not get it open, I stayed, tell them I stayed.  It was not comfort.  It was pure, and it was his, and she had brought it up whole.  Sund left before the candles guttered; the mothers stayed, and one of them asked to hear the sentence again exactly as it came.  Afterward Ilse took the melted snow globe down from the warm shelf and put it in the daughter's hands.  She had kept it warm until it lost its shape, the way she had kept the truth warm until it lost its edges, and now she let it be exactly what it was.  Around her the shrine burned on borrowed light, one flame handed to the next, the dead lending the living enough to see by.  Nothing in the shrine made its own light, and yet everything in it could finally be seen.
