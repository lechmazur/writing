Every spring the town sent its children down to laugh in the crystal cave, and every spring Vesna waited at the back with her charcoal-black fingers folded out of sight.  The ritual was older than the accident: laughter first, so the cave would remember something besides grief.  Vesna remembered everything else.  She had been nineteen, the guide, the one who lifted her lamp and showed twelve children how the selenite needles broke the light into slow green curtains, an aurora folded underground.  She could still draw each face without hesitating, could place the exact crooked tooth in the exact crooked smile, because her memory kept everything and forgave nothing.  The town's plaque said eleven.  The twelfth had been a miner's boy who followed the group in without a ticket, without a name on any roster, and afterward without a family willing to claim him.  For twenty years she had earned her bread sketching the eleven for widows and grandparents, charcoal portraits, faithful copies of a perfect record.  Never color.  In her satchel, wrapped in a rag, stood the bottle her old teacher had corked for her: ground malachite in oil, green as the cave's cold sky, saved for the day she stopped copying and made something.  The cork had swollen shut with waiting.  The children filed up the shaft, the laughter went thin, and then the last echo of it faded off the crystal, and the cave gave back only the drip of water.  That silence was hers; she had waited a year for it, and she was the only one in town who ever asked to stay behind.  She walked to the steel door of the collapsed gallery and spun the combination lock, though she knew the numbers the way she knew the faces.  Nineteen, four, thirty-one.  The dial turned loose and dead in her hand, its tumblers ruined by twenty winters of seeping lime, and no amount of remembering could open it.  That was the joke she lived inside: a flawless memory and a broken lock.  She had petitioned the council twice to rectify the count, and twice they had asked her for proof, and she had offered the only thing she owned, which was recall.  A woman who remembers is not evidence, the clerk had said, kindly.  In her pocket she carried what she had fished through the gap under that door twenty years ago: a brass lamp tag stamped twelve, and only eleven lamps had ever been signed out to the group.  A number was not a boy.  They would file the tag and forget it, unless someone made them see him.  So she set the board on her knees, the panel she had gessoed and hidden and carried for years, and worked the cork free with her teeth.  The smell of the oil came up like a room opened after a funeral.  Her memory gave her his face for nothing: the flat nose, the eyelashes pale with rock dust, the mouth open at the light.  But memory had never stored the color of that light, and the green had to be invented, and inventing was the thing she had spent twenty years not doing.  She painted the curtains of light anyway, badly, then less badly, her charcoal fingers smearing green into the seams of the crystals.  Under his face she wrote the promise she had broken: tomorrow, I will paint it for you.  When the oil was tacky she turned the board over and wired the brass tag to the hanging cleat, twelve against the wood, hidden where only a curious hand would find it.  She hung it over the ruined lock, on the door that would not open, and beneath it she pinned the corrected list: eleven names, and one blank line where a face would have to do for a name.  When the council lifted the painting to check her arithmetic, the tag would drop into their palms, and the count would be rectified by something no one could call a memory.  She could no more stop making faces than the water could stop finding the low places.  That was the calling: not talent, not choice, only a debt that kept arriving.  Her lamp threw the aurora across the floor, green and rose and cold, and she sat in it with her wet green hands and let herself be sorry out loud.  Next spring the children would come down and laugh, and she would wait for the silence, and she would bring more paint.
