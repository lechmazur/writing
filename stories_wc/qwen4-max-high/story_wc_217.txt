The kite string snapped with a sound like a plucked harp wire, and the whole lagoon seemed to hold its breath.  Rain had just quit the coast, and the water below the spit had gone cobalt, that deep blue the lagoon only wore when the sky was scrubbed clean.  Maren heard the snap from her stool at the weaving frames, where she had been sitting since first light.  She was the shoreline poet, though she had never once read aloud.  Her poems were woven.  Every morning, whoever woke first in the village came down and worked a hand's width of the dawn into the long cloth: the colors of the water, the weather, the mood of the boats.  Four generations of woven dawns hung along the shore, and Maren was the one who tended them.  Tending did not mean correcting.  When a grieving widow wove black into a rose morning, or a boy jammed in a green that fought everything beside it, Maren never pulled the thread out.  She wove around the discord, easing neighboring colors toward it until the clash became a chord.  Her tool was a drake's tail feather, molted last autumn, its left vane split so that half the barbs stood free.  She used the quill to lift warp threads and the broken vane to comb them straight, and she had refused three offers of a proper brass hook.  A whole feather could only smooth; the split one could reach into a tangle and separate it without breaking anything.  Her mother had begun the dawn of the eleventh of March and died before the sun cleared the headland, leaving six inches of bare warp.  The elders had wanted to finish it for her, neatly, so the record would not limp.  Maren had asked them to let it stand, and had only bound the loose ends so nothing else unraveled.  Visitors called it a flaw; she called it the truest dawn on the shore, because it admitted that a morning can be interrupted.  She had thought all of this in the time the kite took to fall.  It came down across the newest band, today's, which the rain had cut short at four inches, and its splintered crossbar snagged the warp and dragged.  By the time Maren reached the frame, three threads had parted and the cobalt was bleeding open.  The boy arrived a step behind her, soaked and shaking, the dead string still wound around his fist.  "I ruined it," he said.  Maren knelt in the wet sand so their eyes were level, and she waited, warmly attentive, while he explained about the kite, his uncle in the north who had made it, the two months he had saved for the string.  She did not tell him it was only cloth.  She asked what color the kite had been at its highest, and he said red, almost orange, and she nodded as if he had handed her something valuable.  Then she took the snapped string from his hand and held it against the torn band.  "Hold this end," she said, which were the first words she had spoken all morning.  She lifted the broken warp with the drake's feather, combed the snarls apart with its split vane, and laid the kite string into the gap.  The red fought the cobalt at first, hard and ugly.  So she did what she always did: she left it alone and worked around it, threading a rust from the wet sand, then a thin copper from the light on the water, until the red had neighbors and the clash settled into a chord.  She stopped short of the frame's edge, leaving two inches of warp bare, and bound the ends.  "It isn't finished," the boy said.  "No," she said.  "The rain stopped it, and then you did, and now it says so."  Out on the lagoon the drake was paddling in circles, his tail full of gaps where the molt had taken the feathers, preening around the holes as though they belonged.  The boy watched the bird, then the cloth, and something in his face let go.  Later, in the small book she kept for anyone who could not read cloth, she wrote the day's title, four words long: The String Let Go.  The light went burnished and quiet, the way it does after rain, and the two of them sat in that hush while the lagoon held the whole broken morning without trying to fix it.
