Every night for nine hundred years, the herald climbed the emerald lighthouse and laughed the storm in.  It was not a metaphor.  Her laugh was the lamp.  Three notes, one for the far water, one for the near, one for herself, and the flame caught in the glass lantern wick, a thread of drawn silica coiled like a sleeping worm.  Light entered the wick and could not leave it.  Total internal reflection, the keeper's manual called it, back when there were manuals and keepers and pay.  The light ran the coil in perfect silence, turning every corner without spilling, until she scratched the glass with a diamond point and let it out exactly where she wanted a ship to look.  That was how she steered: not with a sweeping beam, but with a bright stitch sewn into the dark, one leak at a time.  Under the stars the tower stood green as a bottle pulled from a wreck, and the sea beneath it was black and patient.  The routine had never varied, because she was not a woman but a function the coast had grown, the way a reef grows.  Laugh, trim, steer, sleep, laugh. 

Then came the last print newspaper.  It was pinned beside the lamp still, gone soft as cloth, and on its weather page some bored editor had run her joke.  She had told the harbor boy, laughing, that on the ninth of the month the sea would come up the church steps looking for its mother.  She had meant nothing.  She was a herald; her laughter went out ahead of the weather, and sometimes the weather listened too closely.  On the ninth, the water climbed the steps and stood at the font, and eleven people who had gone there to pray were taken.  The presses stopped that winter, and no one printed anything again.  She had been prophetic by accident, and now there was no record left to catch her at it.  Nothing would remember the eleven but the green glass. 

The tower kept them.  Every storm she had ever called had left its shadow in the panes, and the panes had drunk it, and nine centuries lay stacked in the emerald like silt in a bottle.  She had learned to read the strata: the drowned mast here, the winter of the ice bells there, the church steps near the top, still wet. 

Tonight she did not laugh the storm in.  Tonight she meant to make a dawn.  Not the old sun, which rose the same way over the drowned as over the dry, forgiving nothing because it noticed nothing.  She wanted a morning cut from the shadows themselves, so that the coast would wake already knowing what it had cost. 

The method was the same method.  She took the diamond and scratched the wick, not outward toward the shipping lane, but down and inward, at an angle steeper than any keeper's manual allowed.  Then she laughed.  It was the worst sound she had ever made, and it was still laughter, and the flame ran into the glass and obeyed.  Trapped, turning, spilling nowhere, the light went down the coil until it reached the wound she had opened, and there it broke the critical angle and poured out into the emerald wall.  It went through nine hundred years of shadow.  She steered it with her hands flat on the panes, tilting the beam a degree at a time through the strata, mast and ice bell and church step, and the light came out the far side carrying them. 

Below, the black water took the color and gave it back.  The horizon did not brighten so much as remember.  A green dawn came up the sky with eleven dark shapes walking in it, and a mast, and the small bright bells of a winter nobody alive had seen.  In the harbor, doors opened.  People came out in their nightclothes and looked up and said names, and the names were right. 

No press would ever print it.  It would be told wrong within a week and sung wrong within a year, and that was allowed; a coast can carry its own record if it is given one worth carrying.  The herald unpinned the newspaper, folded it, and put it in the lamp housing where the heat would take it slowly.  Then she leaned on the rail and laughed the old three notes, because the ships still needed steering and the storm was still hers to call.  Under the stars she was delighted and unabsolved, and she steered her new dawn up the sky.
