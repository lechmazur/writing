The silo at the edge of town was tiled in bottle glass, emerald from footing to cap, and the town hung its bell inside when the works closed.  Marta rang it, as her father had before her, a timekeeper running on borrowed time.  The heart in her was a loan; the surgeon had named a number, and then the numbers changed.  After the Reassignment, a numeral meant a place in line, not an amount, so her prognosis became third instead of three years, and the clock faces became queues.  The town went suddenly timeless: nobody could say how long a thing had lasted, only how it had felt.  So Marta kept time by texture, ringing when the bread cooled, when the light thickened, when the swifts came back.  She wanted to learn it properly, the grain of an hour, the difference between a summer and a waiting room.  That was why she went down into the silo floor for the capsule the town had sealed the week the river rose.  Everyone had forgotten it, which is the point of a capsule; you cannot rediscover what you never let go.  She loved the hour before opening anything.  The flood had found it anyway; the seam had split, and the ledger listing who gave what had gone to gray pulp.  The objects survived; their stories did not.  A thimble, a harmonica, a photograph of a dog, a shard of green-glazed pottery with a hairline crack.  Marta knew the glaze: her mother's urn, which broke on the kitchen floor that same week.  Her father must have swept up a piece and sealed it in with the town's other leavings, and never told her.  She held the fragment and waited for grief to arrive, the way you wait at a door you have knocked on.  What came instead was a question: who else had knelt here, and what had they hoped the future would feel like?  The pulp could not answer, so she asked the town sideways.  The objects were the only addresses left.  She held a garage sale on the silo's apron and wrote the descriptions herself.  Lot fourth: harmonica, water-stained, from the capsule under the green silo; if you played it, come tell me what that summer sounded like.  Lot ninth: thimble, brass, dented; describe the mending to me and it is yours for nothing.  She was not selling; she was fishing.  They came all week, because everyone loves a mystery that has been waiting for them personally.  The harmonica belonged to a man who had practiced in the silo's echo to hide the wrong notes from his brothers.  The thimble's owner remembered a whole winter of mending as a single warm hour, and said the waiting afterward had lasted longer than the war.  Marta wrote it all down: not dates, which meant nothing now, but grain, weight, temperature.  She was learning the texture she had wanted, and it was made entirely of other people's patience.  Last of all she listed the fragment.  Green shard, hairline crack, cold in the hand; it was my mother; if you were in my kitchen the week the river rose, tell me what he said.  On the last evening an old woman came up the road holding the printed card like a summons.  She had held the dustpan.  "Your father stood a long while with that piece in his palm," she said.  "Then he said, this much of her still has a future, and he carried it out here and sealed it in."  Something of a stranger was keeping her own future going, too.  Marta turned the shard over and felt the crack under her thumb, rough as a seam in cloth.  There it was: the texture, not a number of years but a weight passed hand to hand, still warm from the last one.  She did not ring the bell that evening; the town would know the hour by the green going dark.  Instead she found an unbroken jar and put the shard in it, with a card describing the harmonica summer, the mending winter, and the dustpan woman's words.  She sealed it under the silo floor with the seam soldered properly this time, and wrote no date, because dates meant nothing.  She wrote: whoever opens this, you are holding what is left of somebody; take your time.  Then she climbed up into the emerald dark and sat with the pleasure of a thing not yet found, waiting for whoever would need it.
