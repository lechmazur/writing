The arches over the orchard world of Vessaway were blue as a whetted blade, and they floated because they were awake.  Odo Pell, the meek smith who apologized to his own anvil, read the magician's ledger on a Tuesday and understood.  Their waking was borrowed, renewed each dawn from someone below, and that someone rose fogged, half-knowing, forever after.  His wife Meri had sold hers eleven years ago, when the great arch called the Kettle listed over their pear terraces.  Since then she sat in the doorway and almost knew him.  Now the Kettle was listing again, and the guild had come to ask for their daughter's mornings.  Odo said no, softly, twice, and then went to find the magician.  He did not want another loan against a child; he wanted stone that could hold its own weight.  He wanted resilience coaxed out of rubble.  The magician bought his mornings and paid in azure silk, the cloth that carries waking, and Odo carried forty years of dawn home over his shoulder.  Every thread he spent would cost him a little understanding, and at the end of the spending lay confusion.  He had that narrow country between the two to work in, so he worked deliberately.  He chalked his plan on the forge wall in letters a fogged man could read: SHAFT SEVEN, WRAP THE RUBBLE, LET GO.  Beneath the orchards lay the abandoned mine shafts where the arches had been cut out, and the rubble of their cutting still lay at the seams, blue-veined, kin to the sky.  Awake stone rises; that was the whole of the magic, and the whole of the mistake.  But rubble at the bottom of a shaft has the whole world above it; woken, it cannot rise, so it must do the other thing waking allows.  It can hold.  It can bear.  He tested it for nine days with chips no bigger than a plum, wrapping each in a thread of the silk and a minute of dawn.  The chips did not float; they knitted, edge to edge, into a slab that took his hammer without cracking.  Each test cost him a word, then a name, then the reason he had gone down.  He wrote those losses on the wall too, so the man he was becoming would not be surprised.  On the thirteenth day the guild came again with a contract for the girl, and Odo, who had never raised his voice, set his blue-hot iron on the paper and let it burn.  He apologized for the mess, and did not move the iron.  That was the shape of his defiance: quiet, azure, at forging heat.  On the fourteenth day he could still read the wall, barely, and he went down shaft seven with the silk over his shoulder and his daughter guiding the lamp.  At the seam he spread the azure cloth over the rubble of the Kettle's own cutting, the mother-stone, still tied by every vein to the arch above.  He put his hands flat and gave it every dawn left in the cloth.  The rubble woke.  It could not rise, so it held; it drew itself tight under the mountain's weight, the veins went bright, and far above, the Kettle stopped needing to borrow.  The arch came down two feet onto its own awakened root and stood.  Every waking it had ever bartered for came loose at once, like pears in a wind.  In the doorway at home, Meri stood up and knew her husband's name, and could not find him.  They found Odo at the seam, sitting in folds of grey silk, smiling, fogged, his hands still flat on stone that would not let go.  The silk had given up its blue; the rubble had it now, a low azure pulse under the orchards, holding.  Meri chalked new letters above his old ones, and every dawn she brought him down shaft seven to lay his palms on the warm stone.  The rooted stone did what the floating arches never did: it paid back.  A minute at a time, the way it had taken them.  His daughter took the forge, badly at first, then well, and the pears were picked before they fell.  By the first frost he could say the word rubble, and he said it like a man naming a friend.
