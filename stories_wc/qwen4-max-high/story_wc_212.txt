The atrium had frozen in the shape of its own falling water.  Ice held the ferns mid-bow, and through the shattered glass roof the stars came down in cold, unhurried columns.  Vera knelt among the seed jars with her grandmother's acorn cap in her palm.  Three hours ago the egg had hatched.  It had sat in the vault for ninety years, labeled in her great-grandmother's hand: OAK, ON LOAN.  Her grandmother's notes said that a loan kept cold and ordered long enough would curl itself into an egg.  Now the shell lay in two neat halves, and above it the air was turning. 

Vera was a seed saver, the fourth of her line, and she knew what the turning was.  Her family did not inherit eyes or tempers; they inherited a spin.  Her grandmother had called it vortex inheritance: time curling down the generations, each woman borrowing hours from the ones not yet born.  Vera had been born blue and silent, and had lived.  Her mother had gone at thirty-one, smiling, unbothered, her debt called in early.  Vera had spent her life saving seeds to learn one thing: the texture of borrowed time.  Whether it was gritty like a seed coat, or slick like the ice on the ferns, or simply cold. 

The vortex above the shell was the color of nothing, and it pulled at her wrists.  To read it she had to reopen the spiral, and the spiral would not be forced.  The notes were plain about the method: you open a lineage by decreasing entropy.  Not heat, not violence; order.  A spiral is held shut by its own chaos; strip the chaos out and it has nothing to grip. 

So Vera worked.  She sorted the jars by species, then by year, then by the field each seed had come from, until the shelves ran in perfect gradients.  Beans named for a trail, a tomato carried here in a hem, oats that had outlived three famines.  She swept the frost into one white drift and packed it against the broken door.  She opened the vents to the starlight and let the atrium go colder, and colder, until her breath fell as dust.  She grew furiously tranquil, the way a river is furious under ice.  Every jar in its place, every degree surrendered, the disorder draining out of the room like water out of a cracked bowl. 

The vortex slowed, unwound, and opened.  Inside it she saw the loan.  She saw her great-grandmother kneeling in a winter field, pressing an acorn into ground that would not take it.  The nut rotted; the cap stayed.  And the hours the oak never spent went into the family instead, spinning, unspent, borrowed forward and backward at once.  That was the inheritance: a tree's unlived time, wearing human shapes. 

Vera reached into the turning with the acorn cap.  It was the socket the loan had come out of, and the only thing in the atrium that could hold it.  The hatchling was still there, a pale coil of green no longer than a finger, hunting for somewhere to be.  She set it in the cap.  Then she gave the hours back.  It did not hurt; it was like exhaling a word she had been holding since birth.  She felt them go out of her wrists and knees, the extra winters, the blue silent hour that had been paid for by a daughter she would never have. 

And in the going she finally touched it.  Borrowed time was scurfy, crosshatched, warm on the wrong side; it felt exactly like the inside of an acorn cap, the velvet socket where a nut once sat.  Not cold at all.  That was the joke her family had died without learning. 

The coil in the cap straightened, put down a root the color of a star, and began, very slowly, to spend.  Vera sat back on the frozen tiles, lighter than she had ever been, and had perhaps an hour of her own.  She used it watching the oak take its first minute under the starlight, in an atrium so ordered and so cold that nothing in it could decay.  The wind went wild above the broken roof, and she let it; her hands were empty, and steady, and hers.
