Rui said, You called me by your sister's name yesterday, and then she stopped talking.  The pause that followed was the country Wen knew best.  She had spent forty years chronicling a tidal pool in the grotto below the cliff road, and every entry she ever made belonged to a between.  The roof had fallen in nine centuries ago, so the stars came down into the water and lay there, wet and slightly wrong.  Monks had cut the steps; their lamp niches still held soot.  Now it was slack water, neither flood nor ebb, the sea holding its breath the way Rui was holding hers. 

Wen noticed that her daughter had come down the wet steps in city shoes, and that she had not once looked at the pool.  She loved her for coming anyway. 

Wen had never owned a clock.  She measured time sideways, by what it did to other things: the barnacle rings on the old mooring stone, the depth of silt over a limpet she had marked in a spring when Rui was small.  Direct questions slid off the years; the tangential ones stuck.  What she wanted, under all the counting, was the texture of time, its grain, whether it ran smooth like jade or ribbed like the roof of a mouth. 

In her coat pocket lay the jade cicada her grandmother had been buried with, a tongue stone, dug up in a bad year and sold twice before it came home.  The old people laid a cicada on the tongue of the dead because the insect waits years in the dark and then climbs out of its own skin singing.  Its wing lines had been worn shallow by three generations of thumbs, which was another kind of ledger.  Wen used it as a thermometer for silences.  Cold jade warms in the palm at a rate you can learn, and she had calibrated hers against the tide.  She closed her hand on the cicada in her pocket, at the beginning of the pause, and felt the stone take her heat. 

From the ledgers, sideways as always, she could foresee the rest.  Not the dates, never the dates, but the shape: entries thinning, the handwriting leaning, a spring when she would record the same anemone twice because it felt new.  She had seen it in her mother, and in the pool, which lost its old residents every winter and took in strangers each spring.  Above, the star her grandmother had called the Ferryman slid toward the notch in the cliff, and Wen knew the water would begin to climb in eleven minutes. 

Rui's jaw was set the way it had been at seven, waiting out a scolding, and Wen wanted to put a hand on it.  Instead she did what she always did with a hard thing: came at it from the side. 

Hold out your hand, she said.  Rui, who had come prepared for arguments about the flat in town and the stairs, took the cicada instead. 

Cold, said Rui. 

Wait, said Wen.  That is the whole of it.  Nothing in the pool is ever only itself; the anemone is a mouth and a garden, the hermit crab is a tenant in a dead snail's house, and this stone is a bug and a grandmother and a minute.  Between the cold and the warm there is a country, and I have lived in it forty years, and I am asking you to visit. 

Rui's fingers curled.  It is warming, she said, surprised, as if she had caught something. 

That is time, Wen said.  Not a line.  A grain.  You can feel it going through you.  And I am going to lose the ledger, Rui, and I would rather hand it over than have it taken. 

She had foreseen this too: that Rui would not say yes, but would ask a question.  The pause ended there, on the far side, where she had known it would.  Rui asked what the white scars on the rock were, and Wen said barnacles, and told her how to count the rings. 

The water began to climb.  Eleven minutes, give or take the width of a star.  Wen opened the ledger on her knee and wrote the hour the only way she knew how: slack water, stars in the pool, my daughter's hand warming.  It was not a measurement, but it was true, and it would keep.
