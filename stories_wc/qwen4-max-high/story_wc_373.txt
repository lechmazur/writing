The pirate market under Cormorant Pier opened at slack water, when the tide stopped talking and the rotting boards overhead quit their creaking.  Lanterns hung in the piling shadows, and women sold jars of white lightning, oysters, gasoline, and forgiveness, all in whispers.  My father had run shine through these channels for twenty years, and I had inherited his stall, his debts, and his rules.  The chief rule was the truce of hush: no names under the pier until sunrise, and no voice raised above the water.  After sunrise the market was only rot and pilings, and a name was only a word.  Before sunrise, a name spoken by the holder of the fob was a sentence, and the market carried it out.  The fob was mine now: an antique brass thing worn smooth, a hare stamped on one face, its watch long gone, its clasp still snapping shut with a small dry click.  Corley wanted it, and he had a revenue man dozing in his skiff to make the wanting official.  He offered to wipe my father's debt and pay a doctor for the leg my father broke the night Corley talked.  All he needed from me was the fob in his palm and my father's name in the air before dawn.  I had not come for the money or the doctor; I had come for the sound of trust breaking, which I had never heard, though I had heard everything it leaves behind.  I had brought my cassette recorder, which nobody minded, since it had only ever carried home water and whispers.  I had taped my mother's kettle, the gulls, and the empty room after the raid.  I thought that if I could hold that one sound in my hand, I would know it coming next time.  Corley sat on a crate with his knees apart, patient as a stove.  Somebody handed us chicory coffee in jelly jars, and the woman beside us quilted a tarp against the damp, smiling at me the way you smile at a child you have decided to let win.  He could not snatch it; a taken fob broke the hush, and the market answered that with hands.  I set the recorder on the crate and pressed record.  "Wait," I said. "The tide's over it."  I stopped the tape, moved the machine six inches, and started again.  Corley laughed low and told me to take my time; he had until sunrise too, and thought the night belonged to him.  "Levels," I said, and stopped it again.  The truth was I had begun to hear what I was buying.  My father was asleep in the marsh under a tarp, trusting the hush to hold his name in place like a nail.  Corley would say it, the market would honor the fob, and the man in the skiff would row home with my father in his mouth.  They called me curiously steadfast, meaning I did not run, meaning I could stand in one place all night if the place needed standing in.  So I thwarted him through false starts.  The battery was cold; I warmed it under my arm.  A gull screamed on the far piling and I insisted on waiting it out.  I asked him to say the name once for level, empty-handed, the fob still in my fist, and then complained that the clasp rattled.  He said it, and my father's name went into the tape and did not count, and something in my chest tore quietly.  Each time he reached for the fob I found one more thing wrong, and each time he settled back, because the night was long and I was only a girl with a machine.  The quilt woman poured me more coffee, and her eyes said she had counted my starts and knew the sum.  The hush held around us like a hand over a candle.  Then the east went gray, the tide turned, and the market folded itself into the water without a word.  Corley stood up in the light with his palm open, and the fob in it would have been only brass.  "Keep it," he said, and the sound of trust breaking was two words, flat and tired, recorded clean.  I had made that sound myself.  He walked out along the sand to his skiff, and the revenue man rowed him away with nothing to swear to.  I went to my father through the marsh with the fob in my pocket and the tape of a night that never quite started.  He still needs that doctor, and Corley will want the fob at the next slack water, and I will be there, pressing record and stopping it, until the light comes.
