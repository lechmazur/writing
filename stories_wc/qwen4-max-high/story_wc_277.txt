After the journalist called, Ilse Rennick climbed to the dome and let the echo finish her sentences.  The observatory stood above the cloud sea, nine thousand feet up, where the air was thin enough to make honesty feel like altitude sickness.  Below, the clouds lay flat and silver as a fresh sheet of newsprint, which was the family joke and the family fortune.  Her father had built the dome for her mother, then ruined her mother in print, and Ilse had inherited the telescope, the presses, and the mills that fed them.  The journalist wanted a quote for the nineteenth anniversary of the retraction.  Nineteen years, exactly the period of the comet her mother was accused of inventing.  Ilse had learned that regret keeps an orbit: it swings out into the dark, goes quiet, and returns on schedule, sweeping fastest when it passes nearest the thing it cannot touch.  She was, her mother used to say, devotedly observant, which was a compliment in astronomy and a curse in a family.  So she had observed.  For nineteen years she had logged the dome's temperature, the shutter's groan, the exact hour the ink froze, and every word her father ever printed about the woman who taught her to look.  Tonight she opened the safe where the retraction lived.  One page, signed in her mother's hand: I fabricated the plates; the object does not exist.  Ilse had believed it the way one believes weather.  But belief is not knowledge, and she had spent nineteen years wanting to know.  Now, with the comet due back before dawn, she decided to test it properly, the way her mother had taught her: assume the thing is true and follow it until it breaks.  Assume the retraction was genuine.  Then her mother wrote it.  Her mother wrote everything with a nib she had whittled from rosewood during a winter when the metal ones froze to her fingers.  The rosewood nib had a split left tine, and it laid every stroke twice: a bold line and a faint twin beside it, like a body and its shadow.  Ilse had checked a hundred letters, grocery lists, ephemerides; the doubling never failed.  She put the loupe to the signature.  One line.  Clean, single, steel.  The echo in the dome seemed to step back.  If the retraction were genuine, it would carry the doubled stroke; it did not; therefore it was not genuine, and her mother had never confessed to anything.  Her father had needed a scandal that year more than he had needed a wife; the mills were failing, and ruin sells.  Her mother had gone down the mountain that spring, and the clouds had closed over her like a page.  Somewhere below the clouds, a printing plant was rolling paper made from trees her family owned, and the presses were warming for the anniversary piece.  The journalist had called her the newsprint heiress, kindly, as if the word were a compliment and not an invoice.  He had said, everyone knows the story, Miss Rennick, and she had said, everyone knows the headline.  She went down to the wire room off the dome and called the night editor.  Stop the front page, she said.  He explained, patiently, about deadlines, contracts, and the cost of a stopped run.  She told him she would pay the cost out of the dividend that the lie had bought her, and that she was coming down the mountain with the plate, the log, and the page.  Then she went back up, because the comet was still due, and a correction is not proof until the sky agrees.  At four in the morning the shutter groaned open on a black gash of sky.  The cloud sea lay still beneath her, and the echo of the dome held its breath.  There it was, nineteen years older and exactly on time: a smudge of light where her mother's arithmetic had promised it.  Ilse warmed the ink against her wrist, dipped the rosewood nib, and wrote the correction in her mother's hand, which was also her own.  Every letter came out twice, a statement and its shadow.  It was, she thought, the correct instrument for a family apology: it could not write anything without admitting there had been another line all along.  She signed it, then added, playfully, at the bottom, the only joke she had left: The object exists; the objector regrets.  Regret would keep its orbit; she could not cancel it, only stop feeding it lies.  Below, the clouds began to brighten, sheet after sheet, ready for print.
