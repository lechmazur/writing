The forty-first morning after Nadia died, I pawned my drawplate and kept only her hair.  Mourning jewelry is honest work: you braid the dead into a rose that will never rot, and a widow pins it over her heart.  I had made three hundred such roses, and then I could not make one for myself. 

The cranes started in November.  Paper birds on my doorstep, folded from something stiffer than paper, translucent, faintly greasy at the creases.  I swept the first two into the gutter.  The third I opened, because a bird is a thing that expects to be opened.  Inside, in Wren's small square hand, were coordinates. 

Wren had been my apprentice until the shop closed, and now she tattooed the bereaved with ink cut from their own ashes.  The numbers pointed to the observatory above the reservoir, a building I had walked past for eleven years without seeing.  Its clock had no hands.  Inside, Wren said, the hours were kept but not spent.  Nothing there aged; the dust never thickened; the tea in the pot stayed at the temperature it had been poured.  She had found it the year her mother died, and had been coming back ever since. 

She did not scold me for the two in the gutter.  She said knocking had stopped working in September.  She led me up the iron stair to the dome and put her hand on the lamp housing. 

The observatory did not look at stars.  It looked at the city's intentions, which hung in the dark above the roofs like curtains: long veils of light, each one a thing somebody meant to do.  Most were bright and slow.  Some had withered to a thread, and those, Wren said, were people who had stopped meaning anything about tomorrow.  She showed me mine.  It was the color of dishwater and about as wide. 

The lamp could illuminate them, she said, but only through something placed in the housing, and what we placed there mattered.  A living rose browned in a minute and cast nothing.  A silk rose from the pound shop cast nothing either; it was a counterfeit, pretending to a life it never had.  What lit the curtains had to be genuinely artificial: made, and admitting it, owing nothing to soil. 

I said I had nothing like that.  Wren took a sheet from her bag.  Vellum, the kind we use for stencils, and on it, in my own old line, the rose I had drawn for Nadia's brooch the winter she got ill and never finished.  Wren had been pricking that pattern onto forearms and shoulder blades for two years, ash ink, forty people, forty roses.  The cranes were folded from the spent sheets. 

I sat down on the floor and cried the way you do when the debt is finally itemized.  Then I took the twist of paper from my coat, where Nadia's hair had been since the morning I pawned the drawplate.  Wren had tools; apprentices always keep the tools.  I laid the hair along the stencil's lines, the way you lay hair for a mourning rose, and coiled it, and let the vellum hold the shape.  It was not a rose.  It was not pretending to be one.  It was a made thing that said so, and it was warm in my hands the way work is. 

We slid it into the housing.  Light came through the vellum and through the hair, and the dome filled with a rose forty feet across, and under it my thin curtain drank and widened and went on widening until it was the color of the inside of a shell.  Not restored, I want to be exact.  Taught. 

Wren said the curtain would keep as long as I kept making, and that the observatory would be here, since it was in no hurry.  I walked down past the reservoir at four in the morning and slept nine hours.  Then I redeemed the drawplate. 

The first order was a woman who wanted her son's hair set in a brooch.  I told her the truth, which is that a made rose brings nobody back.  It only teaches the withering part of you to bloom again, and it does it slowly, and it does it by hand.  She sat down, and I took out the vellum, and we began.
