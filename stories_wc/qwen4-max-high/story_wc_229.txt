The heat wave broke on the ninth of August, and the sea rose like something let out of a cage.  By four in the morning the rain came sideways, and the fog horn, which had baked for eleven weeks in its iron throat, cracked and gave up one long dying note.  Bram, the head keeper, shouted at it.  He shouted at the radio too, and at the water, because shouting was the only prayer he had ever been taught. 

Ines Vaz, assistant keeper, could not shout.  Scarlet fever had taken her voice at seven and left her a whisper thin as wire, and eleven years on the rock had taught her what a whisper is worth against a man with lungs.  The Marigold was out past the shoal with four men and a boy aboard, and no way to hear themselves called home. 

Ines went up to the lamp.  She had a theory she had never spoken, because speaking would have made it small: that a lamp does not send brightness but vibrations of intent, and the shutter is the mouth it speaks with.  A panicky hand makes a panicky beam, and a panicky beam is only weather.  Bram came up and worked the shutter lever like a man beating a drum, and out there the light screamed, and men drowning in noise turn away from it. 

In her apron pocket she carried a vial, empty these nine years, and its cork.  Her grandmother had distilled a garden at night into it: phlox, jasmine, wet earth, the hour when the flowers stop performing and simply mean it.  The oil was long gone, but the cork had drunk it and kept it, the way wood keeps a fire it stood near.  Her grandmother said the scent purifies intentions, burns the vanity out of a wish and leaves only the part you mean.  Since she was seven she had wanted one thing: to prove that whispers travel farther than shouts.  She had never trusted the cork, because wanting it that badly was its own kind of shouting. 

She put her hand on the lever beside Bram's and he shouted at her to get down, get away, leave it to a voice that could carry.  She dropped the cork onto the hot mantle of the lamp.  It blackened, curled, and gave up nine years of a garden in the dark.  The smell went through her like a hand through hair, and everything in her that was not love burned off: the years of being unheard, the wish to win, the small hot coin of proving him wrong.  What was left was the boy's name, Tomas, and one sentence with no ego in it: come home. 

She took the lever.  Not the frantic stutter Bram used, but the slow curl of a finger toward the palm, open and close, open and close: the oldest word, the one the body knows before the mouth does.  She beckoned.  She whispered his name into the glass until her ruined throat tore, until tenderness came out of her at the pitch of a scream that made no sound at all, and the beam went out over the water carrying it.  The wind was offshore, and it took the garden with it.  Bram stood with his mouth open and said nothing, which was the first useful thing he had done all night. 

Dawn came the color of the tower.  Cobalt: her grandmother's village had painted its boats that blue, and the light station too, and now the whole hour was that blue, wet and ringing, and the rain thinned to a mist that smelled of phlox.  The Marigold came around the shoal at six, low and slow, her bow turned toward the smell of home. 

The skipper climbed the stair with the boy asleep against his chest and stood in the lamp room turning his hat in his hands.  He said the horn had died and the radio was a man yelling, and he had turned away from both, and then somebody had said his son's name very quietly, and the air had smelled of his mother's garden at midnight, and he had known exactly which way the shore was. 

Ines could not answer him.  She had no voice left at all, and the cork was ash, and Bram was looking at her the way men look at weather they cannot explain.  She wrote on the log, in the small hand of a woman who has finally been heard: a shout is about the shouter, a whisper is about the one who listens, and that is why it goes farther.  Then she went down to put the kettle on.
