Echo Harbor was named for what it gave back.  A violinist stood on the mole at dusk and played one long note, and the cliffs returned it seven times, and the pilots counted the returns and steered through the fog.  Her name was Sela Voss, and she was the last of them.  Then the rainforest burned, a thousand miles south, and the ash came north for a year and lay on everything, soft and gray, and it drank sound.  The doctors called it chronic hush: not the fire's roar, which ended, but the quiet that stayed.  Sela played, and the note died a yard from her hands.  That night the Marigold went onto the shoals with forty people aboard, because nobody heard anything at all.  Sela wound seven strips of her mother's shawl around the violin, one for each echo the harbor had lost, and did not play again for three years.  She could not stop hearing the note she had played, going out and not coming back.  The wrecks piled up around the point until the pilots' children used them as bridges.  Inside that ring stood the greenhouse.  A rich man had built it to grow apples in a salt country, and the ground had settled since, so the glass roof lay level with the sand and you went down steps into an orchard.  Half the trees were drowned and black; one still leafed.  Sela loved it because nothing in it was finished.  She was acoustically perceptive in a way that could not be taught: she heard the shape of a room before she saw it, heard water under sand, heard the small tearing when ash settled on glass.  So she noticed what nobody else did.  Standing in the sunken orchard, she could hear her own breath come back to her, faint, from the glass.  The glass held a bowl of still air, and a fire beneath it would lift the ash and open a little room for sound.  She had one thing left to try, and it was not music.  Her grandmother had been a beggar at the harbor, and she had carried a mendicant's bowl, brass, dented, mended twice with solder.  In their family, on the coldest night of the year, they built a bonfire and told stories, and no one was allowed to finish one.  You stopped where you liked, and set the bowl in the next person's hands, and they carried your story until the next fire.  A finished thing is a closed thing, her grandmother said.  Leave a door.  Sela sent word along the wrecks: come down into the orchard, bring your children, bring the names of your dead.  Eleven people came, out of curiosity and pity.  She broke a rib out of the Marigold and burned it, and the fire stood up straight in the glass bowl of air, and the ash rose and went out through the broken panes.  Then she put the mendicant's bowl in the hands of a pilot's widow and told the first story, about a girl who learned the channel by singing at the cliffs, and she stopped in the middle of a sentence.  The widow, angry, took up the sentence to correct it.  Her voice went out and came back off the glass, thin but real.  Everyone leaned in to hear, and the leaning made a smaller room, and in that room voices carried.  The bowl went around the fire nine times that night.  Nobody finished anything.  A boy said his father's name and stopped, and someone else said the name of a ship, and someone else began the story of an apple tree that had never been picked clean.  Sela listened to the seams of it, the little returns, the way a voice frayed at the edge of the warmth and was caught by the next voice.  The hush was still there; it would always be there; they had only made a place inside it where people could answer each other.  Before the fire went down, she began to unbind the violin.  Seven strips, and she laid them on the coals, and the wool smoked, and the smell was her mother.  She played the long note of the channel, the one she had played the night the Marigold struck, and she did not play the last note of it.  She left the phrase open, and set the bowl in the widow's hands, and the widow hummed a note that was not the right one and not wrong either.  Outside, the ash went on falling on the wrecks and the drowned trees.  Inside, unfinished, they were beautiful.
