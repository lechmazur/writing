The grotto breathed mist in slow coils, and Ilsa Venn stood at its mouth counting the minutes left before the world turned over.  Once a year, for one hour, gravity inverted along this stretch of coast, and the sea climbed into the sky like a rope being pulled.  The archivists called it the Turning, and had built their whole discipline around it.  Ilsa had been forbidden to reenter.  She intended to anyway, with a ledger under her arm and her jaw set like a closed grate.  Three winters ago the Order had burned her teacher's papers and struck his name from every index, because he had walked the bends and then said a combination aloud.  They had not burned Ilsa.  They had only told her that the vault combinations were lost, and watched her face while they said it.  She had let nothing move in it.  She was a prism-minded archivist: one fact entered her and seven came out, sorted by angle.  The Order called this a defect.  Her teacher had called it the only honest way to read. 

The ledger she carried was a tally of lamp oil, dull as dust, and in its margin someone had doodled a spiral with seven small ticks.  She had found the same doodle in nine other books, drawn by nine hands across two centuries, always beside an entry no one would ever reread.  Not a cipher, she had decided, but a map, and a promise.  Her teacher had named such marks seeds of vigilance: small, patient things planted by people who knew they would not live to see the harvest.  The spiral was the grotto's seven bends; the ticks were where to stand. 

At the first threshold the mist thickened into a wall of white, and her lamplight came through it broken into bands.  Threshold effects, the old texts called them: light bent at every boundary of density, and the grotto was nothing but boundaries.  She held the margin to the green band, and the doodle's first tick woke.  Clandestinely luminous ink, ash colored by day, alive only under one refracted color, and only here.  The tick unfolded into a number.  She did not write it down.  That was the genius of it: no book held a whole combination, only the walk did, one digit per bend, and a walk leaves no copy.  The Order had not lost the combinations.  They had scattered them into margins and let the centuries misfile them. 

Now the hour turned.  Mist began to fall upward; the pool lifted off the floor in slow grey sheets, and Ilsa's braid rose and hung above her like a plumb line pointing at the ceiling.  The seventh threshold gave her a band the color of deep water, and the last digit with it.  She walked that bend along the ceiling, because the vault door was set there, beyond any ladder in an ordinary year.  Seven thresholds, seven bands, seven digits, held in the order the grotto had given them.  The lock took them. 

Inside lay her teacher's papers, unburned, only shelved, and on top a note in his hand: do not carry me out.  Copy me small.  Plant me where nobody looks.  The blaze in her wanted the whole testifying weight of it, wanted to walk into the Order's hall and set it on the table like a lit lamp.  They would take it within the hour and burn it properly this time, and her with it, and the margins would be scoured, and the seeds would end.  So she sat on the ceiling with the mist rising past her and read her teacher seven ways, until she could say him backward.  Then she closed the vault, and let the numbers go out of her like breath. 

Gravity righted itself while she was still walking out, and she came down the last bend with her boots finding the floor like an old argument.  In the archive she opened a ledger of grain receipts to a page no one would ever reread, and mixed her ink from lamp ash.  A spiral, seven ticks, and one sentence small enough to pass for a smudge.  It would stay dark all year, and burn only under a green band, in mist, in a grotto, in an hour that came once a year.  Let them keep their indexes.  She had learned where forgotten combinations hide: in the patience of margins, waiting for someone prism-minded enough to walk them into light.
