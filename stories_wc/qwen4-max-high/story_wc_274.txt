Snow had been falling on the library for nine years, and the reading rooms had learned to hold their breath.  Beneath them, in the cold stacks, Mira Venn served as cryogenic archivist to four hundred sleepers.  Her work was temperature, and paperwork, and silence.  The Charter said every sleeper had volunteered.  The city repeated this the way it repeated the weather: often, and without looking up.  Mira had stopped believing it in her third year, when she found the consent forms all signed in the same hand.  She had said nothing, because saying something would have cost the sleepers their power.  Contraband truths were the only kind the vault produced.  Every capsule came with an effects tin, and every tin held something the Charter could not survive.  She logged them as ephemera and hid them in the catalogue, where truth could sleep beside its owner.  Audit season required her to open one tin a day.  For nine years she had skipped capsule one twelve.  Ada Venn, admitted in the first frost, had been her mother.  Today the ledger had no gaps left, so Mira lifted the lid.  Inside lay a letter, folded twice, in Director Sorel's unmistakable hand.  It began, "My darling," and it did not stop being intimate for eleven lines.  A liaison letter, then: proof that the man who signed the Charter had loved a woman he froze.  He wrote that the tribunal wanted her shot, and that cold was the only mercy he could smuggle past them.  Then came the line that stopped the room.  "I have given the vault to our daughter; she will keep you, and never ask."  Surprise went through her like a floor giving way.  Within the blink that followed, while her eyes still stung and the compressors hummed their one flat note, Mira decided everything.  She understood, in that vertigo, that she had been built to be quiet.  Her whole career was hush money, paid in warmth.  Sorel had signed her appointment every winter, and had never once said her mother's name aloud.  She also understood what scandal would do.  The Directorate would call the letter a forgery, and to be safe they would cut the cold.  Four hundred names, including her mother's, would thaw into nothing.  To scandalize the city was to gamble her mother's life against her mother's voice.  She wanted the quiet kept, and she wanted it broken, and both wants were love.  She did not want a riot.  She wanted the conversation the city had been not having for nine years, the one that lay under every greeting like a stone under snow.  So she chose the slowest, quietest way to be loud.  Beneath the reading rooms ran the old interlibrary relays, copper lines to eleven buried libraries, dead since the frost and never dug up.  Mira had tested them one sleepless spring and told no one.  Dormant networks, unwatched because everyone believed them corpses.  She did not send a manifesto.  She sent a catalogue record.  Accession one twelve, ephemera, one letter, eleven lines, full transcription, linked to the forged consent forms she had photographed years ago.  Librarians are the last people who cannot leave a record unread.  By evening the entry would sit in eleven catalogues, and by morning in eleven hundred mouths, arriving not as an accusation but as a citation.  Nobody riots over a citation; they ask about it.  Then Mira did the harder thing.  She copied the letter into the Directorate's own registry with a note: the vault stays cold, or every catalogue under the snow reports why it stopped.  It was blackmail, and it was the most honest thing she had ever filed.  Her hands shook all through it, and she let them.  Then she went up into the echoing library, where snow pressed the windows and every footstep came back to her twice.  The quiet up there was dizzying, the kind that makes a person lean on a shelf.  That evening the desk clerk, a man she had known for years, did not mention the weather.  He said, very quietly, "One twelve."  "Her name was Ada," Mira said.  "Was she a volunteer?"  And there it was, the question nobody had asked in nine years, standing in the stacks with its coat on, waiting.  Mira answered it, and the echo carried her answer down toward the cold, where four hundred sleepers waited out the frost, and her mother kept her name.
