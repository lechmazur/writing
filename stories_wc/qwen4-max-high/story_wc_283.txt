Beneath the cliffs, the amber warehouse held everything the town could not say aloud.  Shelves of hardened resin, jars of trapped sound, and one glass taller than a man.  Sela was the hourglass turner, and she had turned the prophecy glass twice a day for nineteen years.  Each turn let the sand fall, and each falling grain settled the prophecy a little harder into fact. 

The wardens had read it from the jarred canyon echo on the morning of the Asking: the water will rise and the low town will drown.  So the wells were capped, the terraces went to thistle, and everyone climbed to the cold ridge to wait.  Sela had been the girl who carried the jar down from the canyon lip.  She remembered that the echo inside had not been one voice.  It had been many, folded over each other like wet cloth.  The wardens heard a sentence; she had heard a crowd. 

Now the sand was down to a fistful, and the last turn would finish the waiting.  She did not want to turn it.  She wanted to spin the prophecy back into raw possibility, to let the town be unfinished again.  For that she needed the echo unscrambled, and she had only one way to do it.  She would do it by cataloging lost buttons. 

Everyone who spoke at the lip that morning had dropped a button into the toll jar, since nobody carried coin up the goat path.  Its neck was too narrow for two fingers, so the buttons lay in the order they had fallen, like sediment.  The ledger that named them had rotted in the warehouse damp; the buttons had not.  She emptied the toll jar onto the long amber table, one button at a time, oldest last. 

Bone, brass, shell, horn.  Two holes, four holes, shank backs.  Thread still knotted in the eyes: indigo for the dyers, green for the terrace farmers, red for the ferry family.  Wear told her which hand a man buttoned with; a mend told her whose wife was patient.  She was not a priest and she made no announcements; she was quietly inspired, the way water is inspired by a slope.  Her apprentice, Bel, brought bread and stayed to sort the shell buttons by their shine. 

Eleven nights, and the line on the table ran three hundred buttons long.  Each button was a name, and each name was a place in the queue at the lip.  Order was the key; with the queue restored she could unscramble the echo.  She set the prophecy jar beside the line and loosened the lid a thread's width.  The voices came out braided, as always, but she knew the braid now.  She pulled them apart the way she had pulled the buttons apart, by material, by wear, by the small patience of a mend. 

The dyer's wife had said: if the springs fail, dig the old channel.  The ferryman had said: the water rises in the nineteenth spring, and falls by summer.  A child had said: I think the cliff is only sleeping.  Three hundred answers, most of them conditional, none of them a decree.  The wardens had taken the ferryman's first half and let the rest drown under the others. 

As the truth unraveled, the warehouse went quiet in a new way.  Not the sealed quiet of resin, but the unearthed silence of a floor lifted after twenty years.  Bel stopped sorting.  Sela's hands shook, which surprised her; she had turned the glass through two funerals without spilling a grain. 

She carried the jar up the goat path at dawn, Bel behind her with the button line copied onto a slate.  The town came because the wardens rang the bell, expecting a confirmation.  Instead Sela opened the lid all the way and let three hundred voices out over the lip.  The canyon gave them back one at a time, each from its own shelf of rock, the way the jar had given up its buttons.  People heard their own dead, and their own younger selves, saying if, and maybe, and it depends.  That was the whole of the shared wisdom they had paid for and then buried: not a fate, but a conversation. 

Below, in the amber warehouse, the last fistful of sand waited in the upper bulb.  Sela did not go down to turn it.  She handed Bel the slate and the toll jar, and the girl began, quietly, to catalog the buttons the town was already dropping into it.  Nobody knew what would happen next, and for the first time in nineteen years that was good news.
