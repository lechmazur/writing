The raft city of Kettledrum never closed its carnival, because the charter said the lagoon belonged to the fair only while the fair kept turning.  So the carousel ran through fog and through funerals, and the calliope wheezed at three in the morning, and Mira Santos walked the gangplanks meeting the mainland boats.  Her job title, printed on the company badge, was culture shock absorber.  She took their vertigo into herself so they would not have to: the fry oil stink, the breathing floors, the clown who wept real tears.  By noon she could hand them a funnel cake and a story about the fog, and their hands would stop shaking.  Her supervisor called her orderly intuitive, meaning she alphabetized the intake manifests and still knew which visitor would bolt before they bolted.  The company that paid her also planned to buy Kettledrum on Monday.  Privatization, the brochure said, would bring mooring rights, insurance, dignity.  Mira had been born on Pontoon Nine, and her mother still ran the ring toss there.  The company called it divided loyalty; her mother called it dual allegiance, and Mira had learned to stand straight under both.  The charter's other clause was the one nobody at the company had read closely.  The lagoon's bed was held in common by the holders of chips struck from the foundation stone, the granite block laid under the customs pier in 1891.  One chip per founding family, numbered, unglamorous, mostly lost.  Mira kept hers in her badge lanyard, a gray triangle the size of a tooth.  Her grandmother had used it to level a table.  After Monday, the buyer would honor only registered instruments, and a chip in a lanyard was not an instrument; it was gravel.  Unless the registry could be reorganized: every chip matched to its founding berth, every berth to a living name, filed before the sale.  Three days, all of them before privatization.  Mira could not go door to door; the company logged her hours, and the families would not open to a badge.  But Kettledrum talked to itself in paint.  Every raft wall was a mural, repainted weekly by the sign crews, and for eighty years the crews had smuggled news through the pictures: a fish facing left meant the harbormaster was drunk, three yellow teeth in a clown's grin meant a storm berth open.  Messages encoded in graffiti art, the oldest newspaper on the water.  Mira brought the ledger pages to Odile, who painted the carousel horses.  Odile read the numbers, then read Mira's face, and asked whose side she was on.  Both, Mira said, and did not apologize.  She did not want to stop the sale; she wanted her mother to still own a floor when it came.  Odile painted nine horses with numbered ribbons, and the numbers were berth numbers, and the ribbons' colors were the streets.  Under the ferris wheel mural she painted a clown holding a gray triangle up to the light.  By dusk the whole city was reading it: bring your stone to the intake tent, bring your grandmother's table shim, bring the chip you skipped across the lagoon and dived for.  They came in carnival clothes, smelling of sugar and diesel, and Mira logged them the way she logged visitors, gently, so nothing in them shook.  She absorbed their shock too, this time the shock of being counted.  If the clause failed, she would have handed the buyer a list of everyone to evict.  Forty-one chips by midnight, sixty by the second night, and the registry reorganized itself under her hands into something the buyer's lawyers could not call gravel.  Sunday, her supervisor asked why the intake tent glowed all night.  Community engagement, Mira said, which was true, and he wrote it down as a win.  Monday the sale signed at ten, and at ten-fifteen the lawyers found ninety-three founding shares recorded against the lagoon bed, common and inalienable, and the carousel still turning.  The company owned the fair; the families owned the water under it.  It was not victory, only leverage, a rent that could be argued instead of dictated.  Mira kept her badge and her lanyard chip, and went down to meet the morning boat.  A woman from the mainland stepped onto the breathing floor and grabbed the rail.  Mira took her elbow, said something about the fog, and pointed at the horses, whose ribbons now spelled out the names of everyone who had come to the tent.  The woman said the paint was beautiful, and Mira said yes, it is the local paper, and felt both allegiances settle like ballast, low and level, keeping her upright.
