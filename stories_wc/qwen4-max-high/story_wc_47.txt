Moss eats sound the way snow does, only slower and sweeter.  Wren called it the honeyed hush, that thick amber quiet under the collapsed roof of Bay Three, where four hundred dead server racks stood furred in green.  The fans had screamed here for eleven years, and now her own breathing came back to her softened, as if someone were handing it over.  She was a watcher by trade and a dreamer by necessity, the kind of woman who logged a room's temperature before she logged her own hunger.  At night she dreamed the racks were hives and the moss was the slow bees. 

She planted by a pattern left by the drip of thawing icicles: along the north eave, every March, the melt hammered a dotted line of pits into the slab, and where the water landed, moss would take.  She read the dots like braille and pressed a plug of moss into each one.  She had pried up the raised floor tiles for paths, and the cable trays overhead carried nothing now but fern. 

Eleven days ago, Aleksy's confession had gone viral.  Ninety seconds of an old man at a kitchen table, out of time and out of pride, admitting that he had bypassed the coolant interlock, then let the night technician take the blame.  Her name, a small local shame for nine years, became a large public one, then a large public sympathy, which felt the same in her chest.  At the hearing they had read her own maintenance memo aloud, the one flagging that interlock as unsafe, and called her quietly proactive, the way you would name a suspect. 

Strangers found the loading dock.  A boy held a phone at arm's length and asked her to say something to Aleksy, something real, and she watched his hands shake and understood he was nineteen and afraid of her silence.  She gave him tea and did not give him a scene.  She was never loud, only early: she oiled the gate so it would not shriek, cut the nettles from the path, and shifted the bench out of the drip line, so nobody would have to sit in cold water while they cried.  The first small thing done, she had learned, outlasts the last large thing said. 

By the dock stood a crabapple with a forklift scar down its flank, and for two weeks she had kept a scion of her mother's pear in a jar of water, waiting for the sap to run.  A graft is a wound you agree to keep open until it closes around something else. 

On the twelfth day, Aleksy came.  He stood in the drip line with his hat in his hands, letting the melt hit his shoulders, as if he had calculated that he deserved it.  Wren watched from behind a rack and let herself want, for one breath, to leave him standing there.  Then she came out from behind the rack, said his name, and pointed him to the dry bench. 

"They keep asking me to forgive you on camera," she said.  "I would understand if you never spoke to me again," he said.  "Sit," she said.  "The water is cold." 

From her neck she took the amber pendant, her mother's, a Baltic drop with a midge inside it, wings still open.  She had worn it through the hearing and the nine years after, warm as a grudge.  She proffered it flat on her palm, the way you offer a bird something you do not want to startle.  "Amber is a wound," she said.  "A tree bleeds resin where it is broken, and the bleeding keeps the thing that hurt it.  Look how you can see straight through." 

Aleksy did not take it.  "I have not earned anything of yours," he said.  "It is not a reward," she said.  "It is rootstock." 

She set the pendant in his hand and made him hold the tape while she opened the crabapple's old scar and slid her mother's pear into it.  You cannot undo the cut, she told him, but you can decide what grows out of it.  He wept without sound, and the moss took it.  Let the internet keep its ninety seconds; this hour was walled in green, and she could be seen inside it without being eaten.  Above them the icicles let go one drop at a time, writing their dotted line along the slab, and she would plant it tomorrow, and the honeyed hush closed over the two of them like resin, holding everything, hiding nothing.
