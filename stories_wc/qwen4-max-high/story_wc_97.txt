The arbor floated above the cloud line, an iron bower wisteria had claimed, and twice a day the tide of vapor rose through its lattice and fell again.  Marta was the station pianist, and the station was one platform, one lamp, one piano with the felt gone gray.  Compasses flickered here, needles swinging like moths, so the ferries came home by ear.  Every tide she played the homing waltz, six bars repeated, and pilots steered into the sound the way other men steer into a lighthouse.  She had played it for eleven years, and she had never once played anything else.  On the shelf above the keys sat the anniversary clock Ada gave her, brass balls frozen under their glass dome.  Its torsion spring had snapped at twelve minutes past four, the hour Ada's ferry went into the cloud and did not come out.  Marta had never repaired it, and she had never thrown it away.  She still tuned the piano to Ada's ear, a hair flat on the A, because Ada had liked the sourness.  At the low tide the wireless cracked: the Kestrel, forty souls, lost above the arbor with every compass on her bridge turning useless circles.  She had drifted north of the old channel, where the waltz could not reach through the dense vapor.  Marta played the six bars anyway, harder, until her wrists burned.  The Kestrel reported nothing but a smear of sound, no bearing.  She had until the horizon invited night, when the tide turned cold and the channel closed to any hull.  In the projection booth, under the stairs, lay Ada's calibration reel, black and white, shot years ago to time the arbor's tides.  Marta had watched it a hundred times and learned nothing: vine, vapor, vine, vapor.  Tonight she thought about the shutter, and about how Ada used to splice.  A projector at full speed shows you what a film says; at a crawl it shows you what a film hides.  The booth's crank had no governor, and Marta's hand shook too much to hold a slow, even turn.  So she lifted the glass dome off the anniversary clock and took the brass balls from their dead spring.  She wired them to the crank spindle as a flywheel, weight at the end of an arm, the way Ada had weighted a ferry's rudder once with a cooking pot.  The reel crept.  Between the frames of vine and vapor there were other frames, spliced in singly, too brief to see at speed: pages of a score in Ada's hand, and beneath them the arbor photographed from the north, with a channel marked in grease pencil.  Hidden sections, a whole second film inside the first.  Ada had written a piece for the northern approach and never told her, because Ada had meant to come home and play it herself.  It was not the waltz.  It was longer, lower, full of intervals that carried through wet air, and it ended in a place the waltz never went.  Marta copied the pages onto the back of tide tables, her pencil steady, her jaw set.  Fate had a timetable for this station, and the timetable said six bars forever.  She intended to surprise it with an encore nobody had scheduled.  She carried the pages to the piano and propped them where the clock had stood.  The first chord went out through the lattice and the vapor took it.  She played the low intervals wide, the way you call across water, and the arbor's vines hummed along like strings.  The wireless stopped hissing and said: bearing, we have a bearing.  Marta did not stop playing to answer.  She repeated the phrase that pointed north, then the phrase that said descend, then the phrase that meant here, here, the platform is here.  The Kestrel came out of the cloud with her lamps burning and her hull rimed white, and forty people found refuge on a platform built for six.  The horizon was just beginning to invite night.  Marta kept playing while they moored, because the encore was not finished, and because she had waited eleven years to finish something Ada started.  A child stood at the piano's end and watched her hands and did not ask her to stop.  Afterward she set the brass balls back under their dome and gave them a turn with her finger.  The clock still told no time; it only spun, slowly, the way it had been made to.  Tomorrow the waltz would go out again, six bars, faithful as a tide.  But the northern pages stayed on the piano, and Marta meant to learn them by heart.
