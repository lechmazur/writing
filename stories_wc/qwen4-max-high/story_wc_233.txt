The third warning came on a Tuesday, and the astronomers would not send a fourth.  The stars were bleeding, they said, and the sky would close by midsummer.  Queen Marisol signed the receipt for the warning and went back to her cold soup.  She had stopped believing in the kingdom three years ago, and the kingdom had not noticed.  She ruled in a murmur, and armies moved, and the court called it grace.  Everything in the palace was delicate, and every delicate thing was a leash.  Only the dreams interested her.  Every night for a year: a blue cave above the clouds, her own voice saying come up, and light running out of her wrists like thread.  She wanted to unravel it, to learn who was dreaming her, and why the dreamer knew her voice. 

So after the final warning she stopped waiting and sent for Ilke, the last lamplighter.  They climbed at dusk, two women and a mule, up through the gorse.  On the ridge stood the ember grotto, a hollow in the rock the size of a cradle, where the kingdom kept a coal from its first fire.  It was going out.  Ash lay over the mouth of the hollow in a net, fine as lace, and Marisol felt the old polite dread.  Ilke fed the coal a twist of moss and it breathed, and Marisol took it up in a pierced brass ball and carried it against her ribs.  Below them the clouds were rising, closing over the fields and the courts and the astronomers. 

In the gorse beside the grotto, Ilke said, "Breathe out with me."  They let one breath go together, and in the space of it the first fireflies of summer came up out of the grass, answering.  That was the whole rite, the oldest one: light that answers breath is alive.  Ilke had taught it to her when she was six and afraid of the dark. 

Above the cloud line the air went thin and blue and the path became a rumor.  Twice the mule refused the path and they went on without it, hauling each other up by the wrists.  The cobalt grotto was exactly as she had dreamed it, wet blue stone, a floor of fallen light.  Starlight pooled there and ran out along the cracks in slow bright threads, and the threads were knitting something across the doorway.  A woman stood inside it, made of the leakage, wearing Marisol's face and Marisol's patience. 

"Sit down," it said, in her own soft voice, the one that moved armies.  "Give me your hands."  It had been calling her nightly for a year, politely, the way a spider sets a table.  A star was dying above the mountain and wanted a body to finish in.  It offered what she had lost: belief, purpose, a reason to read the decrees she signed.  Marisol almost sat.  The lace tightened a little at the door, courteous, patient, closing. 

Then she noticed that the small lights hovering in the grotto rose when the thing spoke, and hung still when it stopped.  They obeyed.  They did not breathe. 

Marisol turned to Ilke and held out her hand, and Ilke took it, and they let one breath go together.  The coal in the brass ball bloomed orange against her ribs.  Their two breaths went out white in the cold, and nothing in the grotto answered them.  Nothing there was alive; it was only obedient.  It had learned her voice so perfectly that it had learned her grammar too, and her grammar had only one mood. 

"Let go," Marisol said, softly, the way she had ordered ten thousand men to their deaths.  The threads slackened.  The false fireflies sank.  The thing wearing her face bowed, because it could not do anything else, and poured itself out through the cracks and down the mountain, and the sky above the ridge went quietly dark. 

They came down before dawn.  The dreams did not come back, and she was not enchanted again, and she found she did not want to be.  She kept the coal.  On the ridge, in the gorse, she and Ilke breathed out together every evening that summer, and the small living lights came up to meet them, and that was enough to rule by.
