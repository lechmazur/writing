The moon stood above the notch when they started up, and Vera set her pace to it.  Eleven years on the line had taught her that clocks lie in a fire, but the sky does not.  Behind her, Pilar climbed with a flashlight she was too proud to use.  The ridge path ran silver between black snags, and below it the valley lay replanted in rows.  Vera's hands swung loose at her sides, the left one glossy to the wrist, the skin drawn tight as a drumhead.  In her pack, wrapped in a shirt, was a porcelain box the size of a pear. 

"You already know what I'm going to ask," Pilar said.  Vera did know.  At daylight the girl had to sign a witness statement calling lightning the cause of a fire that had come off a burn pile on a red-flag afternoon.  Two houses were gone. 

"They'll cut the crew to six if it reads escaped burn," Pilar said.  "Six can't hold a line." 

"That's the arithmetic," Vera said.  "I did it once." 

They sat where the path bent, and the moon slid a hand's width toward the far ridge.  Vera unwrapped the box and held it on her palm, and the crack ran through the painted lid like a river on a map.  She lifted the lid with two fingers, gently skilled even after everything, the way you lift a moth off a screen.  Inside: a milk tooth, a brass button, one apple seed. 

"A woman named Adela kept these," she said.  "Her house was in the second fire, the one three years after the lie." 

Vera made herself go back into it, the way she had learned to go back into a burn to find the place where the decision still smoked.  She had been twenty-three.  The captain had put the form on the tailgate and said the valley needed the crew more than it needed the truth, and she had signed, and afterward the men had called her one of them.  For years she had told it as a sacrifice.  Sitting on the cold rock, turning the seed with her ruined thumb, she found the softer, worse truth under it: she had wanted to belong, and the price had been the girl she was before she wanted it. 

"I traded being innocent for being inside," she said.  "Nobody haggles."  "They offer you a clean conscience for a good reason, and the reason is always real, and you never get the conscience back." 

Because the cause was never named, the burn piles kept being lit on bad days, and three years later the ridge went up again, and Vera went in after Adela and came out with her hands ruined and Adela's box.  Adela did not come out.  The porcelain had cracked in the heat and she had never glued it, because a mended thing pretends and a cracked thing testifies. 

Now she carried the box to schools, to grange halls, to the mill town across the border where nobody spoke her language.  She set the tooth, the button, the seed on a table and let people look.  A grandson's tooth, a husband's button, a seed from a tree planted the year a woman married.  Every mother in every language knows what a kept tooth means.  That was her work now: to translate what the fire had done into something no one needed words for.  Pain, made legible, could travel where a report could not. 

The moon had crossed half the sky while she talked, and Venus had come up over the notch, and the two of them kept their slow distance, trading places without touching. 

"I'm not telling you what to write," Vera said.  "I'm telling you the price is you." 

Pilar was quiet a long time.  Vera watched understanding arrive in the girl's face and did not soften it with forgiveness. 

"Six can hold a line if the county sends engines," she said finally. 

"And if they don't?" 

"Then I'll be the one who wrote it down." 

Vera closed the lid, fitting the crack together with the care she used on burned skin, and did not pretend it was whole. 

"I'll walk down with you at first light," she said.  "I'll stand where they can see me." 

Below them the young trees stood in their rows, patient as teeth, and the moon went down behind the ridge it had been aiming at all night.
