My father robbed the border highway for nineteen years without firing a shot.  He dissuaded drivers with territorial markers: a whitewashed stone, a red rag knotted on a culvert post, a boot nailed high on a milepost.  Every marker said the same thing in a different accent: this stretch belongs to someone who is watching.  Drivers pulled over, hands visible, and my father thanked them in a voice like cooled tea.  That was the polished calm I inherited, and the only capital I had.  He kept his take in a tobacco tin: lei, lira, forint, drachmas, paper he could never spend in a town that only knew leva.  I loved that foreign money the way other boys loved maps.  At twenty six I founded Milepost, a routing company that mapped the unwritten borders of cities: which couriers could cross which courtyards, which loading docks belonged to which men with folded arms.  Rent was the problem, until a scrap broker let us have eleven dead circus cars on a siding outside Ruse for less than a garage.  We wired the ringmaster's carriage for servers, put the designers in the elephant car, and pinned my father's foreign notes along the band car's velvet wall.  Investors came out at dusk to see us, and the wind found the calliope's cracked pipes and played three notes that almost agreed.  They called me perceptively calm, which meant I noticed the exact moment a man's greed turned to fear and spoke into it, softly, the way my father spoke into idling engines.  We raised four million, then eleven, and I pinned a note from every new market beside my father's old ones.  Bucharest, Istanbul, Athens, Belgrade: I was hanging my father's robberies on the wall one market at a time, and did not know it.  I started asking why some paths circle back, and nobody in the band car thought that was a business question.  Then a competitor gave the maps away, our contracts evaporated in a single quarter, and the eleven million became a number people said quietly.  Desks went out of the elephant car in the reverse order they had come in.  My cofounder wanted to sell our territory data to a firm that would use it to clear the courtyards we had mapped, and I dissuaded him the only way I knew.  I whitewashed the stones at the siding's mouth, knotted red rags on the switch stand, and nailed a boot to the signal post.  He was a city boy; he read the markers as a threat from something older than law, and he let the deal die.  In November the scrap broker came for the cars, and while I waited for his crew I forced the ringmaster's desk, because I wanted an answer more than I wanted to be polite.  Inside lay a tour ledger for the Aurora Circus, winter quarters at Ruse, its route the same four cities in the same order.  Clipped to it was a police report: the summer takings taken from a truck at kilometer forty one, the circus insolvent by autumn, the train seized.  The money that founded Milepost had been the circus's, and the circus's bones had sheltered Milepost while it spent that money.  That is why paths circle back: a stolen thing keeps its address, and the man who takes it spends his life walking toward the door he owes.  The crew arrived at dusk with cutting torches, and I asked them to wait while I walked the siding.  I set a whitewashed stone at each rail joint, knotted a red rag on every car's brake wheel, and nailed a boot to the elephant car's door.  Then I stood in the doorway with my hands visible and thanked them, in a voice like cooled tea, for coming so far.  The foreman looked at the stones, at the rags, at the boot, and read the sentence my father had spent nineteen years teaching the highway to say.  He said the paperwork was probably wrong, and the crew went home.  I still live in the band car, under one line of paper: his stolen currencies and my earned ones, indistinguishable at dusk.  I am paying the circus's debt in installments to a receiver in Vienna, and it will take the rest of my life.  At dusk the wind comes through the calliope and finds three notes that almost agree, and I let them stand, because a circle is only a road that finally keeps its promise.
