The storm went east before midnight and left the mill holding its breath.  Mira Vance sat in the dark of her escape room lobby and listened to the brook find its ordinary voice again.  She owned the mill and the four escape rooms inside it, eleven locks and one failing furnace.  At dawn the council would vote on the rezoning, and Cipherplex would pave the brook's bank into a parking apron.  They had offered her a fair price and a franchise logo, and she had said no twice, politely. 

Bo came down from the workshop with a candle and the jar.  He was nineteen and notably receptive, the kind of apprentice who took a half idea and turned it over instead of arguing it flat.  The jar held the seed.  It had come in March, unsolicited, in a padded envelope with no invoice and no return address, only a foreign postmark and a slip of paper.  Mystery seed mail, the agriculture bulletins called it; report it, do not plant it.  The slip said Amorphophallus titanum, ten years, then one night.  A corpse flower: a thing that spends a decade gathering itself and then stinks of rot for a single glorious evening.  She had kept it because she kept everything strange that arrived unasked.  She never learned who sent it, and after a while the not knowing felt like an invitation. 

"It could be invasive," Bo said.  "It could," she agreed.  "Cipherplex could be invasive too." 

Outside, the water had stopped clawing at the stones and begun to flow again, low and patient, the way it had before the rain.  Flow was her first rule of design.  A room had to move people the way the brook moved silt: no dead ends, no stalled minutes, every solved thing carrying them into the next.  Her business had not flowed in two years.  She had been pushing on the obvious door: price, advertising, a second location, harder puzzles. 

Bo set the candle down and said the thing she had taught him and forgotten.  "You never open a lock by pushing harder on it."  "You come at it from the side."  "Orthogonal," she said.  Two approaches at right angles, neither of them the front door, meeting at the one point that mattered.  She looked at the seed, then at the ceiling, at the old dye house with its glass roof and its floor drain. 

The first approach was not money but time.  She would build a room that could not be finished for ten years.  Sealed glass, sterilized soil, nothing in and nothing out, and inside it one seed and a clock counting toward a night that might be ten years off.  Sell the decade, not the hour.  Cipherplex could copy a lock in a week; it could not copy patience. 

The second approach came at the problem from the water.  Thirty feet of brook bank ran along her deed, and the county had a riparian trust that paid for nothing but protected it absolutely.  Give them the bank, keep the mill, and write the workshop into the covenant as a public night space, free, forever, for anyone building something.  She had learned her trade in a rented garage the landlord sold out from under her, and she had sworn no one would lose a workshop that way again.  Not a business the town could rezone, but a promise it had signed. 

"They'll say a flower isn't a plan," Bo said.  "No," she said.  "It's a reason to keep the lights on for ten years." 

She wrote until the candle guttered, in the hush that follows a storm, while the idea was still so new it had no name and could be killed by a careless word.  Bo did not argue it flat.  He asked what the room should be called, which was the second question, which was the receptive one, which was how anything got built.  She thought about the seed's ten years of silence and its one loud night.  "The Waiting Room," she said. 

Near dawn she carried the jar up to the dye house and did not open it.  It would wait in its jar until the case, the sterilized soil, and the inspector's stamp were ready.  Being receptive was not the same as being careless; you could take in a strange thing and still ask it to prove itself.  The brook ran quiet beside the mill, carrying the storm away in pieces.  Behind her the workshop windows were still lit, a nocturnal haven for whatever came next, and the door was unlocked, and it would stay that way.
