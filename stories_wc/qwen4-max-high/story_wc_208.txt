The GPS failed on a Tuesday, and by Friday the island had remembered how to be afraid of the water.  Satellites spoofed, screens lying sweetly about where the boats were, so the fleet sat on their moorings and listened to the wind turbine hum on the ridge.  Maren heard that hum as an invitation.  She was a driftwood sculptor, sixty-one and loud in three languages, and she had spent forty years asking the sea questions it answered in oak and pine and crate wood.  Her notebooks filled faster than her shelves: joinery, tide tables, glass cutting, the chemistry of salt.  Learning was her weather, always blowing.  In her shop, a tangle of grey wood read as kelp from the door and as her mother's hands from the bench. 

On the Saturday morning she walked out to the Gale Arch, the basalt doorway the storms had been hammering since before the church had a roof.  Spray went through it like shot, and the turbine's hum came down the cliff to meet the surf, and inside that long droning she could think.  She had brought the tin from her great-grandmother's loft, and she held one magic lantern slide up against the grey hole of the arch.  Painted on the glass: a fish, and inside the fish a woman, and inside the woman a doorway, three meanings stacked so that a child saw a fish and a keeper saw a safe line.  That was her whole method, layering meanings until one simple shape could carry a heavy load. 

Her great-grandmother had painted it for a lantern that hung in the arch, back when the alliance was still spoken aloud.  Keepers lit, fishermen hauled the wood, carpenters shaped the housing, and every generation cut its name and year into the beam.  Nobody had signed anything; the pact was woven through time instead, each strand a person who would never meet the next.  The last name on the beam she had salvaged from the arch was 1974, the year the harbour got its first plotter.  Her father had gone onto the Skerry in 1988, in fog, trusting a good screen and an unlit arch. 

Maren wanted to reimagine that beam, not restore it.  Restoration was obedience; she wanted the old trick to learn a new one.  She found Ivar up the ridge with his harness on, the man who kept the turbine singing, and whose grandfather had cut the last name.  She showed him the slide, and asked for nine watts and a cable.  He laughed at her, then went quiet, then said his grandfather's lantern had burned paraffin and his own hands knew nothing about wicks.  "Good," she said, "we will all be beginners together."  Dee, who had nearly put her hull on the Skerry when the screen lied, came with rope and a grudge against electronics. 

They worked between squalls, in the arch's throat, where the sea came through sideways and the stone shook with the turbine's low note.  Maren rebuilt the housing around the salvaged beam, roofed it with a hatch cover the storm had given her, and set the slide behind an LED and a lens.  The lantern threw the fish onto a whitewashed slab on the far headland, and from the channel the woman inside it stood framed in the notch of the Skerry.  Two marks, two distances, one line: drift aside and the woman slides out of the notch.  "Hold the woman in the notch," Maren told them, "and the rock passes a hundred metres to port."  One line, one picture, and under it all the tide math, the survey, the century of hands.  That was what she wanted: a simple thing to scaffold the complexity, so a tired skipper at three in the morning needed only to keep a woman inside a fish. 

Dee came in first on the second evening, and the fleet followed her through the arch, eleven boats strung along one painted woman.  From the cliff Maren watched them arrive inside her sculpture, and the hum went on and on, and she was crying and laughing at once.  Afterward she cut her name and the year into the beam, under 1974, and left the chisel for whoever came next.  Ivar was already sketching a better lens; Dee wanted to learn glass.  The alliance had a new strand, and it was still simple enough to hang in a doorway.  The turbine hummed, the sea went through the doorway, and the fish swam all night on the slate.
