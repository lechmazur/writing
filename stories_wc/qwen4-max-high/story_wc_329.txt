The sky-bridge crossed the orchard a hundred feet up, iron and cloudy glass, and Odile had bowed the Measure from it every dawn and dusk for nine years.  Below, the apples came out gilt-plated and identical, ticking on their stems.  She had come here at nineteen to wake beauty in a world of gears; the Wardens had hired her to keep its time instead.  Her violin's varnish had gone grey with orchard dust, and the gilt had rubbed off onto her collarbone, a tarnish she used to mistake for a blessing.  The law was cut into the rail beneath her chin: nothing leaves the ground it was planted in.  Living statues walked the rows, marble gardeners with moss in their knuckles, and when a root heaved a clod they pressed it down and held it until it stopped wanting.  That evening a locust shell hooked to the rail caught her sleeve, amber and split along the back.  She had passed a hundred such husks and never once looked.  She held this one to her ear, and the wind went through it resonantly soft, a note with no player left inside.  Nothing had died here; something had walked out of its own armor and left the shape of itself clinging to the iron.  Odile turned the empty thing over and thought about backs that split.  Under the bridge stood the First Tree on a brass plate, its roots bolted through the metal like veins sewn to a clock.  It had not fruited in nine years, and she had been told this was efficiency.  At dusk the statues gathered beneath it to be wound.  Odile always played them the Measure then, forty-eight strict beats, and their stone chests ticked slower and slower until they slept standing.  But once, when her bow had slipped, she had heard a different sound in the nearest one: a beat, a long wait, a beat.  They still had hearts, and the Measure was what kept them at one stroke a minute.  Speech was forbidden on the bridge, and the counting-frames tallied every note she played, but no frame had ever been built to tally a pulse.  She tucked the shell into her breast pocket, over her own heart, and began to play.  Not the Measure: two strokes close together, a rest, two more, the ragged hurry of a living chest.  She kept it low, under the ticking, where the frames heard only weather.  The nearest statue stopped with its hand on the brass plate.  Its head came up, and moss cracked at the corner of its mouth.  Odile leaned in and let the rhythm quicken, the way a heart quickens when it remembers running.  The statues knew the sound; that was old, older than the law, and understanding had not yet caught up to it.  In that gap between knowing and understanding, before the word treason could arrive, eleven marble gardeners took hold of the First Tree.  They uprooted it.  Brass tore, bolts came out of the wood like bad teeth, and the whole orchard's ticking stumbled.  Roots hung in the air, black and dripping and shamelessly alive.  Then understanding arrived, and by every law they should have set it down.  Instead they carried it, their hearts going faster than the Measure could forgive.  Odile played them out through the west gate, past rows of gilt apples that would rot unpicked and uncounted.  The sound she made was not beautiful yet.  It was dirt and torn metal and eleven stone things learning to hurry.  But under it, faint as wind in a husk, the orchard was breathing off the clock.  She set the locust shell on the rail, split down the back, holding nothing, and left the bridge for the first time in nine years.  The stairs were rusted, and she went down them anyway, into the ruined, unmeasured rows, following the sound of a tree being carried.  Somewhere below, a marble hand was learning to hold something without crushing it, and she meant to learn the same.
