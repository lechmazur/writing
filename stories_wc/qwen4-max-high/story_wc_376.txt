The oasis dried on a Tuesday, and by Friday the Bureau had commissioned a plaque announcing that it still flowed.  Sefa was the last gilded-leaf calligrapher in Verdance, so the work came to her.  Verdance was a city of greenhouses, nine hundred glass districts stitched together under a sky the color of old iodine.  Inside, the air was breathable and the ferns were fat; outside, the rain ate the paint off roofs.  Sefa had a condition the city's doctors called leaf-sensitivity: she wilted near dishonesty.  Small lies gave her a headache; large ones bent her spine like a stem in drought and made the gold crawl off her brush.  She laid the first leaf on the Bureau's plaque and felt her wrists go soft.  The gold flaked away like ash.  "The metal is faulty," she told the clerk, and the small lie cost her a nosebleed. 

At home she pried up the floorboards to find the leak in her cistern and found instead a nest of shredded paper.  Her mother had rented this shop from the old post office; the censors used to shred undeliverable mail in the room above.  Among the strips were lines in surveyor's ink, a compass rose, and the words WHERE THE UNSAID GO.  Sefa had wanted that address for eleven years, since the morning her brother left for the northern glass and she said nothing at the door.  He died out there, and the sentence she had swallowed had never gone anywhere she could find. 

She spread the shreds on her gilding table.  The streets on the shreds ran backward, mirrored, because the postmen had traced their map straight off an etched printing plate.  In her tool drawer lay a zinc-etched postal plate fragment, a corner of the old district stamp, salvaged when the sorting house was stripped.  She inked the zinc and pulled a proof onto tissue, then held each shred against the lamp so the streets read true through the back of the paper.  Strip by strip the map came together, and the X sat under the dead-letter vault beneath District Nine.  The postmen had drawn their treasure map and hidden it from the censors, and the censors had shredded it anyway, and the shreds had fallen through the floor to wait for her. 

She went at night, past the sleeping condensers, and down through a hatch in District Nine.  The vault was not a treasure room.  It was shelves: sacks of undelivered letters, last words taken down by clerks, apology drafts, the small true sentences people had almost said.  She searched for her brother's name and found no letter, because she had never written one.  Nothing she had swallowed was here; unsaid things do not travel, they only sit in the throat and go bitter.  A clerk's ledger lay open on the desk, and on the last page someone had written: We keep what was sent, not what was withheld.  Sefa sat on the floor of that honest, useless room and felt her spine straighten, the way a stem lifts when you finally water it. 

In the morning the clerk came for the plaque.  The Bureau's words stood bare in the metal, dull as a dead tooth; the gold had refused them, as it always did.  Beneath them, in leaf so fine it caught only the lamplight, she had written: The oasis is dry.  The gold had stayed.  "You'll be fined," the clerk said, reading it, and then read it again, softer, the way people reread a thing they have known and never said.  "My sister's in District Twelve," he said.  "I tell her the water's fine."  He looked at the small gold line and his mouth went crooked.  "It isn't," he said.  Sefa's headache lifted like fog off glass. 

She took out a card and gilded three words on it, and the leaf lay flat and bright, no crawling, no flaking.  I was afraid.  She addressed it to her brother and dropped it in the sorting-house slot, knowing exactly where it would go: to the vault, to the shelves, to the room where lost conversations wait for someone to come and read them.  That was the treasure: not a place that kept the unsaid, but a place that could finally receive it.  Outside, the sky went on being poison; inside, under nine hundred roofs of glass, one small true sentence was in the mail.
