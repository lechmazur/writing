The last person who remembered my name died in April, so by June I was free.  Freedom is what I call it when the nurses say sweetie instead of Ada, when the dock men look through me like weather.  I was the harbor's interpreter for forty years, the woman who stood between the deaf loading gang and the shouting world.  Nine of them drowned in the storm of sixty two, signing warnings that nobody on the bridge could read.  Now the town has decided to remember them with a brass band. 

That night the harbor was luminous, moonlight laid over the water like sheet tin, every mast doubled in the black.  I carried my father's camera, a heavy black thing from before the war, with no film in it and never any film.  Film cost money; attention did not.  He would aim it at my hands while I signed the catch weights, click the empty shutter, and nod as if he had caught something.  That was his whole teaching: being seen is not the same as being kept. 

Teodor was waiting at the foghorn house with the ledger of the night's timings.  The fire in seventy nine took his voice, so he advised in a whisper you had to lean into, and people always leaned in.  He never argued, never rose above a murmur, and still the whole waterfront did what he suggested.  He had been on the bridge that night, twenty years old, watching hands he could not read.  He learned to sign at forty and never once asked me to forgive him.  I felt his whisper as warm breath on my cheek and read the rest off his hands, which were slow and beautiful. 

For thirty years I had been mapping silence, and I am deaf, so I mapped it honestly.  Not the notes, which I have never heard, but the gaps.  My palm on the iron rail tells me the bell buoy swings every eleven seconds and hesitates before it strikes.  The crane cable holds its breath for two beats before the load leaves the deck.  I wrote it all in a notebook of rests, a score with no pitches, only the shapes of the harbor's pauses.  I wanted to translate that silence into music, because the gang had a language this town never learned, and a language is a kind of music. 

Teodor's whisper: the band plays at nine, and the mayor talks after.  Then his hands: the horn is mine, the crane is mine, and my skiff has already put a rag in the buoy's mouth.  Disrupt, he said, but do not shout; take away their noise and give them ours.  I laughed with my whole chest, which is a thing deaf people do without apology. 

At nine the band started, and the mayor named six men and got two of the names wrong.  Teodor lifted one finger, and the harbor obeyed him.  The foghorn swallowed its own note.  The crane went still, the buoy came up dumb against its rag, and the band faltered into nothing.  Into that made silence I stepped onto the lit pier with eleven grandchildren of the loading gang, and we signed the names.  Hands rising, hands opening, the old harbor signs for rope and salt and brother, held in moonlight so everyone could read them.  I kept the rests: two beats, four, one, the exact silences I had mapped, so the names arrived in rhythm.  That was the music.  Not a sound in it, and the whole town swaying to it.  Someone's mother was crying, and I was laughing again, and both were correct. 

No one there knew my name.  A boy asked what I was doing, and I aimed my father's camera at his hands and clicked the empty shutter.  He understood immediately, the way children do, and held his hands still for me while I taught him the sign for brother.  There is nothing inside to keep it, I signed.  He signed back: then keep it anyway. 

The band never finished its march.  In the morning they would say the harbor had gone strange, and blame the tide, and by winter I would be a woman in a chair by a window whom no one remembered.  Let them.  I gave the boy the camera, and he photographed my hands all the way back to the gate, and the moon kept laying its light on the water for nobody in particular.
