In Lull, the houses hung in the crowns of tide-trees, and the floor moved underfoot like a sleeping animal.  No one remembered who had planted the trees, or why the bridges ran where they ran.  Sela had been born on a slack rope bridge, and she had never once stood on ground that held still.  So of course she became an herbalist of quiet. 

Her nose was the trouble.  She could smell a bruise three branches away, the sour tin of a neighbor's fever, the green shriek of sap when a plank was cut.  The world came to her shouting, and she wanted, more than the guild's ledger and the name it printed, one hour of silence inside her own skull.  She wrote it plainly in her journal: to grow serenity that does not need the world to stop. 

Oduma, the old seed-keeper who had taught her mother, listened to her ask for something fast, fragrant, and famous.  Then she handed over a parcel wrapped in brown paper, folded the way Sela's own mother had folded parcels.  Inside the paper, before the folding, Sela's family always drew something small: a moth, a spiral, a boat.  A doodle nobody saw until the gift was open, and then only once.  Oduma had drawn two arrows on the inner face, one above the other, pointing opposite ways.  Sela turned the paper over twice, looking for the meaning on the outside. 

The seed was a corpse flower's, hard as a knuckle.  Seven years to bloom, Oduma said, and one night of bloom, and a stink that would empty the bridges.  Sela laughed, and planted it anyway, because ambition will take the slow road if it is the only road. 

She journalized the waiting.  Leaf, then no leaf; a spear of stem, then nothing for two seasons; the smell of the mud changing beneath the village twice a day.  Her nose taught her the hour before any bell did.  When the flats exhaled iron and cold, the flood was thinking about turning; the village called that pause the turning of the tide's thought, and settled its arguments then, in the slack water, when nothing was pulling either way.  Sela hated it.  Slack water was the only hour her tinctures worked, and she could not bottle an hour.  She journalized that failure too, in a hand that shook. 

In the seventh year, on a night when the flats gave off iron, the bud split.  The stink came up through the floorboards like a hand.  Doors slammed along the bridges; the wildflowers in the branch-crotches went on breathing their small sweetness into the dark, unheard.  For Sela it was not a smell but a crowd, a rot-mouthed shouting, and she stood in it with her hands open because there was nowhere in Lull to stand that was not moving.  And under the carrion, faint as a thread, she found it: green apple, wet stone, something almost like honey. 

The flower was not lying about the rot.  It was calling beetles down out of the dark to carry its pollen, doing its slow work through the very thing that emptied the bridges.  Sela breathed the whole of it, top and bottom, and did not step back.  Her heart went on hammering; the floor swayed; inside that, quiet arrived, not as an absence but as a current running the other way beneath the surface.  Two arrows, one above the other, pointing opposite ways. 

She understood the doodle then, seven years late.  Oduma had never wrapped a lesson on the outside, where it could be read quickly.  Below the slack water the deep flood was already coming in, and the old woman had known it, and had drawn it, and had waited. 

By morning the beetles had come and gone, the spathe had folded, and the guild sent a runner up the ropes to ask her name for the ledger.  She gave it, and felt the small vanity of it, and let it go by.  Then she journalized the night, the apple thread under the rot, the calm that had come while everything moved. 

By autumn the plant had set seed.  A girl came at the turning of the tide's thought, asking for something fast, fragrant, and famous.  Sela laid a seed on brown paper, and before she folded it she drew on the inner face: two arrows, and beneath them a small open hand.  The girl would open the paper today and understand it in seven years, which was how the wisdom traveled: underneath, in the dark, folded inside the gift.  Above them the village swayed, and the wildflowers kept their hush, and the tide thought about turning.
