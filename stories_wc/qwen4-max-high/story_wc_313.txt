The chime on Vera's porch was made of spoons from her father's bar in Sennet, and when it fell silent she knew the wind had turned to glass.  That hush was her signal, forty years running.  She was the levee warden at Harrow Bend, and she walked the embankment every dusk, reading it the way a doctor reads a wrist.  Seep, slip, boil, crack: she loved the small vocabulary of catastrophe.  This year the drought had pulled the reservoir down thirty feet, and Sennet had come back up.  She found it herself, on her rounds, following a wet seam at station nine down to a curb.  Streets first, then doorsteps, then the church's shoulders, all of it black and shining and streaming.  The water lay flat as a mirror above the ruins, so the town appeared twice, once drowned and once standing on its head.  Nobody spoke up there; the hush had a reflection too.  The Authority's letter came in August: refill in October, and before that, expunge Sennet from the levee ledger.  No town, no descendants; no descendants, no reburial, no claim.  The ledger was hers to keep, and its back pages held the last list of ninety-one households, copied out in her nineteen-year-old hand the week the valley was evacuated.  The county's paper files had burned years ago; the ledger was the only place Sennet still had addresses.  Beside her father's name she had copied the evacuation officer's note: refused the order, lingered with his stock.  She had believed it for forty years, and it had made her vigilant the way grief makes people careful.  She had also kept a tin of photographs.  Twelve frames from a box camera, taken the last week, when everyone knew and nobody said.  Now she pinned them along Midnight Ridgeway, the ridge road above the water, one at each bend where its house lay below.  At twilight people walked up to read them, and the road became a street again, hung in the air.  Vera walked behind them, naming the families, turning ruin into something they could hold together.  That was her whole ambition: to spin the wreck into a story everyone could carry.  The twelfth photograph she had never looked at properly.  Her father's bar, the shelf, a bottle of liqueur whose sapphire-hued label read BLUE HOUR.  On Tuesday she went down the mud stairs and found that label in the silt, soft as a leaf, still blue.  Under the ridge lamp she looked at the photograph again and saw what the mirror behind the shelf had caught.  The open door, the truck, her father's arms lifting a girl into it, and the girl was her.  Then the memory came up like the town: a spoon of blue liqueur pressed on her, cold and burning, and his voice saying, carry something sweet.  He had not lingered with his stock; he had lingered to load the last truck himself.  He went down with the water four hours later.  She had carried a handful of his bar spoons out on the truck, and they had hung on her porch ever since.  She sat with that until the chime's silence told her the hour.  The order said expunge, and Vera, who was precise about everything and humane about most things, took a razor to the page.  She lifted seven words, no more, and left the ninety-one households standing.  In their place she wrote: loaded the last truck; left late; drowned at the ford.  Then she photographed the corrected page, and the twelfth frame, and pinned both at the last bend.  Mercy, she thought, should be exactly as large as the truth and no larger.  Copies went to every kitchen in the valley, ninety-one households' worth, and the story stopped being hers alone.  If the board calls it falsification, she will hand them the mirror and let them count the faces in it.  In October the gates will close and Sennet will go under again, and the mirror will smooth over.  But on the ridgeway at twilight the walkers still stop at each bend, and Vera tells them about the spoon.  A boy asked what the blue tasted like.  She said, sweet, and like a door held open.  The label lives in her breast pocket, sapphire gone gray at the edges, and she shows it to anyone who asks.  Below them the water holds the drowned street upside down, and nobody says anything, and the silence answers itself.  When the wind drops, the spoons on her porch go quiet, and the whole valley listens with her.
