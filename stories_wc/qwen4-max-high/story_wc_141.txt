The demolition crew was booked for dawn, which Nell thought excellent news; nothing improves a legend like a deadline.  Eleven years she had spent busting the coast's myths: the weeping buoy, the drowned choir, the saint of the switchbacks.  Now she climbed the cliff road to kill one last one before breakfast. 

The abandoned observatory stood at the top with its dome slit open like a mouth caught mid-sentence, and the wind went through it, humming.  The village said the dome answered questions, if you played to it properly.  Inside, on the observer's desk, lay the logbook her brother Teo had kept until his heart stopped in that chair.  Beside it leaned his half-finished cello: ribs glued, belly bare of varnish, four strings over the bridge and no fingerboard above them.  Through the f-hole she could see the seashell he had painted and hung on a horsehair, a white whorl crossed by one red meridian. 

The last written page carried his riddle, the one he swore every culture asks in a different costume: where do the gone things go?  Nell had answered it forty times in print (nowhere, they go nowhere, that is the whole cruel trick) and had never believed herself at three in the morning.  She had come to bust the dome and decode his riddle in the same hour, which struck her as efficient. 

She lifted the bow Teo had left in the desk drawer, settled the unfinished body against her knee, and drew a low C.  The open box took the note and multiplied it, a resonant hum with no varnish to soften it, and inside the shell began to turn.  It swung, shivered, and stopped, the red stripe aimed straight at her sternum.  Nell laughed out loud, alone, in a building scheduled to become gravel. 

Then she stopped the string with her palm and watched the bearing dissolve.  Not north, not the sea, not the door: the shell had no north in it at all.  She timed it nine times, bowing, releasing, letting the logbook's cover fall so she could measure the survival against a sound she trusted.  The needle held true for exactly the whisper of closing pages, that soft rustle of a book settling shut, and then it wandered like a dog off leash.  Four seconds of direction, purchasable only by playing again.  An ephemeral compass, she wrote in the margin, in her professional hand, and underlined it twice because it was funny and because it was true. 

So she asked the dome their last conversation, the one she had been reimagining for two years with better lines for herself.  In the improved version she said, Teo, I am worried about your heart, and he laughed and put the shell in her hands.  She bowed the low C and retold that version aloud, and the shell spun, hunted, and settled on the blank wall.  A lie could not hold a bearing for even one whisper. 

What she had actually said was, there is nothing in it, Teo, it is wood and superstition, and he had answered, then hum it and see.  She had not hummed it then, because she had a deadline, because she was afraid of the answer, because he died on a Tuesday and she was busy.  Now she retold it exactly, every ugly word, into the humming box, and the red meridian swung around and stopped on her chest and stayed.  Four seconds; the wind whispered the pages shut; she played and told it again, and bought four more. 

That was the answer to the riddle, and it was not nowhere.  The gone things go into the retelling, and they last a whisper, and you can have them again for the price of another honest sentence.  Nell wrote the measurement, the pitch, the horsehair length, and the failure of the kind version, because she was still a professional and because the myth deserved clean data.  Then she signed the page, for the first time in her career, under a claim she could not bust. 

The machines arrived at 6:40, cheerful as promised, and the dome came down in a slow white shrug.  Nell sat on the cliff with the cello across her knees and the painted shell turning inside it, and told the foreman the true version.  He said that was a sad story, and she said yes, and played, and for the whisper of closing pages the needle pointed at her heart, where the gone things were.  Then it drifted, and she, gloomy and delighted, drew the bow again.
