The beekeeper came in October, when the falls were still loud, and he left nine jars of honey and the news that he would not come again.  His hives had failed in the drowned valley, and the ice road behind the water was closing.  Since then Ilse Marek had rationed the honey the way the city rationed lamp oil, a spoon at a time, and only for the dead.  She was the guide of the Museum of the Valley, three grottoes of salvaged things, and she still said the word museum with a small formal bow, as if the word were a guest.  The city hung behind the waterfall in terraces of wet limestone, forty thousand displaced persons who had climbed out of the reservoir country carrying their doors on their backs.  Now it was February, and the falls had gone silent, frozen into a blue wall, and everyone in the grottoes spoke softly because the silence was a loan.  The thaw would come in nine days, the water men said, and the lower terraces would go under.  No government claimed them; the register in Ilse's museum was the only census of a place that no longer existed.  Ilse had nine days to save what could not be carried.  In the third grotto the frescoes of the drowned town were already dying, their pigments furred with cave moss, the saints' faces going green as pond water.  She had one instrument left from the opera house, a lens ground from emerald glass, pried out of a lady's opera glass, and through it the moss green wash paled into nothing while the vermilion drawing underneath stood up dark and legible.  Ilse was historically oriented the way other people are superstitious, and so she copied hour after hour by lamplight: the weavers' pattern drafts, the gilders' hand positions, the ninefold fold of a burial shroud, every page dated and sourced.  But paper was not the art.  Her family had always baked the dead in bread: loaves shaped into initials, one for each name, glazed with honey water so the letters shone, and eaten warm on the last night of the year so that the names went into the living.  Her grandmother had made forty loaves in the old town; Ilse had made eleven in the grotto, and now there were four hundred names on the register and two jars of honey left.  Nothing flowered behind the water, so there would never be honey of their own.  She thinned the last of it with rosewater until the glaze smelled like a wedding and tasted like a lie, and the whole grotto smelled sweetly of that lie while she worked, which is how she came to think of dread as a perfume.  On the sixth night the water men came with lamps and said the thaw had started early, high up, and the ice road would be gone by morning.  They could take one crate per family up the stair.  Ilse stood in her museum with the crate open and the lens in her palm and understood, with a calm that frightened her more than the water, that she had been saving the wrong things.  A pattern draft is a description of hands.  She packed the register and her copies in the crate and left the reliquary, the looms, the gilded panels standing in the dark.  She woke the children.  She sent them along the terraces to knock on doors, and by the fourth hour she had thirty of them in the bread grotto with flour to their elbows, and she taught them the shaping: the loop of a B, the crossed legs of an X, the way her grandmother's thumb had pressed the serif.  Four hundred names, and no honey for the glaze, so they used rosewater and salt, and the letters came out pale as scars.  Behind them the ice let go with a sound like a door in the sky.  The waterfall came back.  They climbed the stair with the loaves warm inside their shirts, and every child could say the name of the loaf they carried, and knew how to bake another.  At the top Ilse gave the emerald lens to a girl who had asked why the saints were green, and told her to look at the water through it and remember the color, because someday there would be a museum, and someone would have to explain.  Below them the grottoes filled, and the rosewater smell went up out of the cooling bread into the roar, sweet, and thin, and unbearable.
