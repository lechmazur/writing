The Bureau of Continuity listed Sela Voss as negligible: seamstress, thirty-one, no associations.  Every morning the Bureau issued a fair copy of the past, and every evening she mended the coats of the men who wrote it.  In the linings, in buttonhole stitch, she hid the names they had erased.  Two hundred and six names, walking around the city on the backs of officers who never thought to look down.  One of them was her brother Teodor, who had never been born.  She remembered him anyway, which was the whole crime and the whole resistance.  Indifference did the rest; a city that shrugged could be edited forever. 

The plan was simple until the Thursday it unraveled.  Ferrin, the last operator of the drowned exchange, was taken at noon.  The demolition barge would reach the lagoon at dawn.  And the street map she had memorized was revised overnight: the causeway renamed, then unbuilt, then never mentioned.  Sela was carefully resourceful, so she stopped trusting paper. 

Rain came at dusk, and the broken pavement filled, and she walked out with her hem held high.  The patrols let her through both checkpoints because a woman with a bundle of mending is furniture.  Where the old cable trenches had subsided, the puddles lay deep and still enough to hold stars.  Shallow water showed only mud; deep water showed the sky.  She read the reflections like a map the Bureau could not reprint, a dotted line of stars leading north through the erased streets.  She counted her paces and knotted a thread for every hundred, so the route would survive even if the rain did not.  Water remembers the ground it lies on. 

By full dark the trail brought her to the auric lagoon.  Copper tailings from the old works had turned the water to slow gold, and the stars lay on it without sinking.  Exchange Number Nine stood knee-deep in that gold, its booth still glassed, its cable still buried, its company forty years defunct.  The key had lived in her hem for three years, a flat brass thing stamped MERIDIAN TELEPHONE, worn smooth by her thumb.  She had saved it from the scrap crate the week they dissolved the company and sealed every booth in the city.  Now she greased the lock with bacon fat from her ration tin, worked the key slowly, and caught the door before it could shriek.  The air inside smelled of wet paper and copper.  The directory was still chained to the shelf, swollen, but legible: names, addresses, numbers of people the fair copies said had never lived.  Paper rots; copper does not. 

She lifted the receiver and heard the exchange breathe, a live hum under the lagoon.  Ferrin had taught her the code before they took him, the old operator's courtesy that no clerk thought to forbid.  "Number, please," she said.  Then she began to read the names aloud, one by one, and after each name she said, hold the line.  Out in the reed flats the unlisted had tapped the old wires years ago, living unwritten and therefore unseen.  The Bureau had never needed to kill anyone; it only needed nobody to care whether they existed.  A name spoken down a copper wire is a small weapon, and it is not nothing. 

They came out of the reeds to the gold water, forty, then sixty, listening for their own names.  Teodor reappeared at the edge of the starlight, thinner, older, holding a fish trap, saying her name like a number he had never forgotten.  "Still on the line," she told him. 

The barge came at dawn and broke the booth into the lagoon, and the gold closed over the glass.  Sela stood on the causeway that had never been built, with sixty witnesses the fair copies would have to explain.  The clerks wrote the lagoon empty and the exchange demolished, and could not write away two hundred and six names stitched into their own coats.  She went back to her needle.  Let them revise; she would keep the record where they wore it, and the water would keep the map, and somebody would always answer.  Number, please.  Hold the line.
