The aurora came up green over the marble sanctuary, and the stone took it the way a pearl takes lamplight, softly, from inside.  Maren had forged eleven hands in that light, and never her own.  Above her the stalactites dripped into the prioress's bowl, already three fingers deep, and the bowl was the clock.  Sanctuary law held a truce open until the water reached the notch, and no longer.  Twelve drips a minute; she had counted them for nine years.  The prioress had died mid-clause, leaving the Concordance unfinished, and Maren had hated her for it with a patience that looked like devotion.  Now the northern clerk waited at the doors with a soldier's timetable, and the valley's grain rode on what she wrote.  The north would treat only with the prioress's hand, and the prioress was nine years dead, and Maren was the last sister left to know it.  They wanted the spring.  They would take the terraces, the silver, the tithe of wool, but a valley can live without silver and cannot live without water.  So Maren had measured her treason the way the cave measured time, drop by drop.  Give them three clauses, keep the fourth; betray the order's pride, not its people.  A whole betrayal would burn the sanctuary; a calibrated one would only scorch the ledger.  She set out her tools: oak-gall ink, the pumice knife, the brass key the prioress had pressed into her palm on the last night.  There was no lock in the sanctuary.  There never had been; the doors stood open to any weather and any army.  Maren had carried that useless key for nine years, wisely enduring the joke of it, waiting to be told what it opened.  Tonight she laid it on the vellum and saw.  The wards were a stroke.  Traced in one unbroken line, the key's edge gave the prioress's flourish, the crooked little wave she had never taught anyone, a lock that lived in a hand and not in a door.  Maren laughed once, quietly, and the marble gave it back softened.  Then she took the thorn from the key's hollow shaft.  It came off the heritage rose the first sisters had carried north in a pot of frozen soil, a cultivar with no name but ours, and it had slept in that shaft since the prioress died.  The Concordance required the signer's blood in the ink.  That was the trap inside the mercy: a forged hand could be perfect, but the blood would be hers, and blood is a name.  She pricked her thumb.  Above, another drop struck the bowl, and the water climbed a hair toward the notch.  She wrote the three clauses the north wanted, in a hand so exactly the prioress's that her wrist ached with the borrowed patience.  Terraces, silver, wool.  Then she came to the fourth clause, the spring, and to the place where the prioress had stopped breathing nine years ago, mid-word, mid-promise.  All her craft begged her to finish it.  She had finished it a hundred times in the dark, sealing the valley to the crown forever in flawless dead ink.  Instead she drew the key's crooked wave, and left the clause open.  With the pumice knife she worked the braid of seals apart to unlink the fourth clause from the three, so that no reading of them could carry the spring away.  A gap was not a lie; a gap could be argued, amended, lived into.  She had come here to complete her master and found she had come to forgive the unfinished, in the prioress and in herself.  Under the gap she wrote her own name, in her own plain hand, so the treason would have a living throat to answer for it and the dead woman would keep her perfect script.  The bowl reached its notch.  The clerk came in out of the green light, stamped the three, and did not ask about the blank, because clerks are paid to count what is written.  He would notice in a month; the valley would have a harvest by then, and a lawyer.  Maren slid the thorn back into the key's shaft and left the key on the altar for the next forger, whoever she would be.  Outside, the aurora thinned to the color of the inside of a shell, and the stalactites went on dripping, patiently, into a bowl she would empty and set back.  Nothing finished, everything kept.
