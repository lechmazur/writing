The town went silent on a Tuesday; nine days later Nell came to measure its wind.  The terminal was shut pending inquiry: no cranes, no shift whistle, no bar radios, and a port city that lived by that noise had gone quiet with it.  She was an accident investigator, and the silence was useful: nothing moved now but the air, and the air was her evidence. 

Inside the port gate the empty shipping containers rose seven high in corridors a truck wide, a towering labyrinth with weather of its own.  Haze came off the water each morning and hung in those streets like breath in a cold room.  Nell had flown eleven years as a flight engineer before she traded wings for a clipboard, and the patch from that life was still sewn inside her coat: a spanner over three feathers, the crew who kept the air honest.  Aviation had taught her that air is a witness, and that witnesses must be canvassed before they change their story. 

So she did what she always did: a survey.  Tape, chalk, an anemometer on a pole, sixty ribbon telltales, and a notebook ruled into grids for documenting wind patterns between buildings.  Here the buildings were steel boxes, and they moved.  A container city is rapidly adapting: one repositioned box deletes a street and opens another, and the wind learns the new map in seconds.  Every gust she logged described a city that had not existed on the night Teodor Barac died.  So she rebuilt that night from the stowage logs, box by box, until the yard on her paper matched the yard at 02:14.  Then she corrected her readings, intersection by intersection, and the haze gave her numbers to one decimal place and no certainty at all.  Precision in a fog: she had built a career on it. 

The dead man had been a lashings foreman, forty-one, standing in the lee of Bay 12 to radio the crane.  The stack above him came down at 02:14, and the file wanted a cause, singular, with a name attached.  Nell did not believe in singular causes; she believed in singular convergence.  Four lines, none of them fatal alone, meeting once.  A gust funneled down a corridor forty minutes old, opened when one reefer box was shifted for a customs inspection.  A corner casting corroded past tolerance, invisible under three coats of paint.  A gap left at the base of the stack for a forklift, so the gust got underneath as well as against.  And a man who stepped into the lee because the lee was the only quiet place to be heard on a radio.  Take any line away and the night is ordinary.  That was the horror of it: nothing had gone wrong enough to be wrong. 

Two years earlier she had recommended an anemometer array for this terminal, and the recommendation had been filed, and the filing had been excellent.  She had documented the danger beautifully and left it standing.  What she wanted now was not a better report but foresight in action: knowledge that arrives while it can still be used. 

On the last afternoon the ribbons at the mouth of Corridor 9 went flat and stiff together, and the anemometer climbed: 11, 14, 19 meters per second.  A salvage crew had come back early, and a boy in a new hi-vis vest was walking into the lee of a seven-high stack to answer his phone.  She had chalked that stack's corroded corner that morning; she saw the four lines lean toward one another.  Nell did not write it down.  She ran, shouted, and pushed him out into the open, and behind them the stack shifted a hand's width and boomed like a struck drum, and held. 

The boy asked how she had known.  She said the air had been saying it for two years, and she had finally answered. 

That night she telephoned the harbor master and stopped the salvage: no foot traffic in any corridor until its wind had been read, and the map redrawn every time a box moved.  In the morning she unpicked the flight engineer's patch from her coat and pinned it above the control room's new wind map, where the crews would see it before they walked out into the labyrinth.  Three feathers and a spanner: the people who keep the air honest, and who answer when it speaks.  By Thursday the cranes were waking, the town's noise came back, and Nell stood in it listening for the next four lines.
