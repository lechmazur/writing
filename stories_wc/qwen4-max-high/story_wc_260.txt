The fraud had been public for nine days, and Nell still fed the animals at four in the morning, because their hunger did not care what she had believed.  Piers had called it a captive-breeding program; the buyers had called it conservation; the court called it laundering wild-caught chameleons through a rescue station that had Nell's signature on every form.  Now the ministry had frozen the accounts, and the export house's lawyer had offered her a way out: sign that she had been deceived, surrender her notebooks, keep the lights on. 

The workshop stood open on the escarpment side, so mist came through it all night and the stars sat above the mist like lights over water.  Nell worked by a red bulb, because white light woke the chameleons and she had never been willing to wake them for her own convenience.  People called her quietly vivid: gray sleeves, no perfume, a wildlife handler's ruined hands, and a voice that made frightened animals decide to live.  Piers had used that.  He had known that a woman who could calm a wild Trioceros with two fingers would also calm her own doubts, and would sign whatever kept the crates moving toward safety.  Her convictions had been fragile things, blown thin by wanting: that gentleness was a kind of proof, that a man who loved the animals could not be stealing them. 

Tonight she meant to reconstruct the intake ledger, and there was only one honest way to do it: by shedding layers.  Piers had built his paperwork the way a chameleon builds a color, out of thin sheets laid over the truth.  She began with the crate tags, holding each over a bowl of warm water until the new lacquer lifted and the old number rose through it like a bruise.  Then the ledger pages, pasted in pairs; she parted them with a scalpel, one dry sheet at a time, and the shed paper curled on the bench like skin. 

In the third enclosure the female called Nine was shedding too, hanging in ragged translucent flags from her shoulders, and Nell let her be.  Nine had come in fourteen months ago from a boy with a sling: wild, gravid, and missing the last centimeter of her tail.  Nell had drawn that tail tip in her notebook, as she drew every distinguishing mark: a healed nub, pale as candle wax, one dark band below it.  A chameleon's tail does not grow back; the animal learns to curl what is left. 

The top sheet of page nine recorded Nine as hatched on site, clutch three, mother of everything the station had ever exported.  The sheet beneath it, in her own hand, recorded a wild female from the ridge road, sling injury, tail tip gone.  Piers had renamed every animal, but he could not rename a healed nub, and her drawing and the shed page and the sleeping female agreed.  Under the red bulb she lifted Nine's coil onto her flat palm and saw it again, unchanged, the one detail in this whole station that nobody could forge.  Forty-one permits, and every buyer's clean conscience, rested on that one pasted sheet. 

The lawyer's offer sat in her pocket, folded into four.  It was not even a cruel offer: the animals would be fed, Salim would keep his wages, and she would only have to agree that she had been a fool, which she had been.  That was the shape of her whole problem: how to keep her compassion without spending her self-respect to buy it. 

She sat in the mist until the stars thinned, holding both sheets, and understood that she had been shedding for nine days without noticing: the belief that being gentle made her safe, the belief that Piers's love of the animals had been the same substance as her own.  What was left was smaller and harder, and it would hold. 

At six she woke Salim, told him plainly what Piers had done and what she had signed, and asked him to help move the gravid females before the inspectors came.  Then she photographed both layers of page nine, wrote the truth beneath her drawing of the tail tip, and sent it to the ministry and to Hamburg, with a list of what forty-one seized chameleons would need to stay alive.  She softened no line, and she put Salim's name in none of them.  Nine woke, curled what was left of her tail around Nell's finger, and held; the mist went on coming through the workshop, and nothing in it hurried.
