The roof had rusted out in ninety places, and the stars came through every one.  Ilse sat beneath them in the studio library, where the shelves had gone orange and the salt air had eaten the spines off the books.  Six hours ago she had been a stunt double with a hometown, a mother, and a broken arm at eight years old.  Six hours ago she had not known she was the fourth printing of Adria Vane, grown to take her falls.  Her hands shook, which was ordinary; her hands had shaken through every fall she had ever taken.  Heights, fire, deep water: the studio called them her defects, and Sol, who had trained her, called them weather you learn to fly in.  Their word for her was firmly adaptive, meaning she never refused a fall, she only changed how it could be survived.  Shorter breaths, wider stance, a rig checked four times instead of two.  In her pocket lay her half of the cue conch, the nacre receiver she had snapped off its earhook that afternoon when she tore it out.  A broken shell still whispers.  What it whispered now was Sol's voice, thin as wire: the fifth print is clean, no tremor, no phobias, retire the fourth after the dawn take.  So the fear was not a defect they tolerated; it was cargo they were finished carrying.  The archive box in her lap held a child's plaster cast, Adria's at eight, signed wall to wall in marker gone brown and smudged.  Ilse remembered the seawall fall that broke that wrist, remembered the cold and the taste of iron, and the memory was not hers, and it still closed her throat.  Under the signatures the marker had been dragged, letters smeared into each other on purpose, the way a child hides what she is not allowed to say.  Adria had been a clever, frightened child, and she had used a shift of one, the simplest cipher there is.  Ilse worked it with a pencil stub on the inside of the box lid, and her hands shook, and she kept working.  The smudges gave up UIFZ QVU JU JO UIF HJSMT.  They put it in the girls.  Sol had told the star a hundred times, through the shell, that the excision was clean and the terror destroyed.  He had not destroyed it; he had decanted it, eight years of night terrors and every flinch since, into a body built to hold it and fall anyway.  The betrayal ran both directions: he had lied to the star about the double, and to the double about herself.  Ilse sat with that while the stars crawled across the holes in the roof.  Adria had not felt anything in eighteen years; the reviews said luminous, said hollow, said watch her eyes.  Fear is the floor under the other feelings, and when you take it out the whole house comes down.  Ilse had been carrying the floor.  Nobody had ever let her argue that the shaking was the reason she was still alive.  She could hoard it, be the only one alive who could still be afraid for Adria Vane, and be retired at dawn for her trouble.  Or she could give it back.  She fitted the broken half against the bone behind her ear, and it whispered, and she whispered into it, because a shell that hears is a shell that speaks.  Sol had logged her receiver as dead, and the halves shared one channel anyway, so to kill hers he would have to kill the star's, and he would not do that on the morning of a live take.  "Adria, the cast you signed at eight, the smudges under the names, I've decoded it, and you already knew."  She read out the shifted letters and the plain ones, and where the box was, and then the thing she had been trained to be ashamed of.  "I am afraid of heights and fire and deep water, and I have never once missed a mark, because my hands shake, because I check the rig four times, because I know exactly what the ground is."  "The fifth print won't shake, and it won't check either, and it will die on the third take, and they will print a sixth."  "I am not your defect; I am the part of you that keeps you alive."  The shell hissed, and across the black water a light came on in the star's house.  Ilse trembled all over, and let herself, and was certain.  "Come and get your fear," she said.  "I'll teach you to fall with it, but I'm keeping the going anyway; that part I grew myself."
