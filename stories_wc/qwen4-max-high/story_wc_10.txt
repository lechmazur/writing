Noise became impossible for me on the ninth of February, when the ferry capsized and the cold drove into my ears like nails.  The papers called me the capsize survivor; I was the only one the divers brought up alive.  My sister's hand had been in mine for eleven minutes, and then it was not. 

In April the winterglass conservatory took me on, since a tuner with no ears is still a tuner with hands.  Bram, the keeper, had been deaf from birth and was silently expressive: his eyebrows argued, his palms apologized, his shoulders laughed.  He walked me through the aisles of double glazing, where frost grew ferns on the inside of the panes.  The night-blooming cereus still opened at midnight; the lemon tree still ripened; the light still broke into colors on the flags.  Nothing I had lost had shaken them.  That was the first thing I could not forgive, and the first thing I loved: unshaken marvels, keeping their appointments without me. 

I still carried my father's tuning fork case, velvet-lined, twelve forks from C to B, a tool for a sense I no longer had.  Bram took the A, struck it on his knee, and set the stem against the glass.  The frost ferns shivered and drew a new line.  He looked at me the way a teacher looks at a student who has finally stopped arguing.  I did not want to mourn music; I wanted to translate silence into music. 

Bram showed me the thermochromic film we used to hunt heat leaks in the glazing: press a palm, and the sheet blooms color where the warmth is.  That winter we began heat-mapping emotions.  Volunteers from the village sat in the potting shed and held the film while Bram asked his questions with his hands.  Grief was not blue.  Grief was a hot sternum and cold fingertips; joy ran up the wrists; a lie went green at the throat.  I gave each heat a pitch, each spread of color a duration, and wrote the maps onto staff paper. 

Then I composed the capsize.  I scored it cold throughout, eleven minutes of blue, because that was what I remembered.  Bram read the pages, and his face did something I had never seen it do: it closed.  He put my hand flat on the film and held his own over my heart until the color came.  Red, spreading.  My sister's grip had been warm; the water was cold, but she was not the water. 

I had to rectify the score.  I rewrote the eleventh minute as a rising heat, a hand passing warmth into a hand, and let the cold be only the accompaniment.  The film found something else that week: a cold seam along the north wall, a failed seal bleeding heat out of the cereus house.  Bram and I rectified it with putty and new glazing tape, working by lamplight while the frost withdrew from that pane.  Two rectifications, the same instrument, the same lesson: find where the warmth is going and follow it. 

We gave the concert on the longest night, in the cereus house, for nineteen people from the village.  I had built a rail of ash wood along the benches and set the twelve forks into it, stems down, tines free.  Bram played the score with his palms and a soft mallet, and the wood carried it into nineteen pairs of hands.  Behind us the film, hung on the glass, took the room's heat and answered in color, so the music could be seen as well as held.  At the eleventh minute the sheet went red from the middle outward.  I watched Bram's face, which said everything without a sound, and then I watched the cereus.  It opened at midnight, as it had through the war and the flood and the night the ferry rolled, unshaken, indifferent, generous.  The scent went through the house, and the music went through our hands, and no one asked for sound. 

Afterward a woman pressed my hands and held them, and the film between our palms bloomed the color I had written for the eleventh minute.  I keep the case open now on the potting bench.  Twelve forks, no ears, one language: the marvels go on, and we are the ones who must be rectified until we can feel them.
