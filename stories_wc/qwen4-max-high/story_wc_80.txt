The reservoir had been falling all summer, and Ilse recorded every inch of its retreat in a hand that never wavered.  She was the shore surveyor for the water authority, firm to the millimeter, paid to be exact about loss.  Her crews called her stoic; they never saw her sit afterward in the truck with the windows up, shaking.  A level is a sensitive instrument that has to be held perfectly still; she had built herself the same way. 

In August the pavilion came up.  Its dome broke the surface like a held breath, then its eight columns, then the bandstand where her mother had sung at nineteen to forty people and a lamp full of moths.  Her mother had never gone back to it, not even in words.  Now she lay in the care home above the dam, silent as silt. 

The rains were coming; by November the valley would belong to the water again.  The old bridge at the neck of the reservoir would be lifted tonight for the last barge, and then chained open.  Ilse walked out across the mud while the span still lay flat, carrying her level, a pry bar, a trowel, a bag of lime mortar, and the inkwell.  Brass, gimbaled, a screw cap over a felt seal: an antique traveling inkwell her mother had carried to every hall she ever sang, the one thing in the family that never spilled. 

For eleven years Ilse had reset the authority's benchmarks along the shore, and in every foundation repair she placed a note.  Not confessions; inventories.  The pear orchard Mr. Aune never planted.  The bakery the Ferrow sisters traded for wages.  The stage.  She had told nobody her real ambition: to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, digging in the strata of what people put down and never picked up again.  Notes in mortar were that excavation run backward, sealed against the water, waiting for a drier century. 

Behind her the drawbridge rose.  She heard the chain go on, the watchman's bicycle bell, then nothing but the mud ticking as it cooled.  The pavilion smelled of cold iron and rot, and the sunset came through its columns like a fire kept low.  Around her the mud held the town's small bones: a doorknob, a shoe, a tin sign for a bakery that never opened. 

At the north pier, where the water still stood knee-deep, the footing had opened a joint wide as a wrist; she set her pry bar in and wrested the loose stone free.  It came with a sound like a swallowed word.  Inside, in a lead tube gone white with age, lay a folded page, dry as a moth's wing.  She knew the hand before she read it, the looping capitals from the label on the inkwell's case, and the ink was iron-gall gone brown, the same recipe she still mixed to fill it.  Her mother had written it the spring before the flood, when Ilse was ten, and pushed it into a footing repair with her own hands.  "Wanted: to sing on this stage every summer until I am old; wanted: to be asked about it, once, by my daughter." 

Ilse sat back on her heels and made no sound at all.  For forty years she had read the silence as a wall, and it had been a door with no handle on her side.  She had catalogued a hundred strangers' dreams to avoid excavating the one under her own roof.  Two absences, woven: the pavilion her mother would not name, the question Ilse would not ask, warp and weft holding each other upright, an interlaced absence she had leaned on all her life. 

Dark came; she lit her lamp, and the dome held the light the way a stove holds fire, quietly, without spending it.  She worked all night.  She wrested the rotten bedding out, mixed the lime, and reset the stone.  In the joint she sealed her mother's page back in its tube, and beside it her own note, in a new one, written in her mother's ink.  The same work, for forty years, in the same ink.  Her note said: excavated, one stage, one singer, one question, forty years late.  She pointed the mortar smooth and stamped it with her benchmark number. 

At dawn the watchman came to lower the span, and Ilse crossed with the inkwell in her fist.  There was one thing left to wrest, and only one mouth could give it.  In the care home she took her mother's hand, and her hand did not shake.  "Tell me about the pavilion," she said.  Her mother's eyes moved, caught, and burned low and steady, like a lamp in a dome under water.
