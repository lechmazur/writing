The blood work came back at nine forty, and by ten Mira had read it twice and told the kitchen to hold the lunch trays.  Numbers like these meant hours, not weeks.  Saint Aurelia's sat at two thousand meters, an old tuberculosis sanatorium turned hospice, and in November it floated above the inversion layer while the valley drowned in fog.  Down there the buses crawled with headlights on; up here the light came off the snow so hard the windows hummed.  Mira stood in that radiance and felt the usual stillness, which was never still: the oxygen concentrator sighed every four seconds, the clouds below boiled without sound, her own pulse kept time against the glass.  Mira was the hospice's music therapist, serene as a held chord, and today she had one job and intended to do it flawlessly.  Vera Lund, eighty-one, had not spoken since dawn.  In her right hand she held a shard of a broken vinyl record, black and triangular, three grooves and a corner of the label.  Thirty years ago, on the night June left for the valley, she had thrown the record at a wall, and Vera had kept the piece that survived.  Mira had learned not to ask about it.  She sat, tuned her guitar to the room's low pitch, and began hymning the mundane, which was her whole method: she sang what was already there.  The water pitcher, sweating on its tray.  The wool sock halfway off the heel.  The linen drying on the ridge line, which above the clouds took one hour and came back smelling of sun and cold iron.  That was the scent of hope, Mira thought: not flowers, not incense, but clean laundry pulled in from freezing air and still warm at the fold.  Vera's breath caught the tune and carried it two notes.  Mira called June from the corridor.  "She chose the mountain over me," June said.  "Thirty years is a choice, not an accident."  Mira did not soften it and did not argue.  "The blood came back this morning," she said.  "The road is clear above eight hundred meters."  "Bring the rest of the record, if you still have it."  The pause was full of valley static.  "I have it," June said.  Then Mira did the decisive kindness she was known for, the kind that never waits for permission: she turned Vera's bed to the west window, washed her face and hands, laid on the good quilt, and asked the nurse to hold the morphine at the level where speech was still possible.  She had made a study of flawless goodbyes.  Her father's had gone wrong in a corridor while she took a call about a scheduling error, and she came back to a flat line and a room that would never be finished.  Since then she had built endings the way other people built clocks.  June arrived at four, coat wet to the knees from the fog below, smelling of rain and diesel.  She stood in the doorway of all that light like a woman who had walked into the wrong church.  From her bag she took an envelope, and from the envelope, eleven pieces of vinyl.  "I couldn't throw them out," she said.  "I tried twice."  Vera opened her eyes.  Mira spread the quilt flat and began to reunify the record, fitting shard to shard the way she fit chords under a dying woman's breathing, edges first, then the label.  It would never play again.  It did not need to.  Where the pieces met there were hairline seams, and Mira, still hymning the mundane, sang the seams too: the dust, the crack, the wall, the thirty years.  Vera's finger found a join and followed it.  "Side A," she whispered, reading the label whole for the first time since it broke.  "Take two."  June, who had sung along in the car as a girl, sang four words of it now, badly, and stopped.  Vera laughed, one syllable.  Mira had prepared a closing line, some clean benediction; she let it go.  The flaw was the good part, the crack where the breath got in.  Later, when the room had gone quiet in the way that pulses, Mira opened the window a hand's width and let the cold come in.  June slept in the chair with her mother's hand in both of hers.  The air smelled of sun-warmed linen and the hot dust of the lamp, and Mira breathed it in, the scent of hope, and let the goodbye be imperfect and complete.
