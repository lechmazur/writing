At the blue hour the sea and the sky were the same cold ink, and the orchard raft stopped rocking.  Nine hundred lashed logs made the ring; the soil came in barrels, the water from fog nets, the apples small and salted.  Maren felt the slack in her knees before the water showed it.  Twenty minutes of stillness, then the swell would come back around the driftwood ring and the apple trees would sway and groan.  She was the leeward tide prophet, which meant she read the sheltered side of things.  Windward told you what was hitting you; the lee told you what had already been decided.  Her other language was clouds.  She read their branching, the way a cumulus tore into tufts and each tuft tore again, and knew from that recursion how many hours the orchard had.  This evening the tufts were small and hungry and closing. 

The obstacle came out of that closing: a spar, ironwood, forty feet of it, torn from some drowned ship and riding the swell straight at the ring.  The raft families ran for poles and ropes, meaning to shove it off.  Maren had seen that before, eleven years ago, when she named a coming wave wrong and her brother went over the side lashed to the oldest apple, trying to save it.  Since then she had prophesied nothing, only weather.  Everything the sea threw at them had only ever been a wound or a tool, and she was tired of wounds. 

Wait, she said.  She knelt at the lee rail with a stub of charcoal and a square of sailcloth, and she sketched the resonance.  Not the spar: the note inside it, the node lines where the wood would stand still and the bellies where it would swing.  This was the whole of her craft, drawing a sound before anything struck it.  Her hand shook, then steadied, because the sketch wanted a certain length, and the length was not forty feet.  Nine feet, and a cut, and the offcut halved, and its offcut halved again.  The rule was the cloud rule and the apple-branch rule: every piece breeds two smaller pieces, and the smaller ones sing higher.  Fractal generating, her brother had called it, back when he still called things anything. 

Lash it, Maren said.  Not off.  Along the ring.  They looked at her the way people look at a prophet they have stopped paying.  Then the boy Bel, who had no tree of his own, took the first rope.  They took the spar in through the lee, the quiet side, in the last of the stillness, and made it a chord of the circle instead of a hammer against it. 

Maren sawed to her charcoal lines.  She halved the offcuts, and halved those, and hung the shrinking bars from the apple branches on tarred twine, longest to shortest, a ladder of wood going up into the leaves.  Her mallet was the last thing her brother had made, a xylophone mallet with a head of wound sailcloth, cracked now, too soft for bright notes.  She had carried it eleven years without striking anything. 

The swell came back.  The raft lifted, the branches shifted, and the bars began to knock against each other and against the twine, unbidden, a hundred small pitches with no key and no end.  It was beautiful and it would not settle.  Every time the ear reached for a home note, a smaller bar answered above it, and the question moved up into the leaves. 

Bel laughed, half frightened.  It won't finish, he said. 

No, Maren said.  Nothing afloat does. 

She struck the nine-foot bar.  The note came up through the driftwood into every sole on the raft, exactly where her charcoal had said it would be, and the apple trees took it and thinned it and passed it out to the water.  Her cracked mallet softened the attack, and she understood that the flaw was the voicing, that her brother had built her a softness she would need eleven years later. 

Prophets do not predict, she told Bel; they name a thing so it can be used.  She dubbed the spar Leeward, and the orchard rang with it, restless, unresolved, and loud enough to be heard by whatever was still coming.
