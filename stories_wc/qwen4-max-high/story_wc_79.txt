The gate rusted shut nineteen summers ago, and the city has not stopped moving since.  It stands out west, a lattice of iron between two dead shells, welded by salt into one orange scab.  Nine skiffs went through it before it closed, to learn what lay past the shallows, and none came back.  Sefa was six, and her mother was in the third skiff, holding a reed whistle cut from the same stalk as the one she left on the windowsill. 

Now Sefa is the city's spiral-shell cartographer, which means she reads the only archive it has.  Seven turtles carry the city, roped bridge to bridge, their shells terraced with gardens and kilns and the white bones of houses.  The turtles write their lives in the ridges of their scutes, a coil of thick and thin, notch and swell, and every coil is a route.  Sefa climbs down the flank of Old Ash on a rope ladder each morning, chalks the new growth onto linen, and translates.  Pattern languages, her mother called them: turtle coil, gull count, the tapped codes of the pump crews, the whistle tones that make a beast turn or kneel.  Nothing here is written in words; ink fades in this light, and the wind eats speech, so everything worth keeping is kept as pattern. 

She is not loud about her work.  The council stopped funding the sounding surveys years ago, so she pays for her own chalk and copies the coils at night by fish-oil lamp, and no one hears her complain.  What she wants is simple, and she has never said it aloud: to ask the watchers. 

They came the spring after the gate closed, nine pale uprights on the eastern horizon, and they have not moved once, not even in the storms.  Fishermen call them saints and will not look at them; the sun stands on them all day like a hand pressing a bruise.  Sefa has counted them in every coil for nine years.  No one else has noticed: all seven turtles, in different weathers, have laid down the same nine strokes at the same season, over and over, until the ridge goes hoarse.  In turtle coil, a repeated figure is not information; it is a question.  The turtles have been asking something for nine years, and the horizon has not answered, and the asking is turning them.  Her maps prove it: each year the great spiral of the city's course tightens one degree east.  At this rate the city will reach the watchers in her lifetime, and she does not think that will be a meeting. 

So she takes her mother's whistle up to the crown of Old Ash at noon, when the light is worst and the shell is warm as bread.  The whistle language has statements and commands, but a question needs a broken note, two pitches at once, and a whole reed cannot make it.  Her mother told her this on the last morning, kneeling, holding the twin reed: you can call, or you can ask, and asking costs the whistle. 

Sefa sets her knife along the grain and presses.  The crack runs the length of the reed with a sound like a small bone giving way.  There is no repairing it; there was only ever one reed left in the world cut from that stalk.  She lifts it and blows the interrogative, the nine-stroke figure the turtles have been growing, her mother's cadence, the third note doubled the way her mother always doubled it.  The broken note goes out over the water, ugly and doubled, a question with a splinter in it.  Below her the whole city keeps hammering and hauling in the glare, and no one looks up. 

For a long time nothing.  Then the horizon answers.  Nine cracked notes come back across the shallows, thin as heat shimmer, and they are in the whistle language, and they are in her mother's hand, the third note doubled.  But they are not an answer.  They are the same question, asked back, nine times, patiently, the way you repeat a thing until it stops being a question and becomes your name. 

Sefa sits down on the warm shell and understands that the watchers are not saints and not towers.  They are what is left of nine skiffs that stopped, and standing still is what asking does when nobody replies.  They cracked their reeds too, and asked, and nobody answered.  Under her palms, Old Ash lays down another stroke.  She takes out her linen and chalk, because someone must keep the record, and tomorrow she will cut a new reed and learn to crack it into a question that can be answered.
