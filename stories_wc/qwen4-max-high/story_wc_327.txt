In the canyon of endless echoes, nothing shouted ever stopped.  A cry bounced between the towers of bone and came back doubled, then quadrupled, until the air became a wall of noise that could stop a heart.  So the people of the towers learned to be quietly articulate, spending their words like coins in a famine.  Sela was the quietest and most precise of them, a copper-veined storyteller whose arms had been threaded with metal by the blue water of the mine where she was born.  The metal in her blood made her a strange instrument: when she laid her fingertips on carved bone, the grain traveled up her veins and rang faintly in her collarbone.  This mattered because Veth, their language, was not spoken but touched.  Its stories were grooves cut into the towers, and its grammar was roughness, notch, and polish.  When Sela was a girl, eleven people could read those walls.  By her fortieth winter there were three, and the children stood beneath nine hundred years of carved bone and felt nothing but cold stone.  A language does not die in a day; it dies one funeral at a time, and Sela had buried eight of its speakers.  Her obsession was simple and impossible: to translate texture into sound, so that Veth could be heard by anyone with ears and would not need a single surviving pair of trained hands.  She owned the only tool that could do it, and she was afraid of it.  The diamond had come down the trade road with a copper feather trapped in its heart, a flaw that made it sing unevenly when dragged across a groove.  The traders called it cursed and sold it cheap: for every word it gave, they said, it took one from the mouth that used it.  Her grandmother had tested it on a short prayer and afterward could not say the word for river.  Sela had tested it on a recipe and lost the word for spoon.  The flaw was the curse; a perfect stone would have scraped silently, and a perfect stone would have cost nothing.  But a scrape is a whisper, and a whisper in that canyon was swallowed by nine centuries of stored noise.  She needed to harness the echoes themselves.  The swifts showed her how.  They nested in the eye sockets of the towers, and their flock moved through the canyon like a single cloth being shaken.  Sela watched them for two winters and saw that they never flew where the sound doubled; they slid along seams of quiet and gathered where the air trembled but did not roar.  Their patterns were a map of the canyon's resonance, written in bodies, and she read them the way she read bone.  Following the flock, she found the tunnel of echoes behind the seventh tower, a throat of stone where a sound entered, folded, and returned to its own beginning, feeding itself forever.  There, at the seam the birds kept returning to, she pressed the flawed diamond into a handle of shinbone and set its point against the oldest wall.  She began to read aloud with her hands.  The groove's roughness became a rasp, the notch a click, the polish a long clean tone; the tunnel caught each sound, folded it, and returned it undiminished.  Copper rang in her arms; the curse took its payment word by word, and she kept dragging the stone.  She gave it the word for bread, and the word for mother, and the word for the particular gray of the sky before snow.  By dawn she had read four hundred years of Veth into the air, and the tunnel was holding all of it, a braid of voices that would not stop.  She lifted the stone while three words remained, because hearing is not yet understanding.  The children came running, because for the first time the towers were saying something they could hear.  Sela opened her mouth to explain and found it nearly empty.  She had spent her speech to buy the language a throat.  So she did what she had always done best: she took the smallest child's hand, laid two fingers on the bone, and used one of her three remaining words.  "Listen," she said, and the canyon said it back, and back, and back.  She kept her last two words: the child's name, and again.
