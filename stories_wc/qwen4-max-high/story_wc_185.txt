The caves had always sung, but they had sung in a key a body could sleep through.  Sela kept the maps of that old singing.  She was the ripple-mark mapkeeper, and her charts were not of tunnels but of water that had died ten thousand years ago.  Every ridge of fossil sand in the floor recorded a current, and where the ridges flattened, the current had gone still.  She had spent thirty years copying those flat places.  Stillness was her trade. 

Then the energy beings arrived, and the harmonic caves found a voice they had never used.  They came down through the sinkhole in a slow column, gently luminous, bodies of standing light with no edges to them.  They did not speak so much as persist, and their persisting set every wall ringing.  Within a week the village under the caves had stopped sleeping.  Within two, the first ceiling fell. 

Sela went to the arrivals and asked them to be quiet, and they dimmed in what she understood as apology, and could not.  The hum was their body; asking for silence was asking them to stop existing.  So she stopped asking and went to find the quiet in the storm, the way she had always looked for anything: in the record of things that had already survived noise. 

The echo chamber was the worst room in the caves and therefore the honest one.  A single dropped pin came back from it as a fist.  Its floor was a frozen pool, and the ice had begun to crack.  Sela lay on her belly with a lamp and spent four days examining crack patterns in ice.  The cracks were not random.  They radiated from certain points and avoided others; the crowded seams marked where sound piled up, the clear panes where it cancelled itself.  She scraped the ice away at one clear pane and found, beneath it, flat ripple marks.  The old water had stilled in exactly the places the new sound did.  Her maps had been a map of quiet all along. 

At the center of the largest pane sat the echo chamber stone, a grey lump the size of a skull, hollow, the throat of the room.  Everything the caves said, it said again, louder and later.  Sela fetched her chisel and her small hammer.  You cannot conjure silence by wishing at it.  You conjure it by giving the noise something to argue with. 

She struck the stone, listened to the delay, chipped a finger's width from its lip, and struck again.  Each blow made the room worse before it made it better.  She worked for eleven days, tuning the hollow throat until its returning voice arrived exactly against the arrivals' hum, crest to trough.  Her hands split.  The village complained about the hammering, and she kept hammering. 

On the twelfth morning she struck the stone once and did not strike it again.  The hush came out of the stone the way frost comes out of a nail.  It spread across the ice in fronds and feathers, a bloom of hush, and where it passed, the ringing did not stop but cancelled, and the ear found rest inside the noise.  The bloom reached the walls, went up them, and filled the harmonic caves like water filling a bowl. 

The arrivals drifted into it and their light went soft, gently luminous, no longer a glare.  They had not been attacking anyone; they had only been unable to stop, and now there was a place where stopping was allowed.  Sela slept on the ice with her hammer in her hand. 

Quiet is not a thing you find, she wrote later on the margin of her oldest chart.  It is a thing you beat flat, a little at a time, and then keep flat.  Every morning she went down and tapped the stone twice, listening for the drift, correcting it.  Every morning the bloom opened again, and the village slept, and the light rested in its bowl of silence.  She added a new mark to the legend: a small hollow circle, for quiet that is made and must be maintained.
