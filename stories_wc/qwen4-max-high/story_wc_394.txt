The acid sea ate iron in nine minutes, which the founders of the workshop considered a personal insult.  They answered by building their gears from bone and green glass, cutting cogs on ceramic wheels, and floating the whole clattering mess on a raft of fused bottles.  Sela Quist was born on that raft, and by nineteen she had done the one thing the elders swore was impossible: she bolted wings to a haulage cart and rolled it off the launch rail into the thermals boiling off the acid.  First cart glider in history, and the history was four hundred people long.  She was not brave, exactly; she was steadily curious, which is worse, because courage runs out and curiosity keeps asking for more rope.  It was curiosity that made her dredge the silt flats where the old world's bones surfaced, and curiosity that made her keep a black glass arrow tip she pulled from a lump of drowned clay.  A hunter's arrow tip, chipped ten thousand years before anybody thought to float a workshop on poison.  Glass does not dissolve; that is why it outlived him.  Along its edges ran tiny notches, too regular to be damage.  So she took it to the yearning algorithm.  Every generation of the raft had rebuilt the thing: knucklebones in a bowl, then pegged drums, then a loom of glass cogs that clacked like a hailstorm.  Its job never changed: feed it a fragment, and it computed the shape of what was missing, which is to say it computed wanting.  Through every rebuild, from bone to loom, it had been embarrassingly good at grief and useless at predicting weather.  You could not hand it scratches.  You had to transliterate them into the loom's notation, and the only reader was optical: a sealed tube of angled mirrors, a kaleidoscope the elders had built as a toy and then, sheepishly, promoted to instrument.  Sela clamped the arrow tip at the end and turned the tube, and the notches bloomed into wheels of symmetry, and she read them off through the images in a kaleidoscope, marking each figure onto the loom's cards.  Nine hundred and six notches.  Not kills; the spacing was wrong for kills, and the algorithm said so in its clacking, insufferable way.  Nights.  Nine hundred and six nights a hunter had sat by a younger, kinder sea, listening to a low tone that came up out of the water, waiting for it to say something else.  It never did, and he notched his arrow tip and died, and the want sat in the glass for ten thousand years like a held breath.  The loom finished at dawn and gave her its answer, punched in cards: an object, a mass, a curve.  A bell.  Tuned to the sea's low tone, an answer to a question asked before agriculture.  The elders pointed out, reasonably, that no one on the raft could hear that low.  Sela said that was fine, that she was not ringing it for them.  Life in the acid mist changed fast, broods every season, and the loom had run the lineage forward: throughout the evolution it kept finding, four or five hundred years out, a thing with an ear low enough to catch it.  She cast the bell from sea glass in the workshop's kiln, wide as a wash tub and thin as a fingernail, because glass was the only thing the sea would not eat.  She hung it in the belly of the cart.  For a clapper she lashed the hunter's arrow tip.  Then she rolled off the rail, and the thermals took her, and the cogs of the raft dwindled to a scatter of teeth below.  Out over the flat green water, where the tone came up, she pulled the cord above the old mooring buoy.  She heard nothing.  The sea heard, or something in it did: a ring of ripples spread out from beneath her, wide and patient, and far off a slick of pale things lifted their blind heads, not hearing it yet, only feeling the water knock.  Sela laughed at the sheer cheek of it: ten thousand years of waiting, answered by a woman in flying furniture.  She put the cart down on the buoy's flat deck, hooked the bell to the mooring ring, and let the wind take over the clapper.  It would ring out here for centuries, saying: yes, we heard you, we are late, we are sorry, we are coming.  The elders called it blasphemy; the loom called it a schedule.  She rolled off the deck, banked for home, and left the sea answering itself until somebody was born who could listen.
