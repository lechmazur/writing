After the first snowmelt, the bronze grotto began to speak in drops.  Each drop fell from a rib of ore and left a bead of lime behind, and Ilma came down with her balance to collect the weights of different burdens.  The meltwater ran off the village roofs, through the churchyard clay, and carried what people could not say.  A widow's grief came down heavy as a plum; a boy's lie weighed less than a moth.  Ilma was a fractal weaver, and weights were what she needed.  Her cloth repeated itself at every scale, a sorrow drawn large, then smaller inside itself, then smaller again, so that no single thread carried the whole of it.  To tune the pattern she had to know what each burden truly weighed.  The grotto was a whale's ribcage cast in bronze, and starlight came through the oculus above and lay along the wet metal like baleen.  She worked inside a shingled hush: courses of silence laid overlapping, each one covering the seam of the one below, so that whatever fell on her slid off and away.  Her lamp sat on the weighing stone, its kerosene burner turned low, the brass collar warm under her thumb.  She had trimmed the wick to a flat tongue that morning, and the flat flame gave her a clean edge of light to read the balance by.  There was one bead she had never managed to weigh.  Her son had gone under the river ice three winters back, and every melt since had brought him down here with everything else.  Every year she set his bead in the pan, and every year the beam would not settle.  The weight went down and down, smaller inside smaller, and she followed it the way she followed her own patterns, because following was the only love she had left.  That was how the grotto held her.  No wall, no door, no lock: she was boundary-lessly trapped, and there was nothing to push against.  Three snowmelts running she had come down here and gone home empty, having walked a grief with no edge, only ever another room the same shape as the last.  Now the drip was thickening.  Meltwater carries lime, and lime does not forgive; already the throat of the grotto had begun to calcify, a lip of stone closing over the way she had come.  By moonset the passage would be sealed, and she would go on weighing forever in the dark.  She lifted the lamp instead of the bead.  A fractal has no boundary, but it has an axis; her loom had taught her that when she was young and careless.  You do not escape a repeating thing by walking; you escape it by orienting properly.  She turned the burner in its collar until the flat of the flame stood edge-on to her eyes, and the broad light narrowed to a single bright line.  Then she tilted the lamp until that line lay along the star shaft falling from the oculus.  Flame and star agreed on one direction, and one direction is a boundary: everything that is not up.  She let the beam of the balance swing and did not follow it down.  She set her son's bead under the fattest drip and left it there to calcify, course over course, shingled into the floor.  Not lost, only held by something larger than her hands.  The stone would keep his weight, and the hush would shed the rest.  She climbed with the lamp held edge-on, keeping the line true, and the bronze ribs slid past her like the inside of a whale that had swallowed her and finally, slowly, agreed to let her go.  At the throat she had to turn sideways and breathe out, and the new stone took a strip of her sleeve as toll.  Outside, the snowmelt was running loud in every ditch, and the starlight was cold on the balance pan, which held nothing at all.  Tomorrow she would weave again, sorrow inside sorrow, but she would weave toward the star.  Below her the grotto laid its courses of quiet, one over the seam of the next, and the boy's small weight went on gathering stone.
