The package came on the tide, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with signal halyard.  Inside lay a lighthouse lens broken into nine wedges, each one still warm.  No sender, no note, only the harbor's old stamp.  The knot was Mira's, the one she used on the lamp lines, and he untied it slowly. 

Elias Vane had buried four hundred and six people in Kestrel Reach, and he was tired in the way that sleep does not repair.  He set the wedges on his embalming table, meaning to sell them for lamp glass.  That night they began to blink.  Three short, three long, three short, thrown across the ceiling in cold slivers.  The drowned had been sending distress signals for eleven years, since the sea took the lower city, and everyone else had learned to call it weather.  Elias had never managed that.  His wife's name was on the roll of the lost, and he had never written a date beside it, because a grave without a body is a sentence without a period. 

The blinking pointed down.  He followed it with a diving bell, a lantern, and the nine wedges in a sack, past the sunken steeples, into the library of the lower city, where a bubble of old air still held the ceiling up.  Impossible, and yet he stepped into it and breathed.  The air tasted of paper and cold lamp oil, eleven years old and still sweet.  The shelves stood dry to the third row.  Books swelled like drowned lungs, but the catalogue drawers were brass and tight, and the cards inside were legible.  And in the aisles, in the space between one shelf and the next, the drowned were standing.  Not swimming.  Standing, patient as patrons, flickering their hands in the old code, over and over, the same three pulses. 

He had spent eleven years reading that code as help me.  In the quiet of the reading room he heard it correctly at last: where do I lie.  They were not asking to be rescued.  They were asking for an address.  Elias sat down on the wet floor and wept, then took out his ledger.  The town called him stubborn; he had never once decided to be, he had only failed to stop, the way a clock fails to stop, and the failing had brought him here. 

He began to work.  A library is a machine for giving things a permanent location, and a call number is a headstone that anyone can find.  He hauled fresh air down in the bell each morning.  He drained the reading room, shelved the swollen books, and dried the cards over his lantern.  His hands cracked from salt; he slept in the bell and dreamed of filing.  He assigned each of the drowned a shelf, a letter, a number, and he wrote it in the ledger and on a brass tag, and he read it aloud so the code in their hands could stop. 

Then he built the lamp.  The nine wedges would never make a whole lens again, so he set them in a ring of solder and lead, gaps and all, and lit a wick behind them.  Broken glass throws a broken beam: nine crooked fingers of light reaching up through the harbor, into the drowned streets, into the drowned houses, saying here, here, here. 

The city came home to be catalogued.  There were more of them than the town had ever admitted losing.  They settled into the impossible spaces: the gap between shelf and shelf, the inside of a closed book, the air that had no right to be there.  And they were quiet, and being quiet, they were still.  Rest, Elias had learned, is not silence; it is knowing where you are allowed to stay. 

He wrote his wife's card last.  Mira Vane, third row, aisle four, and the date, and the period at the end of the sentence.  Something in the room let go.  He sat in the chair by the catalogue and chased the stillness the way he had chased it for eleven years, and for a moment it let him catch it. 

Then, far out in the black water, a new light began to blink three short, three long.  Someone else was lost and asking where.  Elias sighed, and trimmed the wick, and opened the ledger to a clean page, because the address of rest must be kept, and keeping is a thing the living do.
