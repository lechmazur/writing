The war had outlived its reasons, which is why no one could end it.  Raiders had burned the archive holding the first grievance two centuries ago, so each generation invented a fresher one and bled for it.  Marda Sarn had needed eleven years to muster both delegations onto the same ancient terrace at midsummer.  Now they sat on benches worn shallow by older peacemakers, backs to the valley they had ruined, watching three lights begin their slow dance.  Once in eighty-one years the moon, the red wanderer, and the white star came into a single line above the terrace notch, and for that one night both calendars agreed it was holy.  The alignment took six hours to close and would not return in her lifetime. 

Her instrument was an ebony clipboard, black as a closed eye, inherited from the negotiator who taught her.  Terms were chalked on the wood, never papered; paper could be pocketed, hoarded, forged.  Forty drafts had been written there and washed off again.  The washing was the ritual: cistern water, a sponge of lamb's wool, and the pale runoff drunk by the three who signed, so that the words went into the bodies that would have to keep them.  Marda had been a field surgeon before she was a negotiator, and her hands stayed operatively gentle, the gentleness that presses a broken rib back into place without asking permission.  She used that touch on clauses now. 

She had also, nine years earlier, traded the village of Ess for a relief corridor, and the corridor held, and Ess burned.  Two hundred people had paid for her arithmetic.  Since then she had wanted only one thing: to anchor her ideals in practice, to make a peace that cost somebody something, because free peace evaporates by autumn. 

The delegates had come ready to agree on everything except the debt.  Both peoples needed the war to have an author, a name to carry the two centuries, and each insisted the name be the other's.  The moon slid a hand's width toward the notch.  Marda wiped the board and chalked the borders again, the water rights, the shared market, the amnesty; the old men argued over whose grandfather had started it.  The red wanderer cleared the hills. 

Her teacher had told her that a treaty is a machine for turning grief into habit, and that machines need a bearing to take the load.  She had thought it a cold metaphor until she understood it was an instruction.  Below the last clause the ebony held one blank line, the length of a name.  Marda set the chalk against it.  She thought of Ess, of the smell coming down the corridor she had bought.  Then she wrote her own name, and under it, in the small letters of a legal finding, author of the war.  It was a lie; the war was older than her grandmother.  It was also the only lie both delegations could swallow. 

The upland speaker read it twice, and something in his shoulders came loose.  The delta speaker asked, quietly, whether she understood the sentence that went with the finding: exile from both banks, no burial in either ground, her name spoken each midsummer as the thing the valley had survived.  She said she did.  She had priced redemption before, in Ess, and had let others pay it; tonight the invoice came to her own hands. 

They signed while the three lights closed their line.  She poured the cistern water, and the wool sponge took the borders, the market, the amnesty, her name, and she wrung the pale water into three cups.  It tasted of stone and chalk and, faintly, of the sweet rot of a burning village, which she knew was only her memory being honest.  The old men wept and embraced, already rehearsing the story in which they had been merciful.  Nothing about it was clean.  A false name held the whole weight, and the peace stood on it the way the terrace stood on its cracked pillars, leaning, beautiful, load-bearing. 

At dawn she walked out between the two peoples who now agreed on exactly one thing, and the valley below her was quiet, and children who would grow up cursing her were asleep in it, alive.  She carried the empty board down the terrace steps, and it was the lightest thing she had ever held.
