During the reconstruction, the village boiled its laundry in the hot springs and argued about everything else.  Ash still lay in the gutters like gray snow, and the terraces above the fumaroles were being rebuilt stone by stone.  The elders wanted the roofs back, the walls back, and the Rite of Grafting back, exactly as it had been.  My grandmother wanted only two of those things. 

She had been an insurgent once, back when the Wardens owned the seed vaults and, through them, our lives.  At twelve, every child received an embryo in amber, a bead of resin holding one dormant seed of the lineage stock.  The Wardens slit the skin above your heart and set it there, and the seed woke slowly into you.  Vine meant weaver, resin meant builder, thorn meant soldier, and nobody asked the child or the seed.  They called it an implanted destiny, and they said it kindly, the way you name a shackle. 

The night my grandmother burned the vault, a bead shattered against her palm and left a splinter of amber under the skin.  She soaked the wound in the springs, and afterward she could hear plants.  Not words, she said, but pressures, the way a crowd leans.  The ferns wanted the shade gone; the moss wanted the pipe leak fixed; nobody had ever asked them either.  An accident made her a listener, and she never pretended it was a gift she had earned. 

The elders assigned me thorn, because my father had carried a rifle and their copied ledger said thorn.  My bead sat in the elders' cabinet, sealed, waiting for the first new moon of the rebuilt terraces.  She took it out without asking, held it to the lamp, and went quiet in a way I had never seen.  "It is not thorn," she said.  "It is asking for water and light and it does not know what it is yet." 

The elders told her that unsealing a bead was heresy, and she asked them who had written the rite.  Wardens, she answered herself, men who are dead, whose vaults burned with their houses.  "I did not fight so we could keep their handwriting," she said.  But she would not simply smash the amber, and that surprised me.  A seed killed is only another thing decided for it.  She wanted the rite kept and changed, opened on purpose, with a reason we could say out loud. 

The unsealing words had been the Wardens' secret, and she had spent forty years not knowing them.  Then the children, clearing ash from the dead pine above the fumaroles, brought down a kite.  It had hung there since the last festival before the war, its paper gone to lace, its tail intact.  Knots and dyed rags ran the length of that tail, and my grandmother had carried messages in exactly such knots when she was nineteen.  The Wardens had hidden their grafting keys in plain sight, at a children's festival, where no insurgent would look. 

She worked for six nights by the springs, steam curling around her wrists, counting knots against the old courier alphabet.  Three rags meant heat, a double knot meant hold, indigo meant the number of breaths.  What she decoded was not a spell but a schedule: warm the resin, wait, warm it again, and the seal lets go. 

On the night of the new moon the elders carried my bead to the upper terrace, where the water runs the color of weak tea.  My grandmother knelt in the mud beside the pool and asked them for one hour, and they gave it, because she had burned the vault for them.  She lowered the amber into the heat, counted breaths, lifted it, counted again, and the resin opened like a mouth.  Inside, a pale embryo, no bigger than a tooth, uncurled and reached.  She held it against my chest, and then she moved it away.  "Not thorn," she told the elders, "and not decided." 

I planted it myself in the warm silt at the pool's edge, where anyone could watch it and nobody could hide it.  That is the rite we keep now: the bead unsealed, the seed raised in the open, the trade chosen at twenty by the person and the plant together.  My grandmother says a tradition is a living thing, and you do not free a living thing by killing it.  The seedling leans toward me when I come, and she says that is only water, and she smiles like someone who knows better.
