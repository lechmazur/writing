Mira was a collector of lost souls, which in Echo Gorge meant a collector of unfinished sentences.  The dead who died mid-confession could not leave; the cliffs threw their last words back at them until the words wore smooth and meaningless.  Their confessions were spectral things, half voice and half draft, and they had one shape: an admission with no ending.  She had answered forty-one of them with a perfectly tuned harp and a grave face, and forty-one still walked the scree, repeating.  A true note only makes a confession solemn, and solemnity is a circle.  Her brother Tobin had taught her otherwise, and she had not believed him.  A confession, he said, is a wound with the lid off; tears wash it, but laughter closes it.  He kept a crocodile's tooth in his coat for the purpose.  The river beast had taken two of his fingers and left the tooth in his boat, and Tobin called that an even trade and laughed until he wept.  Mira had thought it a cruelty, dressed as wisdom.  Then the gorge took him in a rockfall, mid-sentence, and she inherited the harp, the tooth, and forty-one patients who would not stop talking.  For three years she carried the tooth in her pocket like a relic and never once used it.  She was modestly fearless, the way some people are modestly rich: she went into the places the echoes were thickest, she never boasted of it, and she was afraid the entire time.  What she would not do was pursue Tobin.  His voice worked the upper narrows, snagging on the same four words: I laughed, I laughed.  At their mother's burial he had laughed, one bright crack of it over the coffin, and Mira had not spoken to him for eleven months, and then the rocks came down.  She knew what a full confession would cost her: the whole reason, in her own ears, with no way to argue.  But the gorge was filling.  Unfinished souls crowd; the echoes braid; soon no single sentence could be heard entire, and then nobody in that canyon would ever go home.  So she climbed to the narrows to pursue her brother, carrying the harp and the tooth.  There is one hour in Echo Gorge when the echoes fail: the hush of a hidden sunrise, when the sun is above the rim but has not yet reached the floor, and the cold air stops moving.  In that hush a sentence can be finished.  She sat on the wet stone, set the harp on her knee, and wedged the crocodile's tooth under the fourth string, the way she had watched him do a hundred times and called cheating.  The harp went misaligned; the notes came out bent, sour, comically wrong.  She played his confession back to him through those distorted notes, and the gorge, which could only repeat what it was given, repeated it crooked.  "I laughed" slid up a quarter tone and became a hiccup, a goose, a hinge.  The spectral confession stumbled, and in stumbling, got loose enough to continue.  "I laughed," Tobin said, "because she told me to."  The hush held.  "At the end she could not swallow, but she could hear, and she made me promise that the last sound in her house would not be crying."  "So I told her the crocodile joke, and she laughed too, Mira, she laughed with hardly any breath left to do it, and I have wanted to tell you that for eleven months."  Mira sat with her hands on the sour strings, and the ache in her went luminous, the way water does when the sun is near it but not on it.  Then she laughed, one bright crack of it, exactly his.  The gorge, in its hush, could not throw it back; the sound went into him instead, and he finished, and was gone.  Light came over the rim twenty minutes later, ordinary and late.  Mira left the tooth wedged where it was.  She went down to the scree with the harp out of tune, and found the ferryman who had drowned owing his wife an apology, and played it to him wrong until he snorted, and swore, and let go.  Forty to go, and one hour of hush each morning, and she was afraid the entire time, and went anyway.
