The orchard stood on nine terraces cut into the sea cliff, and every terrace was also a corridor of the labyrinth, so the trees grew out of the walls like prisoners.  Marta had never asked to keep it.  Her mother ate one apple in a hungry autumn and forgot the way out of her own house, and Marta stayed to mark the paths because nobody else would.  The curse had come down into her hands.  Whatever her bare skin touched forgot itself: stone slumped to sand, and a man forgot his name and wandered until the frost took him.  So she guided the lost by leaving impressions without touching.  Every dusk she walked the safe turns with a stamp of dead ash wood on a pole, pressing splayed hands and arrows into the grey drifts, and travelers followed those ugly marks out to the road.  Nothing she touched could live, so everything she used was already dead: wood, wax, brass.  The mechanical birds were dead too, which was why she could mend them, and why they had never once obeyed her.  They flew the corridors in figure eights, wing edges honed, scattering her marks with their downdraft and shrieking at anything warm that touched the walls.  Her mother had said, before the forgetting, that the man who built the birds meant them to lead people out.  Then she forgot his name, and his blueprint went out of the world with it.  That evening the sea-gate bell rang twice: travelers inside, two of them, and the sky already going the colour of a knife.  Night frost forms in the labyrinth an hour after that colour comes.  After frost the drifts freeze hard as mortar, her stamp leaves nothing, and whoever is still inside stays inside.  Marta swore, gracelessly, and took up her pole and her wax.  Under the lintel of the ninth terrace, where no hand had reached in forty years, the builder had carved his plan.  She could not touch it, so she softened a cake of wax over her lamp, set it in the pole's iron cup, and pressed it up into the grooves and let the stone print itself.  The forgotten blueprint came down backwards in yellow wax: a throat, a comb, a governor, a screw.  Not a hunter's head.  A singer's.  She had the parts, swept out of the corridors over years and kept in a sack, because throwing away brass felt like murder.  She laid the wax on the bench and began to assemble, squinting at the mirror writing.  Comb into throat, governor behind the comb, dead brass under her scarred hands while the birds went round and round above her, honing the dark.  The last piece was the screw, and the screw wanted a nut.  She had one: a small nut, six-sided, worn smooth by eleven years in her pocket, dropped from the last bird her mother mended.  Left-hand thread, seven turns, her mother had said offhandedly, shelling beans, and been exactly right, as she was about everything she could no longer remember.  Seven turns backwards, and the throat closed on the comb with a click Marta felt in her teeth.  She netted the lowest bird as it came round, hauled it down screaming, and knocked its hunter head loose with the mallet.  The singer went on crooked and stayed crooked; there was no time to make it fair.  She threw the bird up.  It sang, a rusty grinding three-note thing, and the others fell in behind it along the safe turns, and their downdraft swept the drifts smooth for her stamp instead of ruining them.  She found them in the last of the light at the sixth terrace: a woman and a boy who had eaten an apple and no longer knew his own name.  The boy reached for her hand.  She stepped back, and turned the stamp, and pressed her ugly splayed hand into the ash ahead of him, and the birds sang the turn, and he followed the print, and his mother followed him.  They went out through the sea-gate with the first frost crackling behind them on the ninth terrace, silver on the cursed fruit, beautiful and useless.  Marta stayed, as she always stayed, hating it, and worked the small nut out of the discarded hunter head and put it in her pocket for the next one.  Above her the crooked bird sang its three rusty notes over the labyrinth, and nothing about it was lovely, and it would bring them home.
