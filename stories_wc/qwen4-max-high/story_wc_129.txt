Ilesu came up from the marsh with a bundle of reeds on her back and no word anyone still used.  That was the age beyond the murmur of lost languages: speech had gone soft and private, and the orchard people kept their meanings in marks.  Their trees were glass, grown from cuttings, and every family's branches had been spliced into every other's until the orchard hung above the shimmering crater in webs of kinship.  To belong there, you had to be grafted: someone had to cut you a slip of their tree and bind it to your stock.  Ilesu had nothing to bind. 

She sculpted in marsh reed, which was quick and warm and rotted in a season, and the orchard folk praised her figures the way you praise weather.  By spring nothing of her work remained but a smell of wet rope. 

Then the tag began appearing.  Three strokes and a hook, scratched small on trunk glass, on the crater's rim stones, once on the underside of a leaf where only a patient eye would find it.  Nobody knew the artist, who tagged the rim lanes and the orchard's little streets and never left a face.  Hunting the tag was the orchard's one common errand: children traded sightings, elders compared hooks in the dust, and strangers who had no shared words walked out along the rim together, pointing.  Ilesu joined the search because it was the only door left open to her. 

She walked with Berel, whose wife's branch had failed to take last winter and stood black among the bright ones.  The orchard's manners were gentle and evasive; nobody mentioned the black branch.  Ilesu was boldly sympathetic, which the marsh had taught her and the orchard had not yet forgiven.  She stopped at the dead tree, put her palm on it, and said the one word for grief that still worked, and Berel wept in front of her without shame.  Afterward he showed her the tag he had found that morning, scratched at the base of his wife's black branch. 

That was the pattern she had been half-seeing for weeks.  Every tag marked a failure: a graft that never took, a child cut off from the web, a name no mouth could still shape.  The artist was not signing walls; the artist was keeping count of the lost.  She told the orchard, and the orchard went quiet, and then it went looking harder than before. 

They followed the hooks inward, to the oldest thing on the rim: a glimmering obsidian arbor, black ribs bent into a bower, standing where the crater's light came up strongest.  No one remembered who had raised it.  Inside, at the crown of the arch, the tag was worn shallow by generations of thumbs.  Ilesu climbed and looked and began to laugh, and the laugh came apart into something else. 

The hook was not a letter.  It was a marsh knot, the binding knot her mother had tied to hold a splice until it healed, drawn by a hand that knew reed.  Between the obsidian ribs she found the old grooves where lashings had sat, and the dry ghost of fiber in the stone's teeth.  Someone from her drowned country had built this bower, bound it with reed, and been forgotten when the words went.  The reed had rotted, as reed does; the mark had outlived the language that could have said her name.  Berel said his grandmother had cut hooks, and her grandmother before her: the tag belonged to no single artist but was a duty passed hand to hand, like a cutting. 

Ilesu asked for the arbor, and for the hook.  She cut reed all autumn and lashed the black ribs, splice over splice, the way her mother had taught her, and the crater light came up through the lattice and made the whole bower glow like a held breath.  Then she dedicated the arbor to the woman whose knot she had inherited, and to every graft that had not taken. 

The orchard came, because a dedication was a thing you could do without words.  Berel tied the first length of reed beside hers, and then his neighbors tied theirs, and the black branch of his wife's tree was bound into the arch where the light would keep it.  They grafted her at last: a slip of the oldest tree, bound into the arch with her own knot.  The reed will rot; someone will have to climb and tie it again, and that someone will be kin.
