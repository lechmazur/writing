My sister taped the recall notice to the studio door, beside our childhood photograph of a split geode.  COHERE: all lots, discontinue immediately.  Adverse event: static discharge, then seizure.  June had swallowed it for eleven months so she could hear me thinking.  I drove her to every appointment and paid for the last two bottles, and I told her each time that the warmth she felt was her own longing wearing a costume.  That is my kind of skepticism: the kind that keeps paying.  The shock came here, in the studio, three weeks after she stopped.  She was holding the light wand while I opened the shutter, and the charge jumped from the aluminum handle to her thumb with a snap we both heard.  She sat down on the floor and cried, not from pain, she said, but because the spark had felt exactly like the pill's hello, and it was already gone.  In the dark I could not argue with her, which was a mercy, because I had run out of arguments that did not sound like cruelty.  Our studio is a blacked-out warehouse where we paint with light: long exposures, thirty seconds a frame, wands and fiber whips drawn through the air until the camera remembers a shape no eye ever saw.  Hidden pathways are our stock in trade.  We make them visible because the shutter is patient.  So, in the quiet weeks after the shock, I devised a protocol.  If telepathy had left anything behind, it would not be words; a recalled pill does not leave vocabulary.  It would leave the body's small verdicts: the gut drop, the prickle across the shoulders, the breath that catches before the mind knows why.  Somatic markers, the textbooks call them: feelings that arrive as sensation and decide for us in advance.  June would sit in the dark holding the wand, and I would stand behind the black curtain with an image in my head, and she would paint whatever her body did.  No guessing, no naming, no cheating with hints.  She agreed because she wanted to be argued with kindly for once.  For the lens I used the muscovite sliver we had pried from that geode when we were nine, a leaf of mica thinner than a fingernail, clear as pond ice.  Muscovite cleaves fractally precise: split it and you get two sheets, split those and you get four, each a thinner copy of the last, down past where fingers can follow.  As children we passed those leaves back and forth and called them thoughts, and we named the stone willowglass, because it bent like a willow switch and still let the sun through.  Taped over the lens, the sliver split every point of wand light into a branching ghost of itself, so any honest motion would record its own family tree.  Night one, forty trials: nothing but scribbles.  Night two, I held the memory of our mother's kitchen and June's stomach turned over, and her hand drew a slow spiral she said she did not choose.  Night three, I held the geode itself in both hands, and she painted a shape that stopped my breath.  Under the mica, the frame showed a stem of light splitting into leaves, and those leaves into leaves, self-similar, fractally precise, the same recursion as the stone in my hands.  Her body had known the image before her mouth could name it.  I am still not saying the pill worked.  I am saying my sister's body carries a map of me, laid down over thirty years of shared kitchens and shared silence, and that a chemical once made her notice it, and that noticing survives the chemistry.  That is what I wanted to illuminate: not a wireless channel between skulls, but the hidden pathway that was always there, wired through muscle and gut and the old habit of attention.  We printed the frame large and hung it where the recall notice had been.  June stands under it sometimes and rubs her thumb, remembering the spark.  Our hope is willowglass now: it bends, it is thin, you can see straight through it to the light behind, and it has not broken.  Tomorrow we run forty more trials, and I will keep the score honestly, because she deserves a witness who does not lie to her, and because the shutter, like love, is willing to stay open a long time in the dark.
