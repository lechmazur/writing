In the nebula garden, time is not measured in hours but across the quiet sigh of falling petals.  One sigh lasts as long as it takes a bloom's light to drift from branch to soil, and only in that while can the light be jarred and carried.  The blooms grow out of drifting rock, rooted in dust that never settles, and they glow because I made them glow.  I am the gardener who stays.  I keep things: seed husks, the shed skins of my daughters, and a brass gear worn soft, salvaged from the Ancestor Engine.  My kind is not human, but my sentimentality would be recognizable to any human who ever kept a dead mother's spoon. 

The Engine was built to calculate our transit through the dark heart of the nebula, where dust swallows every lamp.  Its final ratio never resolved; the number it climbs toward approximates infinity, so the answer never came.  Nine generations waited on that number.  Each polished the gear, wound the crank, and taught its children to wait exactly as it had been taught.  Before the Engine there was fire.  Our grandmothers burned lanes through the dust with torches, and the fire ate the blooms, and the ash fed the next garden, and the next generation burned again.  I remember, because I keep everything. 

So I bred a colder light.  A lifetime of pruning gave me petals that hold light the way a jar holds water, bright and bloodless.  To share light without using fire: that is my whole creed, and the reason my daughters are alive to leave me.  Beyond the dust there are settlements that still burn gas for light, and cough, and scorch their own nurseries.  My daughters carry the alternative in glass jars. 

Now the sigh has begun, and eleven of them hang in the dark, waiting for me to give them a number.  I hold the gear instead.  It is warm from my palm, and it smells of my mother's palms, and I love it more than I have ever loved any living thing.  That is the pattern: we keep the relic and lose the road. 

I set the gear on the soil, fold my hearing frills forward, and ping.  The sound goes out into the dust and comes back shaped like the world: a branch, a ridge, the soft hole where a lane begins.  The Engine wanted the entire path at once, perfect, and perfection out there is a number that never ends.  Sonar asks only for the next thing.  I ping; my eldest answers from thirty body-lengths out; her daughter answers beyond her, and the dark fills with small returns, a chain of voices laying a road one echo at a time.  Where each one passes, she leaves a glowing petal on the dust for the next, cold light handed down without a single spark.  No ash, no scorch, no burnt garden to feed the next forgetting. 

The petals are halfway down.  I call the youngest back and press the gear into her jar, among the light.  She asks what it is for.  I tell her it is for remembering me, and not for waiting on.  I know how that sounds; I know what my mothers told their daughters, and how a keepsake becomes a doctrine by the third generation.  But I cannot send her out with nothing of me in her hands. 

She goes.  The pinging recedes, one voice at a time, until the last return is too faint to shape anything.  The petals finish their sigh and touch the soil, and their light goes out the way a held breath goes out.  The garden is dark now, and quiet, and I have never been happier.  I ping once more, and again, keeping this end of the road open, and the dark hands my own voice back, unchanged and empty. 

Somewhere out in the lane, a daughter is already polishing the gear with her thumb, the way I did, the way my mother did.  It is a beautiful thing to keep.  I sit down in the unburned soil and wait for the next bloom, and I am perfectly, perfectly calm.
