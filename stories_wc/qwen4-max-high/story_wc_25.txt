In Karst Harbor the containers stood eleven high, and the corridors between them were accidents of the crane schedule.  Nadia greased their hinges, swept their floors, and replaced the lamps that made the maze survivable.  Her title was maintenance worker, grade three, and the tally clerks called her the mouse.  She could name anyone in the yard by their walk: the harbormaster's dragged heel, the customs boy's hurry, old Piet's two-step stumble.  Steel floors told her everything; steel walls, she suspected, would tell more. 

Her mother had said so, pressing a zodiac coin into her palm the winter she took the job: a crab, worn smooth, Cancer.  "Crabs hear with their legs," her mother said, "so stop shouting and stand still."  Nadia had spent nine years being talked over by horns. 

She kept her maps folded, never rolled.  Each night the cranes rearranged the labyrinth, so each morning she creased a fresh sheet into sixteen panels, one per corridor, and folded it until it fit her breast pocket.  In the dark she read it with her thumbs, opening one fold at a time like a mouth. 

The storm arrived on a Tuesday, a geomagnetic one, the kind that turns compasses into liars.  Green light rippled over the gantries; the radios went to static, the gate scanners died, and every lamp she maintained went out.  Somewhere in the stack, a ship's engineer named Tomas Reyes had gone in to check a refrigerated container and not come out.  Forty minutes in, the dispatcher ordered the horns and the search crews to shout his name.  Shouts bounced off eleven stories of corrugated steel and came back doubled, tripled, from every direction at once.  A lost man hearing his own name from six walls would walk toward the wrong one. 

Nadia found the dispatcher and challenged him: nine minutes, every horn and every throat silent.  "You'll find him by whispering," he said, not as a question.  "I'll find him by listening," she said.  "Nine years I've been telling you: whispers go farther in this place than shouts."  He laughed, then waved the crews quiet, mostly to be rid of her. 

She carried a brass key, four inches long, from the old padlock days; there was no lock left in the port that it opened.  She had kept it because of what it did to steel.  Drawn along a container's ribs, its teeth made a small dry stutter that ran the length of the box, into the next box, and the next, the way a whisper runs down a spine.  A shout scatters; a whisper stays in the metal. 

At the junction of Corridor Six and the empty bay she flipped the crab coin: heads for the reefer lane first, tails for the empties.  The maps had already narrowed the yard to two lanes; the coin only decided the order.  Heads.  She went left at a run, as if she had not planned at all. 

She unfolded the map to the fourth panel, thumbed the crease that marked the reefer stack, and pressed her ear to the wall with the key's stem between bone and steel.  In the new silence, three containers away, she heard a boot drag, recover, and drag again: a limp, not a loose strap.  She whispered into the seam, close as a kiss, "Tomas, hand on the wall, walk toward my voice."  Then she rasped the key along the ribs, three teeth, three teeth, a sound too small to echo and too stubborn to die.  Sixty feet away the dragging stopped, hesitated, and came on toward her, and by the second bay he was whispering back.  She moved backward down the corridor, whispering, rasping, folding the map one panel shut each time he cleared a bay, so the maze closed behind them like a hand. 

At the gate the aurora was still going, green as a bruise, and the dispatcher stood with his mouth open.  Seven minutes.  Tomas sat down on a bollard and cried, and the crane crews, who had shouted themselves hoarse, listened to a woman explain that steel carries a whisper better than air carries a scream.  She gave Tomas the coin, crab up, so he'd have something to tap against the wall if the yard ever swallowed him again.  The key she kept; it still opened nothing, and it had just unlocked eleven acres.  By morning she could name a new walk in the yard: Tomas Reyes, who now walked with his palm brushing every wall.
