The ivy had eaten the timetable, which seemed fair, since no train had stopped here since the flood.  I slept in the ticket office and mended kettles for anyone who climbed through the window.  Nobody had owed me anything in four years.  Our town told one story about itself: they forgot us.  The logistician arrived in the second week after the water, and the story changed in the sixth, in eleven minutes I will get to. 

Nobody ever learned where she slept.  She had moved insulin past three checkpoints and a broken bridge without issuing a single order.  Her method was rumor.  She mentioned to the barber's aunt that a refrigerated truck would sit and die at the weigh station unless a retired judge happened to be walking past at noon.  By Tuesday the judge was walking, the truck was rolling, and no one had been told to do anything.  That was her anarchy: exquisite manners, total ungovernability.  She said please to the soldiers while she quietly sent their pallets to the church basement. 

She joked without pause, badly, the way a person bails a boat.  "Why did the flood cross the road?" she asked.  Nobody laughed, so she laughed enough for the room.  "I am not elusive," she said.  "I am simply terrible at goodbyes, so I skip to the leaving." 

Her single law was reciprocity.  She would not drink my tea without leaving batteries, a hinge, a rumor of work.  "Loneliness is not the absence of people," she said.  "It is the absence of anyone who owes you a Tuesday."  She had carried that theory through nine disasters, testing it the way other people test a bridge.  So she wove debt through the valley like wire, and the valley held. 

In her coat she carried a fragment of moon rock, a grey chip in a plastic capsule, pulled from a museum fire two countries ago.  For two years she had tried to give it away.  Nobody would take it, because you cannot answer the moon with a kettle or a hinge, and a gift you cannot answer is a door that opens only outward.  That was her whole disease: she could bind everyone to everyone except herself. 

So she used the stone to catalyze us.  She told a boy at the pump that the rock came from the moon, and that whoever held it must give something away before morning.  The rumor went where her trucks could not.  It crossed the river with the ferryman's daughter, doubled back through the choir, and acquired a clause about soup.  Within a week the chip had passed through forty hands, dragging forty small debts behind it: eggs, a haircut, a patched roof, an hour spent sitting with a dying man.  It came back to the station in the fist of the barber's aunt, who set it on my counter with the manners of a woman depositing a bomb. 

Then the logistician came for her coat, and the story changed.  Eleven minutes, as promised.  She put the capsule in my palm.  "A gift, please, no arguing, I have a truck at nine."  I opened the drawer and dropped it in.  "I will borrow it," I said.  "You will have to come back for it, and I will not post it, and I am unreliable about phones." 

She laughed, and it came out cracked, because she understood the arithmetic.  "That is a debt," she said.  "That is a Tuesday," I said.  "Four o'clock, kettle on, ivy to cut."  "Even is only goodbye with better manners." 

She stood in the doorway with a joke loaded and nothing to fire it at.  She said thank you like a woman setting down something heavy.  The truck went at nine. 

She came back the next Tuesday, and eleven Tuesdays after that, always apologizing, always carrying batteries.  The capsule sits in my drawer, borrowed and never returned, and she never asks, and that is the cure: not love, not company, only a debt someone intends to keep.  The town tells a different story now: we are the ones who give.  The ivy goes on eating the timetable, very courteously, and no one has told it to stop.
