Maren tuned bells on the whispering bluff above the cloud sea, where the wind arrived in syllables it had lost somewhere below.  She was the last tuner the valley kept, and she tuned for harmony: nine bronze throats that had to agree before anyone would believe them.  Words had become unnecessary three generations back, when the chords proved harder to lie in.  A symphony of truth was simple arithmetic to the ear: one voice sounding what it was, eight answering, no seam anywhere for a falsehood to hide.  But for ninety days each year the cloud rose over the lip of the bluff, and the bells went seasonally mute.  Frost packed their mouths; the damp swallowed every strike; the valley below sat in a white silence and told itself nothing.  Maren had been born in a mute season, and she knew what it cost.  People did not lie in the silence; they simply stopped being astonished, and their eyes went the flat gray of the cloud.  Her apprentice, Odile, was eleven, born in a mute season like her, and had never seen the color of wonder whole.  In the snow cellar behind her workshop lay the reason she had climbed down through the cloud every spring of her life: an iceberg fragment, blue as a struck note, older than the valley's first word.  The glacier had stopped calving blue ice nine years ago; what remained in the cellar was all the wonder left in the world, and Maren had rationed it by the ounce.  Each mute season she cut a sliver, hung it in the wind, and watched it glisten for a week before it wept away.  A week of blue, then eighty-three days of gray.  Odile had learned to look at the sliver politely, the way one looks at a relic.  Wonder could not be rationed; Maren understood that now, filing her tools with a quiet, stubborn speed.  She would spend the whole fragment at once, and she would spend it moving.  The way was through kinetic sculpture: a wind-frame on the bluff's edge, vanes of hammered tin, arms balanced on a knife of bronze, and at the hub the iceberg fragment, cut into a lens.  Sound was dead for the season, but the wind was not; the whispering bluff never stopped talking, it only stopped being heard.  Odile held the ladder while Maren bolted the frame, and asked her question by tapping the tuning fork against her palm: why spend it all?  Maren answered by pressing the girl's hand flat against the ice until she flinched from the cold, then pointing at the vanes.  The cloud came over the lip that evening, and the bells went quiet in their towers, and the frame began to turn.  It did not ring; it glistened.  The lens swung, caught the flat white light, and broke it into that old blue, and the blue ran across the frost, across the tin, across Odile's face.  The girl made a sound that was not a word and was not a chord either, and Maren let it stand.  All that season the sculpture turned, telling the truth in motion: this is the wind's weight, this is the hour, this is what light does when someone troubled to catch it.  The valley climbed the stair to watch it, and climbed down quieter, and came back.  The ice wept, as ice does; Maren caught every drop in a bronze cup and poured it into the mouth of the greatest bell, where the frost took it and held it.  By the eightieth day the lens was a thumbnail, then a tear, then nothing.  Odile struck the loss-note, and Maren shook her head and set the girl to work hammering tin.  The frame would need new vanes; the glacier might calve again; a hundred years is not long to a mountain, and someone would be here to climb down for it.  On the ninetieth morning the cloud fell away below the bluff, and the frost let go of the bronze, and the nine throats found each other again.  The great bell spoke last and slightly wrong, a hair flat, sweetened by the blue ice frozen in its lip, a note the valley had never heard and could not doubt.  Maren did not correct it.  Harmony, she had learned, was not the absence of a strange note but the willingness to make room for one.  She stood in the wind with her hands quiet at her sides and watched Odile hear it, and the color came up in the girl's face like something remembered.
