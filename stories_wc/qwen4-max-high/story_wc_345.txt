The nebula of respite hung over Halloway, our orchard world, like breath on glass, and for eleven years it kept the seasons calm.  Ships came out of it emptied of everything but people; we took the people in, the strangers became family, and then the respite began to thin.  The floating rock arches drifted lower each season, dragging their shadows across the rows, and the wells sank a finger's width a week.  That was when the chalk started.  Nobody in Halloway walks the sidewalks at dawn; we are all in the terraces by then, so whoever wrote there had the streets to herself.  MERA, DIG THE SWALE BEHIND THE DRY HOUSE, SIX FEET, NO DEEPER.  I dug, because I am stubborn, and because the message was strangely trustworthy, the way a hand on your shoulder in the dark is trustworthy.  At four feet I hit clay, and at six I hit water.  By the next dawn there were nine messages, and by the third there were forty, each one a job, each one signed with the same small crooked V.  Osei was told to unroof the packing shed and lay the tin under the north arch, where the fog dripped.  The twins were told to carry blossom-nettle up from the gully, bucket by bucket, for the butterflies that pollinate the late pears.  Nobody asked who was writing.  We had learned in eleven years to take what the nebula gave and not look too hard at it.  But I keep the settlement's ledger, and a ledger will not let a thing stay unexamined forever.  I reexamined the first winter.  Ilsa Vey, last name off the last ship, dead of the cough before the pears ever set fruit.  I had remembered that we voted her water ration down to half to save the grafting stock, and that she cursed us from her cot.  The page said otherwise.  She had signed her ration over herself, in a hand with a small crooked V, and written beneath it: for the trees, they will need shade before they need me.  Every pear we had ever eaten came out of that woman's thirst, and we had spent eleven years remembering her as bitter.  I went out at dawn with a lamp and waited in the cold beside the dry house.  She was thin as the nebula, chalk in her fingers, and she did not stop writing when I spoke her name.  "You could have asked us for this," I said.  "I asked," she said. "You were tired. Nobody hears a stranger."  "You are not a stranger now."  She kept writing.  "The respite is ending," she said. "I cannot lift a bucket. I can only tell forty people where to set theirs, and be believed."  "That is all a ghost is good for, and it is enough, if you do the rest."  So we did the rest.  We swaled the terraces, roofed the cistern with packing tin, and hung fog-nets under every arch, and the children carried nettle until the butterflies came up the gully in a ragged yellow river.  It was not clean work.  Bramble took our forearms; two arches came down in one night and crushed the south rows, and we buried Osei's brother under the stones he had been netting.  In the last dry week I carried the watering bell out to the butterfly garden the twins had made beside the dry house.  It is a copper bell you sink mouth-down in the soil; you fill it, it rings once, and then it weeps water to the roots for days.  Ilsa had carried it aboard wrapped in a shirt, and for eleven years we had used it as a doorstop.  I filled it from the new cistern, and it rang, thin and clear, under the sinking arches.  Every hand in Halloway stopped, because we had agreed that the bell meant come.  They came with buckets.  The pears will be small this year, and the nebula will let go of us, and the arches will keep falling.  She is thinner every dawn; soon the chalk will be mine to write.  I have been practicing her crooked V.  Family is the ones who keep telling you where to set your bucket, even after they are gone.
