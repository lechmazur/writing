After the symphony ends, the quay empties, and the real harbor comes upstairs.  Mira Solvig brokers cargo for a living, which means she knows what a ship's hold truly carries and what the manifest pretends it carries.  Owners call her the Needle: she finds the loose thread in any contract and pulls.  She also knows that the gulls prefer the north bollard, that the customs clerk doodles tiny schooners in his margins, and that the library above the old salt house has no roof.  Books sleep in waxed cases; readers sit under stars, in a breeze that turns pages by itself.  The orchestra plays below for people who can afford to sit still; the ones who load the ships hear it from the cranes.  Wren, the night copyist, unlocks the doors at eleven, when the sweepers and crane women can finally come.  Mira met her through a mistake.  In March an envelope reached her office addressed to N. Halloran, Ledger Clerk, Pier Four, the numeral smudged from nine.  Halloran had been dead since autumn.  Inside, in a careful hand: thank you for the coins in the return slot, and for never signing your name.  Mira could have forwarded it to the dead letter box.  Instead she wrote back, admitted the wrong address, admitted Halloran was gone, and asked who had needed the coins.  Wren answered: her father's lungs, two winters ago.  They kept writing, and kept addressing the envelopes wrong on purpose, because the mistake had been the door.  In June, Wren brought the locket.  Halloran had left it to the library, which meant, Wren said, to whoever would open it.  Inside was a photograph: forty-one men and women on a deck, squinting, unnamed.  The Marigold, lost in the squall of '09.  The quay's bronze plaque lists six officers.  Halloran's brother is somewhere in the locket, third from the left, and Halloran spent thirty years afterward leaving coins where nobody would trace them.  Someone had once paid his passage home; he never learned who.  Mira understands ledgers, so she understands this: kindness that cannot find its creditor goes looking for a debtor.  She is careful, though.  She has been ruined twice by generous men, and she distrusts her own delight the way she distrusts a manifest that balances too neatly.  So she tested it small.  One charter, one clause: a tenth of a percent of freight, booked as sundry labor, payable to the night fund of the roofless library.  Nobody argues with sundry labor.  It is the line where the port hides its people.  The first payment bought an inhaler, a winter coat, and paper for the customs clerk's boy, who doodles schooners too.  By August she had written the clause into every charter she brokered, and told the shipowners it was standard.  Two refused; she moved their cargo to men who did not.  Then she went backward.  The harbor keeps every manifest since 1871, and hired hands were logged as units, forty-one of them, each with a pay chit and a name.  Mira spent nine nights in the waxed-case dark with Halloran's tally book, matching chits to faces by the order they stood on the deck.  Wren held the lamp and remembered which ones had come up to read.  Forty names by the fourth night; the forty-first took five more, because he had signed with a thumbprint and a nickname, Gull.  She found him in a berth roster, spelled wrong.  She paid the engraver herself.  Tonight, after the symphony ends, the sweepers climb the stairs and find the locket photograph blown up to the size of a sail, and beneath it forty-one names cut into fresh brass.  The breeze takes the pages; nobody chases them.  Wren asks who paid for the brass.  Mira does not say, because Halloran taught her the trick of never signing your name.  She is grinning anyway, carefully, like a woman carrying full cups up a stair.  In the return slot lie three coins and a note asking who to thank.  Mira writes the only honest answer she has: the man who never signed his name, and forty-one people you can now read.  She addresses the envelope wrong, on purpose, and slides it under Wren's lamp.  The breeze turns the page; the harbor, upstairs, keeps reading.
