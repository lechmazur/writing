The kettle went on the stove at four, the way it had every afternoon since the funeral.  Vera gave herself until it whistled.  Grief was a lion; you could work it, but you did not turn your back on it, and you did not stay in the cage past closing. 

Rain beaded the skylight above her and ran down the iron ribs, and the light it let in was rust-hued, the color of the wagons that had wintered in this barn.  The loft had been the harness room once; now it held her jars.  Nine of them stood on the shelf, because nine was the number her hands knew.  Nine lions in, nine lions out, counted at the gate every night for twenty-two years while Emil cracked his whip and bowed.  She had been the one who counted.  The cats had gone to a sanctuary in Ohio; she still woke at the hour of the count. 

The jars were her method.  You could not keep a man, but you could keep time: syrup supersaturated, a string, a seed, and week after week the sugar laid itself down in glass facets until the hours had a shape you could hold.  Crystallized time, she wrote on the labels, and under it the lesson.  Around his rosin block: WARM HANDS, STEADY VOICE.  Around a brass button from his coat: CHECK EVERY LATCH YOURSELF.  That was the harvest she wanted.  Not the sorrow, which was chaff, but the small hard usable grains inside it, threshed out and stored against winter. 

For three months she had not known what to seed the tenth jar with.  Emil had not died under a lion, which was the death everyone had braced for and quietly rehearsed.  He died on a Tuesday, in a hospital corridor, of a clot the size of a pea, while she was parking the car.  That morning he had asked her whether she still loved the work, and she had said she would answer him after they parked.  Here, and then gone, in the time it took to find a quarter.  That was the lesson she could not get to lie flat in a label.  She had never once screamed; she had filled out forms, and the forms had been correct. 

The kettle ticked as it heated.  On the bench lay the ash blank, a squared arm of wood he had bought for a new chair leg.  The old chair stood in the corner with its short leg shimmed by a folded matchbook.  Ash bends; ash does not splinter into a cat's pad; he had chosen it for the same reason he chose her, because it held under pressure.  He had also promised, one winter, to carve her a lion out of the offcut, and had never done it.  Here and gone: the promise, the wood, the man. 

Vera took up the knife.  She was not an artist and did not intend to become one at sixty-one.  She cut the way she had counted, in order, checking her work.  Four legs, a block of a body, a head no bigger than her thumb, a mane suggested by six notches.  It was a poor lion and an honest one.  The shavings curled onto the floor, pale and smelling of the barn in summer. 

The syrup she had made at dawn stood in the tenth jar, cooling, still waiting for its seed.  Rock candy grows on a wooden skewer; every child knows it; the sugar needs something rough to hold to.  She had known it for weeks and had refused to know it.  She hung the lion in the jar on a string and screwed the lid down.  By Friday it would wear a coat of glass; by month's end, a lump of amber light with a lion buried in it. 

Contained, not kept.  That was the difference she had been missing.  You cannot keep a thing that is here and gone; you can only contain the time it stood in the room.  She wrote the label: HERE AND GONE, BOTH TRUE.  Under it, the lesson, in her counting-hand: SAY IT IN THE CAR. 

The kettle whistled.  Vera lifted it off the flame, poured, and sat down in the rust-colored light with her tea and her ten jars.  Outside, the rain went on beading and running; inside, the sugar began, invisibly, to take hold.
