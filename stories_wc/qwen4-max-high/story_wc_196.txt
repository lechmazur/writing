The dust in the foster file room kept better records than I did.  Two years after emancipation, the Bureau still sent me its children's papers, and I still could not find my own son among them.  His name was Willis, and he was sold south in 1859, and the ledger that carried him burned.  So I learned to write unknown where other clerks wrote dead.  Unknown is a door; dead is a wall.  I kept the door open for strangers, since I could not close it for myself.  My title was archivist; my work was grief, arranged alphabetically. 

The shelves held four thousand foster files, each one a child bound out to a family that wanted work more than it wanted a child.  The room smelled of paste and mice, and in the evenings the lamp made the shelves look like a hillside of graves.  In the margins I kept a second ledger, unofficial, of small kindnesses: a farmer who taught a boy his letters, a woman who saved a girl's shoes for Sunday, a name written correctly on purpose.  The Bureau counted rations and bonds; I meant to magnify the mercies, to make them large enough that a later reader could not miss them. 

In October a girl named Delia came to the counter with her father's name and regiment written on a scrap.  She had been eleven when he enlisted and thirteen when the word came that he had died at Petersburg, and she had never believed the word.  She had grown up in one of these files herself, which is how she knew to ask.  I searched the catalog and found nothing, which meant only that a clerk had once been tired.  I told her the truth as I had learned to tell it: that I could not promise her father, but I could promise the looking, and I would keep the looking honest.  She said she could wait, and she came every Thursday, and I did not tell her to stop. 

That winter I began reading the shelves instead of the papers.  Dust settles evenly, so any unevenness is a sentence: a clean rectangle where a box had stood, a fan of finger marks where a hand had swept, a pale arc where something had been dragged out and pushed back wrong.  Behind the last press, where no catalog reached, the dust lay thick except for one narrow trail, as if a flat packet had been slid in and left.  I reached in and brought out letters that had never been filed, forty or fifty, all from men in the United States Colored Infantry, all asking after children.  Someone had shelved them out of sight because answering them would have taken a year. 

I sat on the floor and began to unfurl them, one at a time, the creases so old they tore along the folds like a hem giving way.  I held every superscription to the lamp and read it against her scrap.  In the ninth letter, something hard rolled out and rang on the boards.  An infantryman's button, brass, the eagle worn smooth, the I for infantry still legible in the shadow of the wing.  The letter said: Tell my daughter Delia that I cut this from my coat at Camp William Penn so she would know the hand that sent it, and if I do not come, tell her I asked for her every month I was a soldier.  He had died at Petersburg after all; the word had been true, and it had also been almost nothing. 

On Thursday I put the button in Delia's palm and closed her fingers over it, and I told her what the letter said, and I did not soften the part about his dying.  She asked whether he had spelled her name right, and he had, twice.  Then I copied the letter into the file, and beside it I wrote the kindness plainly, in a hand large enough to be read by whoever came after: he asked for her every month. 

There are forty-eight more letters, and I am working through them, and the dust will tell me if there are others.  Willis is still unknown; I have not made him dead, and I will not make him found.  But I have learned what the room can say, if you let it: not who lived, not always, but who was loved, and by whom, and how long they went on asking.  That is not the whole history, and I write it anyway, in the largest hand I have.
