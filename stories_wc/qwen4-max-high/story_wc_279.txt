The mist came into the grotto the color of bruised lilac, and Pim chalked a whale on the wet stone anyway.  It was the third whale that week, and the tide had taken the other two.  He drew them with top hats and unnecessary eyelashes, and beside them a hopscotch grid for his sister Nell, who had drowned at nine and never learned to skip. 

This was the second spring of the unlearning, when the coast towns were taught to let go of the names of the drowned.  Grief, the assessors said, was a habit, and habits could be unlearned like a knot untied.  In his pocket Pim kept a flake of tile the size of a thumbnail, lilac glaze on one face, bare clay on the other.  It had fallen from the whispering gallery, the curved dome that carried a breath from the north arch all the way to the south, so that a fishwife could tell a secret to a stranger forty feet away.  He had picked it up the morning the whispers first thinned, and he had assumed the dome was simply dying.  A crack, a flake, a failing curve: a tidy explanation, and wrong. 

Pim was a fool with chalk, but he was never a fool about people.  He noticed who stopped speaking, and in what order.  The fishwives went first, then the boys who mended nets, then the children, who had only just learned the names and found them easy to put down.  Nobody had ordered silence.  That was the trouble: the hush had grown on its own, out of a thousand small politenesses, each person swallowing a name so as not to embarrass the person beside them.  It moved through the town like weather that people make.  He could not prove it until he read the sand. 

Old Sela still swept her mandala on the grotto floor every dawn, rings and spokes and little gates, though the words that used to go with it had been unlearned along with the rest.  Pim had always loved the mess of it, the spilled edges, the footprint somebody left, and he had always wanted the mess to mean something.  Now he crouched with a stub of chalk and copied the patterns, and the patterns told him.  Sela smoothed a ring flat for every visitor who came and said nothing, and left the sand ridged where a name had been spoken aloud.  She thought she was only tidying.  But the bare rings spread outward from the north arch in a spiral, in exactly the order Pim had watched the town go quiet, and the ridged sand had shrunk to one small gate at the center.  One ridge left, one speaker, and by evening the pattern would close. 

The mist thickened until the grotto felt like the inside of a lilac, sweet and close and slightly wrong.  Pim stood at the north arch, where every whisper in the grotto begins.  He had spent two years wanting the flood to add up to a message, some proof that the drowned had left a meaning in the silt for him to find.  The sand had finally given him one, and it was not from the drowned at all.  It was a portrait of the living, drawn by their own small refusals. 

Below him Sela knelt with her broom, tired, ready to smooth the last ridge herself.  He knew the shape of the moment the way he knew a room: one breath of hesitation, and then the hush would seal itself for good, polite as a closed door.  So he interjected.  He held the flake to his mouth like a small cold lip and spoke into the north arch: "Nell, who could not skip, who counted the ropes."  Then, because he was who he was, he added the rhyme she used to get wrong, and the silly tune with it. 

The dome was cracked, not finished, and the whisper crossed the curve and arrived at the south arch whole.  Sela did not smooth the last ridge.  She said the name back, badly, the way the unlearning had left her mouth, and then a boy at the mouth of the grotto said it too.  The hush did not shatter; it frayed, the way a habit does, one answered word at a time. 

Pim set the tile flake into the sand at the center, glaze up, a small lilac interruption the broom would have to go around.  Then he chalked the grid again, crooked, where the tide would take it by morning.  The mess meant nothing on its own, he thought, and that was fine; meaning was the part you answered.
