At 4:11 the farm glowed like a slow fire nobody had bothered to put out.  Mira watched it on eleven monitors, from the office she had fortified fourteen months ago.  Bioluminescent oyster caps, June's strain, breathing green through the racks.  Outside, the ridge held its birdsong; the echo would not come down the valley for another hour. 

Mira was an intrusion detection specialist, which is a polite way of saying she was paid to distrust movement.  After June died she had wrapped the farm in thirty-four sensors and called it grief's practical cousin.  The co-op had hired her to keep the strain from being stolen; she had instead built a mausoleum with excellent logging.  She was spatially gifted the way some people are musical: she could hold the whole building in her head as a volume, walk its lattice, feel a door open as a change in pressure.  So she knew the night crew only as shapes: Badge 4 who limped, Badge 7 who sang to the racks, the driver who idled nine minutes at the gate.  In her field they were called familiar strangers, the traffic you see every day and never once greet.  Her systems trusted them because they repeated, not because they were known.  June had known everyone's name; Mira had known everyone's vector. 

At 4:19, Zone Nine breached.  No door contact, no heat, just two hundred grams of new matter where none had been.  She unfolded the building in her mind and went to the place the geometry pointed.  The packing shed wall, poured concrete, six inches, no penetrations.  And it was glowing.  Green thread ran the hairline cracks, and on the far side, in the dark she had locked, three caps had fruited out of the floor.  Mycelium does not believe in walls; it believes in pores.  June used to say the substrate was one animal wearing many rooms. 

In her coat pocket, Mira's thumb found the laser pointer June had used to trace spore drift through the dark.  The button was worn to a shine.  It was a dead man's switch: the beam lived only while you pressed.  She pressed it, and a red dot crossed the concrete, and the green ate it.  Fourteen months of holding that button down.  Her thumb had a callus from it, a small hard coin of skin.  She had wanted, since the funeral, to understand the physics of letting go: where the thing goes when you stop supplying it.  The answer was embarrassing in its simplicity.  Release the button and the dot dies, but the light already loosed keeps going until something takes it in.  Nothing is lost at the boundary; it is only handed over. 

Behind her, Badge 7 said, "I opened the vents."  He was standing in the doorway with a crate of caps against his hip, and he did not run, which she noted the way she noted everything.  "June let them out at four," he said.  "So the spores reach the hillside before the birds stir."  Mira asked his name, and it was Teo, and it took eleven seconds, and she felt the whole lattice of the farm rearrange around that small admission. 

She sat down on an upturned crate and rewrote the rule set until the sky went grey.  Not walls: membranes.  Barriers that admit and watch, that let the familiar stranger through and learn his name, that measure what passes instead of pretending nothing can.  Permeable, and therefore honest.  The architecture she sketched that night would, within three years, revolutionize intrusion detection, though the papers would never mention mushrooms, or a woman's thumb on a worn button. 

At 5:02 she walked the perimeter and unsealed the vents herself, all nine, and propped the packing shed door with a brick.  The green went out into the hillside like a fire that has decided not to hurry.  Teo sang to the racks.  Mira put the pointer on the shelf where June's tools lived, and did not press it, and let the last of the red go where it had already gone.  Then, from the ridge, the first bird, and its echo coming down the valley to find the doors open.
