The flood had a name by then, and Vera resented it.  Naming turned weather into a character, and characters wanted sympathy.  She had come home to strip the needless drama out of her mother's greenhouse, and out of herself.  Three days, one dumpster, no ceremony.  Vera measured pulsars for a living.  Neutron stars: city-sized corpses spinning six hundred times a second, keeping better time than any clock humans had built.  She preprocesses reality; she always had, converting a room into an inventory before she let herself stand in it.  Her father met her at the door with a towel over his shoulder, drying nothing.  "Roof's half gone," he said.  "Don't make a fuss."  She had taught him that, or he had taught her; the direction had blurred.  The greenhouse stood behind the house, where the waterline still marked the siding a dirty brown.  Two panes had blown inward, the frame had racked, and the benches sagged like tired animals.  Her mother had raised monarchs here for thirty years, and the last of them died in April, three weeks after she did.  In the dusk inside, Vera clicked on her flashlight and swept the beam across the surviving glass.  The dust there caught the light and threw it back in colors.  Not gray: faint bronze, faint green, the tiled shimmer of something that had once been alive enough to fly.  She knew what it was.  Wing scales, shed a few thousand at a time, each one a shingle smaller than a pollen grain.  Dust from butterfly wing: the slow decay of everything that had flown here, settling since April in the still air behind the glass.  She leaned closer, and her training took over, and she was grateful for it.  The dust lay in patterns, and patterns were her language.  The flood had splashed a silt line level across the panes three weeks ago, a datum she could trust.  Above it the wing dust had survived the water, and over that lay a thin gray film, three weeks of ordinary falling.  Near the vent crank a clean fan had been wiped through the film, again and again, by the same sleeve reaching for the same handle.  Inside the fan, new dust had settled, unbroken by any hand, six times deeper than the three-week film.  Through accumulated dust patterns she could date the week her father had stopped coming out here.  Not the day of the funeral; that was drama, that was a spike, and spikes were easy.  Eighteen weeks back, near enough: six weeks after the funeral, on some ordinary Tuesday, he had set down the crank and not picked it up again.  Vera sat on the wet bench and let the flashlight rest on her knee.  In her papers she called sudden spin-ups glitches and removed them, because they told you about a star's crust, not its life.  The life was in the spin-down: the relentless, unremarkable decay, one part in a billion a year, that no one applauded and no one could stop.  She had built a career on the truth that gradual change is the real event, and she had never once applied it at home.  The glass had been keeping its own ledger all year, in dust, and it had kept it better than she had.  Her father's boots sounded in the doorway.  "I keep meaning to get out here," he said.  She did not tell him what she had read; there was no need to make him watch his own grief in a graph.  "The frame's sound," she said.  "The glass is mostly whole."  "It's worth keeping."  He looked at the roof, then at her, and something in his shoulders came down an inch.  She fetched the soft brush from the potting shelf and a paper envelope and took a sample, because that was how she loved things.  She wrote the date on it, and the pane number, and the word scales.  Then she handed him the brush and took the dustpan herself, and they worked along the bench together, slowly, clearing the spring and summer an inch at a time.  Her father hummed while he swept, tuneless, and she did not correct anything.  This was what remained when the drama was stripped out: a brush, a pan, his hands, the work.  It would take longer than three days.  It would take, she suspected, the rest of the year, and she found she was not sorry.  Nothing here had been ruined in a moment; it had been carried away a scale at a time, and could be carried back the same way.  Under the flashlight the dust still glimmered, patient, unimportant, and true.
