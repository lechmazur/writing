The cemetery gardener whistled while he worked, because the dead never asked him to stop.  Corwin was blithe in the way of men who have made peace with the only neighbors they will ever have.  His gravel paths ran true, his shears hung in descending size, and every grave he planted was tenderly organized: snowdrops for the infants, rosemary for the soldiers, mint where the mourners would brush their sleeves.  Above the hill stood the cobalt silo, glazed the blue of a bottle held to a candle, and under stars it seemed to hold the night rather than grain.  Ilse the weaver had lined its inside with her tapestry, and the silo had learned to answer.  Speak into it and something came back in a voice you almost knew, from a direction nobody could name: uncharted echoes, the town called them, and the town stopped going up the hill.  Corwin went every Thursday, because he had been left at the cemetery gate as an infant, and the silo was the only mouth in the world that might have swallowed his mother's goodbye.  He did not want her name.  He wanted the shape of her intentions, the way a hand shapes a bowl before the clay hardens.  Ilse had refused to chart the echoes while she lived.  She played chess at the silo door each evening against old Baptiste, and she told Corwin that a chart handed over too early turns a question into an errand.  "Wait until the chess clock runs down," she said.  In October her flag fell mid-game, and Baptiste, who could have reset it, simply closed the board and went home to die of the same winter.  The clock had run down; the condition was met.  In the drawer of the chess table Corwin found an antique sovereign case, silver gone soft as a moth's wing, its lid engraved with shears.  The shears on the lid matched the ones stamped on his tool belt; she had left it for him.  Inside, the coins had been drilled through and wound with silk, seven sovereigns turned to bobbins, each color labeled in Ilse's tidy hand.  He carried the case up the hill into a gloom like velveteen, plush and light-swallowing, where his lantern made a small brown room of the dark.  The tapestry hung in a cylinder around him: the cemetery in wool, every path he had ever raked, and along its border a mess of frayed threads.  Ilse had never finished the border; she had left it knotted, waiting for the hands that came after the clock.  Corwin knelt with the case open beside him and began to untangle.  It was the same work as his flower beds: separate, order, label, and let the pattern declare itself.  By decoding the frayed threads in the tapestry's border he found that each knot was a grave row, each fray a worn place where an echo had thinned to almost nothing, and each silk color a word Ilse had used in her ledger: meant, feared, promised, withheld.  The chart was not a map of voices but of intentions, and it took him until the stars swung west to read six rows.  The seventh thread was black, and it ran to the gate.  It was frayed nearly through, one filament holding.  He stood where the border told him to stand, faced the seam of the silo, and said, "I am the one you left."  The echo came back slow, from low down, in a young woman's breath rather than her voice, and it said nothing so useful as a name.  It said, "Wait here, I will come back for you," and then it said it again, and again, wearing thinner each time, the way a promise wears when the person keeping it dies on the road.  Corwin sat in the velveteen dark with the sovereign case in his lap and understood that he had not been discarded, only interrupted.  Toward dawn he mended the black thread with the last of Ilse's silk, so the chart would show a promise kept late instead of broken.  Then he went down to the gate and planted rosemary there, tenderly, in rows, because someone had meant to come back and he intended to be easy to find.  He whistled while he worked, and the silo, charted now, hummed along a little behind him.
