The pilgrim arrived after dark, which was the only way anyone ever arrived at the singing cave.  Snow fell behind him; inside, water struck the crystal and the whole mountain answered in chords.  Selenite grew from the walls in blades and needles, and every drop that struck them rang a different note, so the cave had been playing one unfinished piece since before there were ears. 

Sibila had seen him coming for eleven days: the boots, the grief, the question he practiced on the road.  Clairvoyance was the least interesting thing about her.  She had spent fifty years telling kings about wars, and what she wanted now was smaller and harder: to magnify everyday magic.  Bread that stayed warm through a long walk.  A kettle one note sweeter. 

The unseen forces of the cave could do such things, if you gave them a door.  Having no bodies, they could inhabit only what stood in doubled shadow, where two lamps' darknesses overlapped.  Sibila had hung the second lamp herself, on a chain, at the height of a standing man.  In that overlap she read the forces' pressing shapes, and called it the future.  She had never read her own; you cannot watch the door and stand in it at once, and she had always preferred watching. 

The pilgrim unwrapped a chipped blue cup.  "My husband stirred his coffee in this every morning," he said.  "Three taps on the rim.  I am losing the sound of it."  Not resurrection, then; only the small music of a kitchen.  Sibila nearly laughed with relief. 

She waded to the drip pool and lifted out the cave pearl.  Nine hundred years of falling water had wrapped one grain of sand in ring after ring of calcite, until it was plum-sized and faintly luminous.  Every drip the cave had ever played lay layered inside it, the way a tree keeps its summers.  The drip was steadily unpredictable, constant in its coming and never twice the same, which was the cave's genius and the reason her prophecies were always slightly wrong. 

She set the cup where the two shadows crossed and laid the pearl against its handle.  The darkness doubled, thinned, became a door.  The forces came in like cold air under a sill and inhabited the porcelain and the pearl.  Centuries of stored water-music poured through the cup and narrowed to one sound: tin against clay, three taps, morning.  The pilgrim made a noise that was neither weeping nor laughter.  He pressed the cup to his ear like a shell.  It was the bright kind of dark in there, the kind that hands back every glimmer you give it. 

"What would you like magnified?" he asked.  No one had ever asked her that.  Before she could refuse, he reached up to steady the chain lamp for her, and stood at her shoulder holding it.  The swinging lamp moved the overlap onto her, and she was standing in it.  The forces turned and inhabited her. 

The ordinary went loud: yeast in her sleeves, the tea she had forgotten and could still taste, the mended seam of her coat, her own breath going in and out like a tide she had never once listened to.  And she saw tomorrow, which held no kings at all: herself at the pool, listening, hungry for breakfast.  She had foreseen his boots, his grief, his cup, eleven days of road.  She had not seen it coming, and she laughed, and the cave took the sound and kept it. 

"Keep the lamp steady," she said.  "I want the whole minute."  He held it while the cave played a chord it had never played before and would never play again. 

Afterward she wrapped the cup for him, which would keep its three taps now, and kept the pearl.  By morning the water would have laid one more ring around the grain of sand, and inside that ring would be three taps on a rim, a stranger's kindness, and a laugh she had not predicted.  Fifty years of looking ahead, and the best thing she had ever held was a stone that only remembered.  She let the second lamp burn all night, so the dark would keep its door open.  Whoever came next, she would ask them what they wanted magnified, and she would not look ahead to find out.
