Ines was the city's natural history illustrator, and she drew the fauna of the nap: moths that hatched in a snore, ferns that unrolled between breaths.  Her plates were the only record, since nothing here survived the giant waking.  One plate stayed blank.  She had labeled it years ago: Echo, undescribed.  To draw a thing, Ines had to find where it was born.  In a wicker cage above her desk she kept her mother's last laugh, netted from the air of the dying room.  An echo, caged, repeats itself forever and never goes home.  Released, it flies back to the place where echoes are born, and a patient woman with a sketchbook might follow.  Ines had been patient for eleven years, and cowardly for just as long.  To uncage the laugh was to spend it, and she had always chosen the loop over the answer.  Her mother's pendant lay on the blank plate: an infinity symbol stamped in tin, the sign of the bell guild.  It meant play it again, and never let it finish.  The city survived on an interrupted cadence.  Every dusk the tower bells climbed toward the chord that would close the lullaby, and every dusk the cantor turned them aside at the last instant.  A phrase left hanging sculpts time itself; the giant's nap pooled in that gap, soft as clay, and the whole city lived inside the suspension.  Her mother had turned the bells aside for forty years, until the night her hands stopped.  Her mother's whole life had fit inside one nap; the giant had not yet turned over.  Now an apprentice rang the changes, badly, and the giant had begun to twitch.  Water came up the towers in screw lifts and ran along rooftop canals, aqueducts of tarred pine weaving between the spires.  That evening the canals ran low and warm, the way they did when the sleeper beneath them started to surface from a dream.  Ines took her cage, her sketchbook, and the pendant to the lock keeper at the ninth tower.  The belfry was six towers off, and the nap had maybe an hour left in it.  So she did what ferrymen do: she borrowed time.  The keeper drew it against her own last hour, pouring it into her wrist, and warned her that time is never made, only moved.  The debt took her hand first: the knuckles thickened, the skin went to paper, and the pencil felt like a fence post.  She poled east under laundry lines and copper rain catchers, while the towers leaned past like the ribs of the thing they stood on.  Twice the water shuddered and dropped a hand's width, and somewhere below, a lung the size of a parish filled halfway.  In the cage, the laugh kept looping, four notes and a catch of breath, eleven years old and not a day older.  The belfry door knew the pendant; the lock turned for the loop the way a phrase turns for home.  Inside, the apprentice was already climbing the rope, and the bells were three notes from the end.  He could not turn them; he had never learned how to leave a thing unfinished.  Ines understood then what her mother had spent forty years doing, and why the guild wore a knot with no end.  You cannot hang a chord on nothing; the ear needs somewhere else to go.  Ines uncaged her mother on the belfry roof, the highest point in the city.  The laugh went out over the parapet, and did not loop.  It ran west along the canals, and every wall it touched gave it back a little changed, and Ines followed it with her eyes as it climbed into the bells' unresolved gap.  The apprentice hauled; the bells swerved onto the laugh instead of the tonic, and the cadence broke open and stayed open.  The giant sighed and sank, and the water rose under the boat.  In the hanging gap, where the phrase refused to land, Ines saw them being born: echoes, pale as fish fry, spinning off the unfinished chord by the hundred.  Her mother's laugh was among them, going out into the world to be a hundred other sounds.  That is where echoes are born, she wrote, in the moment a thing does not finish.  She drew with her ruined hand, badly, honestly, the first plate of the new nap.  Below her the canals filled, the towers steadied, and the city bought itself another sleep.  She hung the loop around her neck and went down to teach the apprentice how to leave a thing unfinished.
