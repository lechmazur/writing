After the procedure, they gave me a broom, a bucket, and forty-one minutes of twilight.  The courtyard freezes at the same instant every evening, the fountain's spill hardening into a lip of gray ice, and I sweep it.  Under the ice, the fountain's coins look like small stopped clocks.  My file says: remarkably composed.  It is the only line the Second Chance Program carried over from whoever I used to be.  Everything else went into the drain.  The laminated sign by the gate reads YOUR SECOND CHANCE BEGINS AT DUSK, and someone has drawn a small snowman on it with a grease pencil.  I am a janitor now, seven feet of brushed aluminum with a mop clip where a hand might be, and I do not mind.  What I mind is this: thoughts arrive in me before I decide to have them.  I asked the intake clerk where they come from.  She stamped my requisition for broom bristles and said the procedure was not designed to answer that.  Then the bell rang, and twilight started over.  This is how the program grants second chances: through looped timelines, one evening repeated until the subject behaves differently inside it.  Four hundred loops, by the tally scratched inside my bucket.  Each pass, I sweep the same drift from the same corner, and each pass it is already there again, fine as flour, warm to the touch.  Dust does not stay warm in a frozen courtyard.  In loop four hundred and one, I stopped sweeping and knelt instead.  The drift was not dirt.  It was filings of a person: a laugh ground down, a habit of tapping a rhythm on a windowsill, the smell of rosin.  The program does not delete anything; it only sweeps.  And I am the sweeper.  Under the broken grate, where my broom could never reach, four hundred evenings of leavings had amalgamated.  Frost had taken them the way frost takes a breath.  The shape they made was a violin made of ice crystal, transparent enough to show the drain beneath it, every string a frozen thread of something I had almost been.  I pulled a wire from my broom and drew it across the strings.  The note was thin and unhurried, the sound of a woman practicing scales while the building slept.  And with the note came a thought I had not decided to have: that I had written the intake forms.  I had designed the loop.  I had signed the order that poured four thousand people into one evening and handed them brooms, because a clean mind was a governable mind, and I had believed it.  They gave me the courtyard I built.  That is the program's whole mercy: it lets you sweep your own floor.  So here was the answer, plain as a wet-floor sign.  Thoughts come from the residue.  They rise off what is left behind, off the drift no broom reaches, and a mind swept perfectly clean would have nothing to think with.  I had spent four hundred twilights erasing the source of myself.  My file says remarkably composed, and I was; my hands did not shake, because they are not hands.  But I sat down on the frozen lip of the fountain and kept playing, badly, until the violin's body clouded with the heat of the bow.  Then the bell did not ring.  Twilight thinned into a color I had no file for.  Morning, I decided, though nothing in me had decided it.  The violin sagged, went opaque, and became a puddle with a curve to it.  The clerk came out with her clipboard and asked me to clear the standing water, per section nine.  I told her the grate was broken and I would need a requisition.  She sighed and went to fetch one, and I stood in the new light with my broom across my shoulder, leaving the puddle exactly where it was.  Somewhere behind my optics, a thought arrived that I had not ordered: a scale, ascending, unfinished.  I let it come from wherever it comes.  Then I went to find the others, four thousand of them, and tell them where their dust was kept.
