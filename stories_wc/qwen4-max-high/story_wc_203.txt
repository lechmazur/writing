The stucco facade of the Ferris Building had cracked in one long seam above the entrance, and under the seam Ada could see older paint: gold letters on brick, spelling TICKETS.  The demolition survey called it a warehouse, vacant since 1971.  Facades lie; cracks do not. 

Ada repaired elevators for a living, twenty-two years of shafts and door tracks, and she read a building the way other people read faces.  Her method was dust.  Dust records every draft, every door, every hand, and it never bothers to lie. 

Inside, the freight shaft had been cut straight through the abandoned circus ticket office, an oak booth left standing in the lobby like a tooth in a jaw.  She laid her flashlight flat across the counter and raked the beam.  The dust lay an inch deep except for one clean crescent, swept by the same sleeve, in the same arc, for fifty years.  There were no footprints under it.  Whoever still worked that window had stopped weighing anything. 

At six the demolition crew's generator timed out, the overhead fluorescent buzz died off, and the ghosts came up out of the silence like divers.  That buzz had always drowned them, and Ada had spent her whole career grateful for cheap ballasts. 

The ticket seller stood at his window with both arms raised, palms flat to the ceiling, wearing the face of a man holding a roof off a child.  He weighed nothing at all, and he was carrying something enormous. 

Ada had been nine the first time she saw a dead woman staring at a ceiling, and she had wanted, ever since, to know what ceilings hide.  Bodies in shafts she understood; it was the overhead she never trusted. 

She engaged the inspection switch and rode the car top up at crawl speed, past the booth, until she could reach into the joist bay above its plaster.  Up there the dust was undisturbed except for a clean circle the size of a saucer, and two handprints kept fresh beside it.  In the circle sat a horologist's mainspring barrel, brass, plum-sized, its arbor still wound tight. 

She brought it down, set it on the counter, and engaged him the way she engaged a passenger stuck between floors: plainly, and without promising anything. 

"You repaired watches in the off-season," she said.  "This came out of the big clock." 

His mouth moved half a second behind the words she heard.  His cuffs were shiny at the elbows, and his hands were a watchmaker's hands, small and exact. 

"I wound it every night," he said.  "When the clock ran down, the season was over, and that year nobody came back from the road."  "So I pulled the barrel out full and put it where nobody would find it."  "A spring that cannot unwind cannot finish." 

Four ounces of brass, holding fifty years of force, and a man holding the brass. 

"If I let it down," Ada asked, "will you let go?" 

He looked at the plaster the way the living look at a door they have been leaning against for years. 

She clamped the arbor in her channel locks, eased the click free, and let the spring walk itself down against her grip, tooth by tooth, until the brass went slack and warm in her hand.  It sounded like a long sigh in a very small room. 

The plaster did not fall.  It had never been holding anything but him. 

He lowered his arms, and on the counter the clean crescent began, at last, to take dust. 

Ada pocketed the empty barrel: four ounces, nothing, the heaviest thing she owned.  The dead weigh nothing; that is the whole trouble. 

Outside, the generator caught, the fluorescent buzz came back, and the lobby was only a lobby.  She wrote "nothing concealed overhead" on her inspection sheet, true by then, and let the survey stand: by Friday the crack in the facade would be rubble, and the gold letters with it.  All the way home she kept one hand on the barrel, and thought about the ceilings of the city, and the quiet weight they were asked to keep.
