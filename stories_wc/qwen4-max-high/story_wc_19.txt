My father sold silence by the carat.  He lined his bunkers with gold leaf, because gold does not ring, and the rich paid him for a hush so complete it felt like being swallowed by a coin.  I was the flaw in his craft, the bunker builder's child whose chest contracted at the click of any latch.  He called it a smallness of nerve.  I called it hunger.  Silences have flavors, and I collected them.  Cellar quiet tasted of wet iron; snow quiet of peeled apple; the quiet after an argument, of burnt sugar.  My father's gilded hush tasted of nothing at all, which is the flavor of a thing with nothing alive left in it.  He began gilding the winter my mother died.  Grief, gold-plated, is still grief.  I kept my collection in a tiny porcelain doll, hollow, with a pinhole mouth my mother had drilled for me.  The trick was limiting exposure.  Uncork her mouth for one breath, no more, and the silence inside kept its own taste instead of thinning into the general noise of the world.  A silence is intangibly tangible: nothing you can hold, yet it presses on the tongue like honey, like a thumb.  That autumn my father decided to cure me by locking me one night in Vault Nine.  He chose the night of the Inversion, when gravity reverses for an hour and loose things fall into the sky, because he believed the safest place for a frightened child was underground.  I went up instead.  Above the cloud deck, on the spine of the mountain, strangers had built a trellis.  It was xenial by design: an open lattice of lashed poles, no walls, no door, a lantern lit for whoever climbed, and a net overhead to catch whatever the Inversion tried to take.  Travelers grafted vines there and left them for the next traveler.  I climbed with the doll in my fist, my breath going short and wide, short and wide.  At midnight the world let go.  My braid lifted, the lantern flame stood up off its wick like a planted flag, the clouds turned into a ceiling of milk, and the sky opened beneath me like a well.  The trellis held me the way a hand holds a kite string.  For that hour the vines sprout.  They wait all year for the pull to reverse, then throw their shoots into the open, growing a hand's width a minute, reaching for whatever is not a wall.  That was the silence I had come for: the hush of an hour with no floor, above the clouds, where nothing is shut.  I wanted a quiet I could carry into small rooms.  I opened the doll's mouth one breath.  It came in weightless and heavy at once, and it tasted of cold grapes and open doors.  Then my father's head came through the trapdoor, his hair standing toward the stars, his mouth open on my name.  He had come to haul me back to the gold.  So I did what I had never done: I opened her mouth all the way.  Everything I had corked came out at once, over-exposed, spilling thin and wild into the lattice: cellar iron, snow apple, burnt sugar, cold grapes.  I gave away nine years of listening in four seconds.  He tasted them hanging head-down, and his face came apart.  "Mine has no flavor," he said.  "You gild it shut," I said.  "Nothing that cannot breathe has a taste."  We hung clipped to the trellis until the hour ended and the world took hold of us again, gently, the way you set down a sleeping child.  Then we went down.  He stripped a hand's width of gold from Vault Nine and drilled a pinhole through the plate, the way my mother drilled the doll.  Over the shaft he lashed a trellis and left a lantern lit for whoever came.  Vines sprout down through it now, into the dark, a hand's width a minute.  I sit inside one hour at a time, no more, limiting exposure, and I taste what comes through the pinhole: cold grapes, open doors.  The doll is almost empty now, and I am almost full.  The hush has a flavor now.  It stretches.  I do not contract.
