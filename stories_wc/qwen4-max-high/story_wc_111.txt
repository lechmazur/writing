The assayer came in April, when the maze was leafing and Anselm was not.  Sixty years of pruning had made him resemble his work: brown, knuckled, bent along the wires.  His lungs had gone to paper; the doctor gave him a season, perhaps two.  Luck had been measurable for eleven years now, and the Verrall estate sold its reading the way it once sold wine.  "The estate assays at nine grains," Perrin said, setting her brass stand at the maze's heart.  At the heart, where the famous vine grew, she measured two.  The buyers were paying for nine, and the heir wanted the heart-vine dug up and shipped to them in a crate, the hedges left to whatever luck remained. 

Anselm had known this would happen eventually, because he had planted the lie himself.  In his first winter he had taken a cutting from a ditch outside the gate and told the old count it came from a monastery famous for its luck.  It was a pragmatic lie: the count funded the maze, and the maze fed Anselm, and the ditch cutting grew.  No one was permitted to touch the monastery vine, so the heart became the one place in the maze his hands had never worked.  He had been nine that winter, and hungry, and the garden had already known him better than anyone.  The dresser before him had died in the maze's third turning, and Anselm had finished his pruning that same week, because vines do not wait on grief. 

Now he wanted the instruments to say what he had always known: that a garden knows its gardener, and that sixty years of attention could be weighed.  He had waited eleven years to confess, until there was a machine that could testify for him. 

"It's ditch stock," he told Perrin.  He watched her face do the arithmetic of ruin.  "Then the whole assay is wrong," she said.  "The assay is right," he said.  "It's just not where you're standing." 

He led her out of the heart and into the turns, eleven of them, four hundred paces of hornbeam with no straight line anywhere.  In his coat he carried a tobacco tin of marbles, every one cracked.  Assayers calibrated their instruments with glass spheres and threw them away when the flaws spoiled the standard, and Anselm had been collecting the discards for eleven years.  When a graft took, or a frost passed without taking the tips, or a gap in the north hedge closed at last, he wrote the date in his ledger, named the victory aloud to the hedges, and polished one cracked marble with beeswax until it held light again.  Then he pressed it into the gravel at the turning where the work had been done.  A cracked sphere holds no standard, but it holds a place. 

"Marbles," she said.  "Cracked stock is worthless to your guild," he said.  "That made a hundred and twelve celebrations affordable."  "Read them." 

She carried the brass stand from turning to turning, and the needle climbed at every place his hands had been.  At the marble for the north hedge it rose highest of all, and the hedge there was thick as a wall, blooming while the man beside it withered.  "The luck isn't in the vine," she said slowly.  "It's in the attendance."  Her chart and his ledger agreed: every rise sat beside a dated victory, and the untended heart accounted for nothing. 

The lie was mended, not by unsaying it, but by telling the rest.  Perrin wrote her certificate for the maze entire, and named its source in the margin: Anselm, vine dresser, sixty seasons.  The buyers could purchase land; they could not crate the attendance. 

By June he could no longer hold the shears, so Perrin, who had stayed, held them for him and he told her where.  He asked for the last ceremony, and she gave it elaborately: the ledger open, the date named aloud to the hedges, beeswax and a cloth.  "What victory is this one?" she asked.  "That it knew me," he said.  She polished the cracked marble until it held light, and set it in the gravel at his turning, where the maze, blooming, closed gently over the gap he left.
