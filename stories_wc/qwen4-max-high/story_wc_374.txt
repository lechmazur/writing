The lavender bluff had been a landing field once, and the wind still smelled of scorched tin.  Vesna climbed it every autumn, while the forests held elections.  Below her the aspens were voting on winter, ballots of sugar and hormone passing root to root, and the whole valley hummed with the argument.  Nobody knew yet when the leaves would fall; the trees had to decide, and the trees were slow and honest.  Vesna glowed.  Her wrists and throat carried the old lighthouse graft, a remedy given to children born in the long dark, obsolete now that lamps flew.  She was the last luminous woman in the district, and the last skeptic in the Bureau of Ambient Coincidence.  Her work was to disprove the weather-carriers.  Everyone knew one: a man who trailed drizzle into weddings, a girl who dropped the pressure in a room until ears popped.  The village called them blessed or cursed, and Vesna, who had been born under her mother's private rain, called them unexplained.  Her mother had drowned in a flood that followed her own footsteps, and the district had voted to remember her as a saint, which Vesna considered an insult with flowers on it.  She wanted a mechanism.  She wanted to know why some people carried weather.  The graft had threaded her with iron and light, and it gave her one instrument: her tongue.  When the field shifted, her mouth changed.  Copper meant pressure falling; salt meant a carrier within thirty paces; tin, thin and sweet, meant something she had tasted only twice in eleven years of climbing.  On her finger she wore her grandmother's tin compass ring, a cheap thing from the Analog Revival, its little needle loose in a cloudy window, and it had never once found north.  Grandmother had called it an emergent compass.  One ring was junk; ten thousand rings, worn by ten thousand people walking toward the same market, the same funeral, the same idea, made a needle that pointed at the gathering.  Vesna had written that off as poetry, which was the family disease.  Tonight she had asked them all to converge.  Eleven carriers, lettered and paid, climbing the bluff from eleven villages, and the aurora was already up, green sheets folding over the sky like laundry nobody had brought in.  She had told them it was a survey.  It was an ambush of the truth.  At the crest the lavender was in its second, engineered bloom, silver-purple and stubborn, bred to hold the bluff's soil against the storms that the carriers' grandparents had supposedly summoned.  Salt flooded her mouth before she saw the first of them.  Then copper.  Then a taste like a spoon left in rain.  They came up through the flowers with their weathers folded around them: fog, a dry crackle of static, a small honest snow.  The boy with the snow was nine and apologetic.  As they closed on her, their separate weathers stopped being separate.  The fog thinned, the static died, the snow stopped, and every one of them looked up, relieved, ordinary.  Vesna's tongue went to tin.  The ring's needle quit its lazy circle and pointed inward, at her own wrist.  Above the bluff the aurora bent, and she understood the scale of the thing: iron in the soil, iron in her blood, the sun breathing on both, one circuit running from a lavender root to the edge of the sky.  The old manuals had a phrase for it: terrestrially cosmic, dirt that thought in stars.  No one carried weather alone.  Weather was carried by everyone a little, the way a vote is carried, and it pooled in whoever stood where the lines crossed.  Her mother had not been cursed; she had been a low place in the field, a woman who listened to everybody, and the sky had drained into her.  Now eleven people were listening to Vesna, and the ring had found its north at last, and it was her.  A cold weight gathered along her glowing wrists.  Snow began to fall out of a clear aurora, only around her, six feet of private winter.  She laughed, which she had not done in eleven years, and the taste in her mouth turned to tin and honey.  Below, the aspens finished counting.  The valley let go of its leaves all at once, a million small decisions converging into one sound, and the sound came up the bluff like applause.  The boy stepped into her snow and held out his hand for the ring.  She kept it, and told him what to taste for, and promised to teach him the rest while the forests argued again in spring.
