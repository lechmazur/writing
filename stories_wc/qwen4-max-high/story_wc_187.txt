After the fog became solid, the town of Vane stopped dreaming other people's lives.  For nine generations the fog had risen from the valley each evening carrying everybody's days, and everyone woke knowing whose goat had drowned, whose son had kissed whom.  Now the fog lay below the mountain like a white floor, and the past had nowhere to travel.  Sela was the summer-quill archivist, licensed to cut pens from birds molting in July, because summer quills took only what a hand had actually lived.  No conjecture would hold in that ink; the page went blank under a guess.  She had always been preemptively nostalgic, writing farewells to a loaf while it was still warm, to her mother while her mother still climbed the stairs.  So she had mourned the collective memory for years before it hardened, and was not surprised to be the only one prepared.  In the observatory's forecourt stood the whispering crystal, pale quartz that had drunk nine generations of pooled remembering.  After rain it gave the voices back, and the town climbed the stair to hear itself.  Each rain now returned fewer words, and the words came slower, and the hush between them deepened.  People began introducing themselves with the wrong childhoods.  Sela wanted the one thing she had never been permitted: a self cut from her own experience instead of poured into her by weather.  To carve that, she had to know which of her memories were hers.  She climbed to the stormlit observatory above the clouds, where lightning moved beneath the solid fog like something pacing under ice.  On its terrace stood the monuments, one basalt pillar for every memory the town had agreed to keep.  Above the clouds the sun was reliable, so she could do what nobody below could: examine shadow lengths.  Each pillar carried a groove chiseled along the pavement where its noon shadow had fallen on the day it was raised.  Measure the groove, and you had the sun's height, and the season, and the truth.  She worked through the stormlight with her mother's brass rule, and every pillar agreed with its inscription until she reached the ninth.  The ninth read: THE GIRL WHO WALKED INTO THE FOG AND CAME BACK CARRYING US ALL.  Its groove was a hand's width too short for autumn; the shaft had been raised at midsummer, three months before the walk it honored.  The monument came first; the town needed a girl to make it true, and afterward everyone remembered being her.  Sela sat on the cold stone with the rule in her lap and the storm blinking under her boots.  She could not verify whose walk it had been; the fog that carried evidence was a floor now, and the crystal was drying.  So she did the forbidden thing, quietly: she conjectured.  Her mother had cut quills every July, had climbed these stairs until she stopped, had chiseled a groove no mason signed.  Her mother had believed a town without a shared past was only a crowd, and had been willing to pay for the difference.  A mother who kept the town's memory might send her own daughter down into the fog to earn an inscription already carved.  The girl, she conjectured, was herself at seven, walking down in autumn because the stone had promised she would.  She had come up carrying other people's voices, and the town called it a gift, and had been drinking from her ever since.  That was why she mourned things early: she had been saying goodbye to a self that was communal property.  She took out her summer quill and wrote the conjecture down, and the ink stayed.  Summer ink could not hold a guess; therefore she had guessed her way to something she had lived.  Below the terrace, the last rain of the season fell on the forecourt, and the crystal whispered a few names, then fewer.  She did not go down to listen.  Instead she wrote the walk as hers: the cold, the wet wool, her mother's voice arriving from a stranger's throat.  Sculpting, not receiving.  By morning the town would wake with nothing to borrow and would have to start making its own lives.  The whispers thinned to one syllable, and the syllable went, and the hush came up the stair like water and settled.  In it, for the first time, Sela heard only herself, and kept writing.
