The pumps had hummed under Veyle for sixty years, and Mira had learned to read against them.  Then the grid failed, and in the blackout the city took back its stillness.  Forty feet of green water lay on the library's glass dome, and without the lamps to flatten it, the surface light came down in moving nets, dappled over the shelves, over her hands, over the flood ledger.  The same mine pumps had kept this pocket of air dry for sixty years, a memorial nobody visited.  She was a logical page indexer: she gave order to documents that had lost theirs, trusting catchwords and watermarks and the arithmetic of a clerk's habits, never the label on the box.  The box said SURVEY FAULTS, and inside it, in another hand, was her grandmother's name.  Four hundred people had drowned when the mine galleries let the river in, and the official finding named Ilse Vantry's survey as the origin of the disaster.  Mira had not come to clear her; she had come to trace honest origins, whichever way they ran.  In the dark she could hear the caisson breathing water in at the seams, one drip, then two, and she counted them the way she counted pages.  An hour, perhaps, before the power came back or the floor went under.  She was tranquilly perceptive in the way of people who work long among paper: nothing startled her, everything registered.  So when the green light slid across the certification marks and one of them flashed copper while the others stayed flat and black, she did not gasp; she readjusted the mirror propped against the shelf until the beam lay full on the page.  Every page bore TRUE COPY and a date, struck by the survey office's antique ink stamp, brass-handled, its date slugs reset each morning by some clerk long dead.  Iron gall ink sinks matte into rag paper; the cheap aniline stuff that came later sits up and shines.  Her working hypothesis had been the kind one: a panicked office, dates stamped in a hurry, pages shuffled by wet hands.  She formed the alternative hypothesis and let it sit beside the first, unhurried, the way she let two page orders stand until the catchwords chose.  Someone had certified these pages afterward, years afterward, with new ink and an old stamp.  The shine told her which.  Nine pages glittered.  Nine pages had been made to say that Ilse Vantry read the rock as sound.  Water reached her ankles, cold as a verdict, and she readjusted the ledger onto the higher shelf and kept working.  Under the ninth substitution, pressed flat by sixty years of damp, lay the original warning: Ilse's own hand, the galleries mapped as voids, the recommendation to stop the mine pumps and let the basin fill slowly, over ten years, with the city emptied first.  It was stamped matte, dated three months before the collapse, and it was honest.  Beside it, in the same hand, was an arithmetic error in the third column, a carried figure that made the void seem smaller than it was.  Mira sat in the reclaimed stillness with the light moving over her wrist and felt the whole shape of her wanting: to have her grandmother innocent, to be done, to be right.  The power would return and the hum would come back and someone would want a clean finding.  She numbered the warning 41.  She numbered the error 42, and did not move it to an appendix.  Then she took the antique stamp from its box and readjusted its date slugs with a fingernail.  She inked it and struck the cover of her index, today's date, her own name beneath it in pencil, so that whoever came next would know exactly how new the truth was and could test it.  The fresh mark shone copper too, and she left it shining, so no one could mistake her page for an old one.  Water took the second shelf as she climbed the stair with the ledger inside her coat.  Above her the drowned streets held their green light, and below, the library filled patiently, page by page, keeping everything she had not moved.  At the lock she stopped, because the pumps had not restarted and the stillness was still hers to keep.  Her resolve came dappled, light and doubt crossing it in the same breath: Ilse had been right, Ilse had been wrong, and both facts now had numbers.  She would come back at first light and index the rest.
