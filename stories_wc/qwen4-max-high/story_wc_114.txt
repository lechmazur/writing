The fever broke at four in the morning, and the station went on ticking around me like something that had never been ill.  Wren had been dead eleven days.  I had spent four of them walking the col in freezing rain, calling her name into the whiteout, and the rest sweating through the wool blankets in the cot by the stove.  Now my skin was cold and my head was clear in a way I did not want. 

The station sat at three thousand meters on the shoulder of Sarn: one steel room, a mast, a stove, and a door you opened with your shoulder against the snow.  In the corner hummed the oracle, the forecasting engine the service left behind when it closed the valley office.  It was built fault-tolerant: lose a sensor and it routed around the wound, invented the missing column from its neighbors, and kept speaking.  Nothing in its architecture permitted it to say I do not know. 

On the night of the storm the aurora came down as far as our latitude, green curtains swaying over the ridge, and the solar wind shook the mast's telemetry into nonsense.  The oracle interpolated and printed CLEAR, RIDGE SAFE, CONFIDENCE 0.91.  I had already seen the raw gust column: ninety, a gap, ninety-four.  Wren asked over the radio whether she had time to finish her plate at the col, and I said yes, because she had been happy all week and I wanted to keep her that way.  Afterward I laid my thumb on the pencil entry in the logbook and smudged it, so the nine could be read as a four.  The board wrote instrument failure, and I signed, and swallowed the rest. 

A swallowed thing does not dissolve; it sits in you, and you spend your days trying to learn its weight.  In the instrument room we kept a balance that read to a microgram, for weighing the filters that catch dust out of the air.  Wren's botanical illustration was still pinned above it: moss campion, a cushion of tiny rosettes, the petals notched, the root drawn long and stubborn into the scree.  She had left the last rosette unshaded, the way you leave a sentence when someone calls you.  All her knowing lived in the margins, in small pencil: wind 40, petals 6 mm, bee at 11:20, sister humming.  That was how she worked, by occupying margins, because the center of the page belonged to the flower. 

The oracle worked the same way.  It was peripherally aware: when a column failed it fell back on the observer's margin field, the free-text box at the edge of the log form where we wrote what the numbers had no room for.  For eleven days I had left that box empty. 

I took the illustration down and weighed it: 4.118 grams.  Then I turned it over and wrote on the back, in Wren's pencil, small and hard: the ridge was not safe.  I saw the gusts.  I told her yes.  I weighed it again, and the finer readout gave the difference: two hundred and ten micrograms of graphite.  That is what a swallowed truth weighs: less than a breath of dust, and enough to bend a life around it. 

I sat at the terminal and typed the same words into the margin field, and logged the hour, and signed my name.  The oracle took the note the way it takes everything, without complaint, and rebuilt the night from the edges inward.  It printed the hindcast: RIDGE UNSAFE, CONFIDENCE 0.94, OBSERVER OVERRIDE.  Then it printed the morning forecast, and for the first time in my three winters there the number beside it was 0.40, and the error bars were wide as the sky. 

I shouldered the door open.  The aurora was still up there, faint now, green fading into the dawn, whole curtains of it swaying like something breathing in its sleep.  The mast iced and ticked; the needle swung and settled and swung.  Nothing here holds still, not the wind, not the green light, not the swaying certainties we buy from machines that cannot bear to be wrong.  Wren died in the gap where a machine had guessed, and I let the guess stand.  I stood in the cold with the illustration in my coat, two hundred and ten micrograms heavier, and let the sorrow move through me the way the light moved, green and slow and beautiful and no use at all.  It weighed almost nothing; I carried it anyway.
