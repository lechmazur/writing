The falcon came back on the fourth morning, alone, and settled on the rusted rail as if nothing had happened.  Ada Kessel noted the hour, because noting things was her trade, and because the bird's return heralded the close of the survey season whether or not Bek came with it.  He did not come.  The tube on the falcon's leg held a slip with one word in Bek's block capitals: ONE.  "Helpful," she told the bird.  "Nine rivers, and he sends me a number."  Ada edited atlases for the Northern Survey, which meant she spent her life correcting other people's certainties.  The outpost was a rusted hut on stilts above a frozen lake, stove in one corner, window facing the ice.  Everything was very quiet, and the quiet was the problem.  Lake ice should complain all night, cracking and settling; this ice had said nothing for three days.  Bek had walked out across it to find where the rivers began.  The atlas printed nine springs, nine tidy blue dots strung along the escarpment, each copied from a map copied from a map.  She had believed for years that the nine were one, and she carried the belief the way she carried her red pencil: without announcing it, and without ever setting it down.  Bek had laughed at her, gently, in six languages.  He was a polyglot, and his phrasebook still lay on the bunk, spine broken, one corner turned down.  She had assumed the dog-ear marked a phrase he wanted: water, help, how far.  That morning, with the bird watching her, she opened it flat.  The turned-down page held the Ushen column, the tongue of the valley people.  Beside the entry for where the rivers begin, Bek had penciled: singular.  Not nine names, but one noun for the place where the water starts.  "A word is not a survey," she said, and the stove ticked, agreeing with nothing.  She fed the stove anyway, and the room warmed, and the window began to sweat.  Bek had a surveyor's habit of scratching notes with his diamond stylus on whatever would hold a line, plates being scarce and paper scarcer.  Dry, the pane looked like nothing at all.  Wet, it drew.  The pattern of condensation on the glass came up in beads along the scored lines, and the lines were rivers.  Nine of them, running down the pane toward the lake, and above them, where the escarpment should have been, they gathered.  They did not stop at nine separate points.  They climbed and bent and met, all of them, in one place no bigger than a pencil dot, and beside it he had scratched a bearing and a distance.  A singular convergence: nine springs that were one spring, a single mouth under the cliff speaking in nine tongues.  The bearing ran from this window across the lake to the far ice wall.  Ada sat down slowly.  Warm water rises; a spring strong enough to father nine rivers would keep the ice above it thin and silent all winter.  The lake had not been polite.  It had been hiding something.  He must have come back in the night, cut his answer into the one surface that could not burn or blow away, and gone out again to measure it properly.  The pane was already drying at the edges.  She had a minute, perhaps, before the map vanished, so she did what editors do.  She copied it.  Tissue over glass, red pencil, nine lines and their one meeting place, the bearing, the distance, the dot that would cost her nine blue dots and every printing plate downstream of them.  When the glass cleared she had it, and her hands were steady, and she was not surprised, which frightened her more than the silence had.  She wrote the correction out fair, and under it, in the same hand, the date the ice took Bek, and rolled both into the tube.  Then she carried the falcon to the rail and let it go south, to herald one source where the world had nine.  "One," she said to the empty bunk.  "You could have written a sentence."  She let the stove die so the pane would stay dry and keep its lines until she needed them again.  Then she sat in the cold with the phrasebook open on her knees, listening to the lake say nothing, and did not go out.
