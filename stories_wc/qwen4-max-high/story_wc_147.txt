The distress signal came up the drowned copper wire at three in the morning: three short, three long, three short.  Nobody had tapped that line in forty years, and only two people alive could read it.  One was Anselm, who had stayed.  The other was me, who had not.  The wire still ends in my boathouse, where I keep the old key and the old grudge. 

I make my living bottling silence.  The rich pay well for a crystal perfume vial holding the hush of a snowfall, or of a room just after someone has stopped speaking.  Anselm taught me the gathering: how to hold a vial open in a quiet place until the quiet thickens and goes in.  Then I left the temple and sold what he gave away. 

By dawn I was over the reservoir, watching my lights find the temple's dome sixty feet down.  Gold leaf still clung to the ribs of it, and the fish moved through the nave like a congregation that had forgotten the words but kept the habit.  My gauge said forty minutes of air.  At seven the turbines would start, and the draw would turn the nave into a throat. 

He was in the reliquary, where the roof still held a bubble of air gone foul and thin.  Eighty-one years old, sitting in six inches of water, his lamp burned down to a blue bead.  "You came," he said, and his voice used up air he could not spare.  He had not signaled to be saved.  He had signaled because he was afraid to be the last one listening.  He waved off my spare regulator; his lungs were already full of the room. 

In his hands was a crystal vial the length of my finger, stoppered and wired shut with a snarl of the Order's hair-knots, one for each keeper who had filled it.  "The hush of the night the water came," he said.  "A thousand people in this room, and not one of them speaking."  Gathered hush, four hundred years of it in these walls, and this was the last vial.  "Take it up," he said.  "You were always the one who could harness what will not keep." 

A full vial is worth a year of my life; an open one is worth nothing, because hush spends itself in the hearing.  And it touches memories.  That is the whole secret of the trade: a gathered hush goes into the ear and finds whatever you have buried, and lets you hear it again in the room where it happened.  My mother had been in that room the night the water came.  I was six, and I had spent forty years trying to remember whether she said my name. 

My gauge said eleven minutes.  I took the vial and worked the wire with numb fingers, and the knots would not give, and I wanted to break the neck of it against the stone.  Anselm put his hand over mine and made me slow down.  You unsnarl a thing by following it backward, the way it came in, not by pulling.  Loop by loop, four centuries of hair came loose in my palm. 

Then his lamp went out, and he stopped breathing, and there was no more time in the room at all.  I pulled the stopper.  Hush does not pour; it leans out, cold, like something recognizing you.  The body's instinct is to gasp when the cold hits, and a gasp is a sound, and a sound shatters gathered hush the way a stone shatters a window.  You receive it only by overriding instinct, by holding the breath while the ribs burn and the throat knocks.  I held.  And the temple filled with a thousand people holding still, and under them, close, at my ear, my mother saying my name twice, the second time like a question. 

It lasted four seconds, maybe five, and then it was spent, and the vial in my hand was only glass.  Nothing left to bottle, nothing to sell, no way to keep it; that is what ephemeral means, and I had built a life on pretending otherwise.  I set the empty vial in Anselm's hands and folded his fingers over it.  Then I breathed, and the sound of it went out through the dome and the gold leaf and the patient fish, and the temple took it back.  Above me the turbines began, and the water leaned toward the dam, and I went up alone into the ordinary noise.
