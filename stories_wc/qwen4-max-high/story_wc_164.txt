The road to Alder Bay vanished on a Tuesday, and Sill took it well, all things considered.  Mira put the kettle on, as one does among subtractions, and joked that the road had gone to look for her mother.  Her mother had walked out along it nine years ago; the road had only just followed. 

Mira was a sea-foam recollectionist, paid to read what the surf had swallowed.  Foam keeps no pictures, only the ache of whoever last stood in it, so she transliterated emotions into eleven hooks and dots, one for each kind of longing.  The vanished road gave her four pages of dots and one blank line, the same missing piece in every sample, shaped like a woman walking away.  For the holes she used crater glass, ground from the green sheet the sky left when it struck the headland.  Crater glass shows absence better than presence; look through it and a room tells you what has been carried out.  Through her lens, the blank line was not blank but faintly warm, like a chair just left.  Somebody had taken the piece somewhere, and Mira meant to learn why paths disappear before her last memory of her mother faded, since recollectionists spend their own memories to hold other people's.  She had three days, perhaps four, and she knew exactly where to spend them. 

Above the town, in the cliff's shoulder, sat the geode: a crystal the size of a church, cracked open by quarrymen who then, sensibly, stopped.  Inside, the cave wore every colour oil makes on water, and the walls held the town's remembered paths as veins of light.  Recollectionists wrote their hooks and dots there, because crystal keeps a feeling the way a cellar keeps apples.  The Alder vein had gone dark in a single night, and the dark ran toward the quarry crack, which was the first useful thing grief had given her. 

Mira conjectured, aloud, to the crystals, that a path is not stone but carried feeling, and that it goes out when the last carrier sets it down.  Then she wondered who had been carrying the Alder road, and the answer came in on wings.  A godwit, storm-blown off its migration, had squeezed through the quarry crack to rest, and it had eaten its way north along the Alder hedgerows.  Its droppings lay in a scatter across the cave floor, and in them, seeds.  Mira laughed, which echoed badly and beautifully, and got a trowel. 

She planted the scattered seeds where they lay, in their own order, and kept them damp with rain caught in a crystal cup.  By the second night, with the foam sample drying to lace, the seedlings stood in a line that bent twice, exactly where the road had bent.  Blackthorn, then dog rose, then the elder her mother used to cut for whistles: the hedgerow, in sequence, held in a bird and set down here.  Through the crater glass the sprouts were not plants but absences, each one a hole the road had left in something living.  So that was why paths disappear: they are made of pieces, and pieces get carried, by birds, by berries, by women walking to Alder Bay.  A road does not die; it disperses, and the dark vein was only a road gone to seed. 

Mira sat with that, cheerful and gutted, in the rainbow dark.  She could gather the pieces and hoard them, and be the only person alive who remembered the way.  Instead she cut her four pages of dots into the wall beside the seedlings, where anyone climbing up could read them.  Feeling written down is feeling handed over; the wall would carry the road so she would not have to.  It cost her the copy in her head, as she had known it would, and by morning her mother's face had gone smooth and pleasant, a stranger's. 

The vein lit, faint at first, then the green of shallow water.  Down in Sill, a boy found he knew the way to Alder Bay, though nobody had told him, and set off with a bag of apples.  Mira watched him go from the geode mouth, watering the seedlings, keeping the road alive the ordinary way.  She could not remember her mother, but she remembered the eleven signs, and she wrote the twelfth: a hook for the gladness of losing something to somebody who will use it.  The godwit, rested, went north through the crack, carrying whatever it had picked up here.  Mira waved it off, cheerfully, in the gloom, and hoped it would drop the seeds somewhere with rain.
