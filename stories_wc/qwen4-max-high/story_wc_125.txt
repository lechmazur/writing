The energy beings came in March, and by August the roadside had stopped being lonely.  They hung above the interchanges like weather that had decided to stay.  I wrote poems for truckers at four dollars a page, and then, when the light came down, for nobody.  What I wanted was small and enormous: to know the texture of time.  Not its length.  Its grain, its nap, the way it runs one direction under the hand and bristles the other.  The beings had that knowledge the way a fish has water, which is to say they had never once noticed it.  I slept in a terra-cotta grotto off the old kiln road, a hollow where clay had been fired until the walls rang.  The roof fell in a century ago; the stars came down into it, and the clay gave their light back warm.  Under that starlight the walls showed their layers: flood, drought, flood, each firing a thin band of a different red.  I had been reading them for years, the way you read a scar.  In my pack I kept a frayed coil of rope.  Nine of us went over the wall at Corbin on that rope, and eight of us reached the field.  Teo was still below me when the searchlights swung, and the rope ran from my hands to the tunnel where the others were crawling.  I cut it.  That is the whole poem; I have never gotten past the third line.  The being that came to my grotto wore itself as a slow braid of light, three strands turning, and it was polite.  It said it could give me what I wanted.  It said they held my whole life at once like a chord, nothing spent, and could open my hand to feel it.  The price was the cut.  They would take that second out of me and hold it forever, and in their keeping it would be soft, revisable, playable both ways.  Teo would fall and not fall.  I would never have written a word.  I sat with that a long while, hating how much I wanted it.  Then I laughed, because I like a game, and a bargain with something that cannot lose is only fair if you make it one.  So I set rules, and I said them out loud, twice, so nothing was hidden.  I would lay the second out in layers, and it had to point to the place where the choosing lived.  If it found the place, I would pay.  If it could not, it would tell me the texture and take nothing.  It agreed the way weather agrees.  I uncoiled the rope and patterned it across the grotto floor in a spiral, one turn for each band of red in the wall.  Then I went down through it.  Tar from the yard, which was the first year.  Salt, which was the winter we pumped the flooded block.  Rust, which was the wire we braided in to make the hemp hold a man.  Under the rust, the fray at the lip of the wall, fibers combed white by stone.  And under that, Teo's hand, which is not a layer you can reach, only one you can keep arriving at.  The light leaned in and turned with the spiral, three strands going around and around, and it could not point.  It had every instant of the rope at once; the tar was still wet in it, the knife was still sheathed, Teo was still climbing and already falling.  Nothing in it was worn, so nothing in it had a direction.  I said the thing I had come to say, and I said it gently, because I was offering it a wound.  Take it, but keep it the way I keep it: closed, unrevisable, one way only.  Hold the second and never open it, and you will have a thing you cannot spend, and that is what time is.  Someone had to keep him and not fix him.  It warned me that it would then be able to lose something.  I said yes, that was the rule, and I had said it twice.  It took the coil and the second inside it, and one strand of its braid stopped turning.  It has been standing in my grotto ever since, waiting, which it had never done, under the stars that the clay hands back warm.  I got my texture, and it is not a chord; it is the nap of a worn rope, smooth one way, bristles the other.  I finished the third line last night, in charcoal, on the oldest red band, where the flood was.  It reads over my shoulder, and it cannot go back.
