In our town, nobody dies behind a closed door.  We open the window to the water and climb the veiled ridgeway to keep the vigil in twilight, when the strait goes slack and the ferry can cross.  My mother's window faced the water, so I opened it wide.  I climbed with Bramble, her old blind dog, because he had howled since noon and I could not carry that sound and my own grief too.  The neighbors were already at their bends, singing, each family keeping its own turn of the path.  The leash was leather, cracked from years of salt.  It parted at the third bend, and I stood in the hour we call after the leash breaks, when the hand is empty and the thing you loved is already running.  Bramble went down toward the water with his nose up, sure of a road he could not see.  The ridgeway does not go straight down.  The ferry guides cut it that way, switchback over switchback, so that no one arrives at the landing in the state they left the house.  You must traverse it through indirect routes, one verse of the tide-song per bend, and the song is a shoreline lullaby our mothers sing so the water will lie flat for the boat.  Hush now, the gray goose is crossing; hush now, the tide is a bed.  I sang it wrong and fast, the way you sing when you are chasing.  Fog took the bends one at a time.  At the ninth, old Sorrel was waiting with her lantern, and at the tenth her partner Emmy, and between them a hundred years of putting people across.  They are the ferry guides; they know what a person carries before the person does.  "Your dog went on," Sorrel said.  "He knows the landing."  "My mother is still breathing," I said.  "Then you want a return ticket," Emmy said, and took out the punch.  It was heavy iron, worn bright at the jaws, and it cut a star instead of a hole, so the little star stayed hanging in the ticket like a light in a window.  One star for going, one for coming back, and the guides never punch the second until it is earned.  "I want her to stay," I said.  "You want your courage," Sorrel said, "and you do not know where you put it."  They told me what everyone here is told: that unused courage is never lost, only set down, and it waits in the stone niche at the last bend, where the living turn back.  "Go and find it," Emmy said, "and take the long way; the short way teaches nothing."  So I went to find where unused courage waits, and Bramble was there, sitting beside the niche with his blind face turned to the sound of the water.  In the niche lay the folded things: a fisherman's ring, a child's shoe, a letter nobody had sent, and my note.  I had left it there the first night I climbed to call the guides and turned back instead.  It said the true thing: You can go, I will keep the window open.  I had kept it folded because saying it would make me the one who stayed.  Bramble leaned against my leg, gently persistent, the way the tide is, the way the lullaby is, wearing the stone down without ever raising its voice.  I came back to the landing with the paper in my fist.  Emmy punched the second star without asking, and Sorrel said the fare would be one true thing said out loud.  Then I climbed, one verse per bend, and the fog thinned at every turn, as if it were being let go of too.  Bramble came with me, off the leash, choosing it.  At the window I read the note aloud, and my mother said, "Wider, then," and I opened the window to the water.  Below, the ferry lantern went out across the strait, small as a star somebody had punched in the dark.  I sang the lullaby down to its last line, and Bramble slept, and the broken leash lay on the sill where the wind could move it.  Courage, it turns out, is only the long way round to a thing you already know.
