The physician on the mainland gave her six months, and Vesna asked him to write it down, because she recorded everything.  Then she took the ferry home to the island she kept.  Forty years of caretaking had taught her that a deadline was only a measurement, and measurements could be worked with.  The temple orchard grew inside eleven pillars of living stone.  They rose gray and faintly warm from the cliff, thickening a finger's width each decade, and between them stood the oaks, the pear trees, the fig.  Pilgrims called it a ruin that God had refused to finish.  Vesna called it a congregation, and she was the only one who swept for all of it.  That night she opened the botanical diary, three volumes now, hers and her predecessor's and the one before that.  She wrote the date, her pulse, the wind, and the sentence: six months, begin the undercroft.  The undercroft had been mortared shut two centuries ago by an abbot who wanted the stone to be material, not member.  The diary's oldest volume explained why, in a hand gone brown as tea.  No mason had ever cut these pillars.  The first keepers had grown them, laying copper wire around oak branches and letting the stone follow the wire the way a vine follows a string.  Every arch in the temple was a negotiation, and the sealed hall below held the terms.  Vesna read it twice, calmly, the way she read frost warnings.  What she wanted was simple and immodest: an orchard where stone, tree, and monk were held to the same manners.  Then she went to the abbot and told him she was dying and intended to remodel.  He said the seal was doctrine.  She said doctrine had let the fourth pillar strangle a two-hundred-year-old oak, and she had the measurements to prove it.  He said no.  She said she would be dead by autumn and would not be available to apologize.  She began the next morning, in January fog, gently systematic, one hour a day so the work would outlast her strength.  Chisel, brush, water, rest; chisel, brush, water, rest.  Novice Petar, who had been assigned to scrape lichen from the pillars and had hated it, began bringing her lunch and staying.  She taught him to read the diary's oldest hand, and to write in it: rainfall, bud break, the pillars' slow creep measured in pencil ticks.  On the nineteenth day the mortar gave, and the undercroft breathed out cold air smelling of wet rock and apples.  Below, the pillars did not end.  They branched into roots, and the roots had grown around the oaks' roots, and everywhere along the join ran old copper wire, green with age, still humming faintly under Petar's lamp.  The temple was not a building containing trees.  It was one creature wearing two bodies, and the abbot's seal had gagged it.  Petar asked whether they should tell the abbot.  Vesna said they would tell him with the wire.  The remodel took the spring.  She wrapped fresh copper wire around the oak branches, loosely, three fingers of slack for growth, and led each line down to the pillar that was crushing its trunk.  Stone follows copper; copper follows the tree; so the stone, at last, would follow the tree.  By May the fourth pillar had turned aside, a hand's breadth, opening a seam of daylight along the oak's bark.  The abbot came, put his palm on the warm gray curve, and stood there a long time.  He asked what the office of caretaker was for.  Vesna said: to make sure nothing here is treated as furniture.  He struck the lichen scraping from the rule, and wrote a new hour into the day: wiring, watering, listening.  Vesna kept the diary through June, her handwriting shrinking, the entries never shortening.  She noted the slack in each wire, the pears' set, the exact date the fig leafed, so Petar would know what normal looked like and could tell when the stone got greedy again.  Respect, she wrote, is a maintenance schedule.  In July she stopped climbing and dictated from a chair, unbothered, correcting Petar's spelling of the Latin names.  Her last entry was a measurement: the fourth pillar, one and a half fingers clear of the oak, still moving.  Petar buried her under the fig, which was permitted, because the roots there were already family.  Every morning he takes the diary and the wire up into the orchard, and the pillars lean, patiently, toward whatever the trees decide.
