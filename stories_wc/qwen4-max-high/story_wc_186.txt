The thistles came up through the pavilion floor the year the world stopped answering.  Oriel let them grow.  She was the beacon-tower custodian, which after the collapse of civilization meant sweeping glass dust, greasing shutters that sheltered nothing, and keeping a ledger.  The ledger was all coincidences.  A gull on the north rail the morning her sister drowned; a bell in the ruins ringing on the hour the last caravan left; three flashes from the sea on the night the tower's prism cracked.  The caravan's quartermaster had read it and laughed, gently, the way you laugh at a child's map.  Coincidence is noise, he said; meaning is what you make before the noise eats you.  If the gull meant nothing, then her sister's last morning had only been weather, and Oriel could not live in weather.  She wanted, once before she died, to prove him wrong. 

Below the tower stood the pavilion, once a music hall, its panes still holding every note they had ever heard.  Oriel was retrocognitively blessed, the polite phrase for cursed: lay her palm on glass and the past came up her wrist like cold water.  She could not steer it by wanting.  Memory has no index, but the panes had also caught the sky, and stars keep appointments.  So she learned to read backward by tracking star paths, walking the slow wheel of them night by night until she came to the hour she wanted.  Seven winters she spent on her knees in the glass humming pavilion, hunting one night. 

To rekindle the beacon she needed the prism whole.  Flame she had: rendered fat, thistledown wicks, a flint worn to a thumbnail.  But a bare flame is a rumor, and she needed a beam, something that could cross forty miles of black water and be unmistakably a question.  The broken crystal prism lay in her apron in five pieces, and the sixth, the wedge that gathered the light, was gone.  She had crawled the pavilion floor for it for years.  That autumn she noticed the hum had a hole in it.  Under the collapsed orchestra rail one corner said nothing at all, and the thistles there grew without rustling.  In the dust lay a shard of hush: the silence that fell when the cities stopped, gone solid, cold as a held breath.  She touched it and got nothing, no past at all, because silence keeps no record; so she laid her hand on the pane beneath and tracked the star paths back.  Nine nights backward, then eleven, then the night.  She saw the tower shudder, the prism fall and break, this shard drop through the roof lantern in the same instant, and out on the water, three flashes.  Three, then dark, then three.  Her own hand had been on the shutter that night, three times, asking. 

Noise, the quartermaster would say.  A lamp swinging in a swell; a stone falling because stones fall.  Oriel set the hush into the prism's missing wedge.  It fit.  Not nearly, not with grinding: it fit, the way a word fits a mouth.  She could have called that luck too.  Instead she carried the mended prism up the tower stairs, seated it in the lamp housing, and lit the thistledown.  The fat caught.  Light went through crystal and then through hush and came out changed, a beam so quiet it seemed to take the sound out of the wind.  She worked the shutter: three, dark, three.  Then she waited with the ledger open on her knees, her thumb on the old entry, ready to be a fool in writing.  The sea did what the sea does.  Thistledown went past the gallery rail, a hundred pale parachutes carrying nothing anywhere, and she watched them go and did not close the book.  Then, far out, three flashes.  Dark.  Three.  Eight years, and the dark had spoken in her own grammar.  Oriel wrote the hour, the bearing, and the star paths that fixed them both, because a thing that can be checked is not a wish.  Under it she wrote: answered.  In the morning she would sweep the glass dust and grease the shutters and let the thistles seed the whole humming hall.  Somebody out there was keeping a ledger too.
