Vera measured faces because she could not remember them.  Prosopagnosia, the neurologist called it; her own word was static.  So she worked by numbers: the width between pupils, the slope of a jaw, the exact hitch of a shoulder when a mother said her daughter's name.  She kept a ledger: date, duration, distances, the exact words a witness used, nothing guessed. 

Nineteen years after the quarry masquerade, the sheriff's boxes were gone.  A courthouse pipe burst, the basement drank, and mold ate the swabs, the photographs, the statements, until the county signed a disposal order.  Cold cases, the county called them, as if temperature were the problem.  What remained was a ribbon fragment, four inches of black silk torn from a mask, which Marta's mother had kept in a matchbox because nobody had asked her for it.  Vera laid it on white paper and logged its frayed end under a lamp.  Torn, not cut: someone had pulled away from her, or she from someone. 

The town had not forgotten Marta so much as mislaid her, the way it mislaid the quarry road under mist each October.  Vera knew that flatness intimately.  A face slid off her like water off glass, and she had spent her life proving she could still care about what she could not hold.  Feeling, for her, was a muscle gone dormant from disuse; she wanted it woken, in herself first, then in them. 

She began with the eleven who had been at the quarry that night.  She measured each of them twice, wrote down what they said they had done, and painted them in the hush of her studio with their brows furrowed over nineteen years of not thinking about it.  Marta she built from the mother's hands, which still cupped the shape of a fourteen-year-old skull, and from a laugh described as a hiccup, and from the ribbon. 

Then came the part she never explained to clients.  Grief that seals over does not heal; it goes on poisoning underneath.  You have to lance it.  She took the scalpel she used to true her canvas edges and lanced each finished portrait down the middle, one clean vertical wound.  Then she sewed opposites together: Marta's left half to each witness's right, cheek to cheek, one eye hers, one eye theirs.  She photographed the fragment from four angles, then unraveled it into black filament and used it as suture, so the torn thing did the joining.  Eleven faces, each one impossible, each one whole. 

In October she carried them down the quarry road in mist so thick the limestone walls were only rumors.  She wired the eleven composites to the shot-scarred rock face above the water, which lay black and still.  The mother had told everyone; that was her only weapon left.  They came because mist makes a room of any place, and because they were curious, and because not coming would have said something.  Vera could not recognize a single one of them.  She watched shoulders instead, and hands.  Nobody spoke for a long time; the hush had a furrow in it, the way a brow does when it is trying to remember. 

A man in a good coat stopped in front of the seventh panel, where his own right eye sat beside a girl's left.  He had been nineteen that night, and he had held the other end of the ribbon while his friend pulled, and he had told himself for nineteen years that it was only a game at a party.  Vera saw his hand come up to the seam, to the black stitches, and stop.  Something in his chest gave way with a sound like water finding a crack.  He said her name, and then he said where they had put her, and the mother made a noise the mist swallowed.  The boxes were pulp and the swabs were mold, and the case would be solved anyway, by a seam. 

Vera stood in the shadowed quarry and could not tell whose face was whose, and found that she was weeping anyway, which was the proof she had wanted: that empathy did not require recognition, only the willingness to be joined.  Below them the black water lay untroubled, waiting to be asked.
