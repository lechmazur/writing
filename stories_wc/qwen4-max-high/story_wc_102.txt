Three nights after the memory surfaced, Ada hauled her black tent onto the viaduct.  In the memory she walked this same span carrying a clay pot of embers, lighting lamps for travelers, and one ember she never gave away.  She had always been spiritually curious, the kind who tested incense and prayer beads and kept notebooks of failed proofs, believing nothing until now.  Now she wanted one thing: to find where unused love is stored.  Her mother had died in the middle of a sentence, and Ada had never finished her own. 

The viaduct had not carried trains in sixty years.  Buddleia grew out of its mortar, purple cones of bloom leaning over the drop, and at twilight the whole valley went to fog and lamplight.  Twilight was her hour: enough light left in the sky to draw with, enough dark to see the lamps.  Inside the tent, on a white table, the world arrived upside down.  Her lens was a drop of glass the size of a thumbnail, sold to her years ago by a temple stallholder who called it Kwan Yin's tear.  The bodhisattva had wept once for the world, he said, and the tear had hardened on the way down.  Ada doubted the story and kept the lens, because nothing else gathered so much light from so little of it. 

Teo came up the stairs at dusk, as he had every evening since February, when his wife died.  He sat on the parapet with a thermos and said nothing, which she had come to think of as a kind of listening.  Tonight she asked him in.  In the dark she could not see his face, only the pale table and the inverted valley, the lamps below burning like something poured.  She had learned to read people the way she read the lens, by what changed.  Faces lie in the dark; breathing does not. 

She asked him about Marta, and his breath went in and stopped, held high in his chest like a bucket at a well's mouth.  On the table, at the far arch, a figure had appeared with a pot of light.  Ada's own breath caught, and the figure stopped walking.  She let the air out slowly, and it walked again, small and patient, a pilgrim of sparks going the length of the viaduct.  It was her, from a life before this one, and it was not only her.  Wherever love had been made and never delivered, someone was still carrying it, walking the same span, waiting for a door. 

She told him what she saw, plainly, the way she would describe weather.  Teo said that he had stayed with Marta through a bad year, and had never told her why, and the why was the whole of his love.  His breath hitched again, and the pilgrim on the table knelt.  Ada understood then that she had been looking in the wrong places, in reliquaries and graves and the drawers of the dead.  Unused love is stored in the breath a living body refuses to let go. 

She put her hand on his back, between the shoulders, and breathed out loudly enough for him to hear how it was done.  He followed her, badly at first, then better.  Each time the air left him, the pilgrim lifted its pot and tipped a spark over the edge, and the fog below took it and turned it the color of the bloom.  He said her name once, Marta, on an out-breath, and it went out of him and into the valley and was, at last, spent.  His shoulders came down; the held thing had gone somewhere it could do work.  She had not healed him of grief; she had helped him spend what grief was hoarding. 

Then her own turn came, and she finished her own sentence aloud, the one saved for her mother, and the ember she had carried since a life she could not prove went out with it.  The tear fogged with their breathing and wept a single drop onto the table, blurring the picture into a field of purple light.  Outside, the buddleia moved, full of moths.  Teo asked if she would be here tomorrow, and she said yes, and went down the stairs beside him, breathing all the way to the bottom.
