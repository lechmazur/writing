The observatory stood on the rim of Tycho, above every cloud the Earth could make, and for forty years it had photographed things that did not change.  Vashti kept its cameras alive.  She was eighty, half blind from a lifetime of darkrooms, and she diagnosed shutters the way doctors take pulses: by ear.  A good shutter, she told her apprentice, sounds like a leaf letting go.  Then the foundations began to shake.  The quake swarm lasted eleven days, the way lunar quakes do, ringing the crust like a struck bowl, and when it stopped the telescope's pier had drifted three millimeters and the drive clock's escapement had sheared clean through.  The hand still moved.  It swept the dial at a changelessly constant rate, faithful and meaningless, counting nothing, and no amount of oiling would teach it to hesitate again.  The plate camera took its exposures from that clock, and so it could no longer take any at all.  The engineers condemned the station on the twelfth day.  One launch, they said, and mass for the archive and nothing else.  Tam would carry down forty years of the same crater walls, the same fixed stars, the same blue weather turning beneath them.  Vashti was allowed whatever she could hold in her hands.  In the stairwell grew a fig tree, planted by Ruth, her wife, who had died before it fruited, in a drum of borrowed soil.  It was the only thing on the Moon that ever let anything go.  Vashti had spent her life on instruments built to hold still, and what she wanted now, with the floors cracked and the launch counting down, was one plate of a leaf in the act of falling.  So she restructured the camera.  She cut the camera free of the clock, stripped out the governor and the trigger solenoid, and rebuilt the shutter as a thing with no time in it at all: two blades, a spring, her thumb.  Tam protested that an exposure needed a number.  Vashti said the number had never been the point; the point was the interval, and the interval could be heard.  In one-sixth gravity a fig leaf takes nearly three seconds to tumble from a branch two meters up, and it makes a small dry tick when it lands on the grating.  The stairwell fans had died with the quakes, so the silence was total enough to hear a stem give.  She would open the shutter at the sound of the stem letting go and close it at the sound of the leaf arriving: an exposure measured by the sound of a leaf falling, start to finish.  Tam held the lamp.  Earthlight came down the shaft cold and blue as the heart of a flame, and the leaf let go, and Vashti listened with her whole ruined face.  Tick.  She closed the blades.  In the darkroom, while the lander's beacon counted, the image came up: a leaf, blurred at its edges, sharp at its heart, hanging in the air of a place where nothing was supposed to move.  Tam wept, which surprised them both.  Vashti said that escape velocity here was two point three eight kilometers per second, and that it was not a speed so much as a promise: go that fast and you never fall back.  Everything else the Moon takes back.  Ruth was in the regolith under the south pier, which was why the shaking had felt personal.  The leaf had fallen; the tree would go to vacuum when the dome vented; the pier, the cracked floors, the survey, all of it would settle back into the dust it came from.  Only the plate would leave, riding a column of fire that had bought the one speed that does not return.  That was what it meant to harness the ephemeral: not to stop the falling, but to give it the one speed that outruns returning.  On the lander, Tam asked which vault the plate should go into, the cold ones under the mountain where the survey would outlast the century.  "A window," Vashti said.  "Sunlight will blacken the silver in twenty years."  Tam said that was no way to keep a thing.  Below them the observatory shrank to a bright scratch on a grey world, its foundations still ringing, its clock hand still sweeping the dial at the same speed it had always swept, faithful to nothing.  "Escape isn't forever," she said.  "It only means you don't fall back."  She unlatched the case a finger's width, so the sunlight could begin its slow work on the silver, and held it in her lap all the way home.
