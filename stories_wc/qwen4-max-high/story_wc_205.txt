Aurelia was a baroque asteroid: a hollowed rock spun for gravity, its inner galleries carved into gilt acanthus, its ceilings painted with skies nobody had ever stood beneath.  Time had been visible for ninety years now, settling on every surface as a varnish of hours, and the Consistory's archaeologists read those layers like rings in felled timber.  Iseult Vane had commanded a revolution once, and now held a dig permit issued by the government she had failed to overthrow.  The rising lasted eleven days; Nissa, who had taught her to shoot and to scan a line of verse, lasted nine.  Since then Iseult kept her politics in meter, the one form of speech the censors had never learned to confiscate. 

The shard in her palm was lead crystal, a fragment of a vase that had stood two centuries on a console table between the Long Gallery's mirrors.  Crystal was the only witness that kept what mirrors threw away: pictures, not merely dates and wear.  Silvered glass reflects time as readily as faces, so a mirror wears no varnish; but crystal drinks the reflection and lays it down, hour over hour, in its depths. 

The Consistory read Aurelia's gilt as proof of a court: kings, a dead consort, a religion of grief.  Their evidence was the veiling: in every storm, the old records showed, the founders had covered their mirrors, and the Consistory called this mourning.  A royal mausoleum could not be lived in, so four hundred descendants in the outer galleries had been given until the end of storm season to leave.  Iseult wanted a different civilization entirely: not a court but a commons, not mourners but maintainers.  Wanting it was not proof, and the bench had already dismissed her first two reports as poetry. 

So she devised a reading.  Varnish fluoresces under storm static; the deep hours glow only when the dust is charged.  But a lit mirror floods crystal with the present and drowns the record, and every survey of the gallery had been made with the mirrors bare.  Iseult carried the shard into the Long Gallery during the season's third storm, hung work-canvas over both mirrors, and set the fragment on the console table where its vase had stood.  The veiling was not superstition; it was the instrument. 

Darkness took the present away, and the crystal began to give back its stored light.  She saw hands first: cracked knuckles, canvas sleeves, a woman's forearms scarred by solder.  They were not draping a shrine; they were capping the light shafts that fed the algae terraces, because grit pitted the silvering and a pitted shaft scattered the sun the crops lived on.  Behind the mirrors, in the reflection's depth, ran ducts and hand-lettered maintenance tallies.  The gilt above them was foil sprayed over foundry slag.  Aurelia had been built by salvagers who carved flourishes because beauty was cheap and they had nothing else to give their children. 

Then the newest hours rose, and the shard showed the night it had been broken: a storm, the mirrors veiled for darkness, rebels moving through the gallery unseen.  Nissa stood against the canvas with a rifle in her arms, twenty-six years old, waiting for an order Iseult had already given.  The old maintenance habit had made her ambush possible, and the crystal had kept the whole of it, her face included.  To submit the shard was to hand the bench a rebel's confession alongside her proof.  She could grind the outer layer away in an hour and be believed.  Instead she sat in the dark until the storm passed, composing the finding in couplets, because a thing said plainly can be filed and a thing sung cannot. 

She submitted everything, Nissa's face and her own, and the bench, having no law against rhyme, adjourned.  The eviction was stayed pending review, which is how a government says mercy without meaning it.  But in the outer galleries the children were already skipping to her verses, and the verses were instructions: cap the glass before the dust, count the shafts, uncover at the all-clear.  Iseult went down with them to the terraces and pulled the canvas from the shafts, and the sun came through, gold as the gilt, and lay on the green.  It was not the civilization she had fought for; it was the one she had finally been able to describe, and describing it had cost her the last hour she owned of Nissa.
