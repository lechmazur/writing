The gold leaf came in books of twenty-five, and Odile could lay it along a page edge without breathing.  Below her shop the town of Vell ran its ceramic works three shifts, and the fluorescent lamp over her bench hummed along with them.  Above her, on the mountaintop, the nursery spread its blooming labyrinth of bonsai forests: nine terraces of dwarf azalea and plum, hedged into corridors a visitor could lose an afternoon in.  Every tree wanted a provenance card, gilt-edged, and the nursery's own ledger had burned in 1962.  So the stationer invented histories.  She wrote of a widow who wired a plum into the shape of grief, of a boy who traded his shoes for a seedling.  She was earnestly studious about the fraud, checking frost dates and rootstock so the lies would hold. 

Only one record in the shop was true: a battered notepad she had carried since her apprenticeship.  In it she charted the faint hum in the fluorescent lamp at dawn, in the minute when the night shift let go and the day shift had not yet taken hold.  The ballast sang higher when the grid was light and lower when the town pulled hard, and she timed its beats against brass forks her father, the works electrician, had left her.  Thirty beats a minute, eleven, none: the numbers were the texture of the hour.  This was how she collected the textures of different times.  Each year got a sheet of her own paper, pulped with whatever the year had left in the air: foundry ash, brick dust, the yellow pollen of the azalea terraces.  Beside each sheet she wrote the year's note, and the note told her what the year had felt like from inside. 

Then the works closed, and after the town went silent the hum stopped moving.  Families went down to the valley in trucks; the school shut; the buses stopped climbing.  For eleven weeks her chart was one unwavering line, the sound of a grid with nothing left to carry.  She could not pulp silence.  Then the power company lifted the slope spur, and her bench lamp died, and the only tube still burning within walking distance was the nursery's. 

The nursery's manager came down in October with an order and a proposal.  Visitors still climbed for the bloom, he said, but they wanted the town too: a heritage pamphlet, a past with a shape.  He would pay for kilns and festivals and a founder nobody had ever met.  Odile said she would think, and climbed the mountain in the dark with the notepad. 

The labyrinth held cold the way a cup holds water.  She walked the corridors between the dwarf plums, whose bloom smelled in the dark like almond and iron, and she counted turnings the way the nursery had taught her.  In the potting shed at the center, one fluorescent lamp still burned over the grafting bench, kept on for the cuttings.  She waited for dawn, the only hour her measurements could be compared.  She set her water jar on the bench and let the tube's light refract through it, laying a trembling white bar across the page so she could read the old pencil.  Gold only throws light back; glass takes it in and breaks it open, and she had always loved glass better. 

She struck the forks one by one and counted the beats until the hum lay still against the brass.  Then she found the same number near the front of the notepad, from the winter of the strike, when the kilns were cold and the nursery had one lamp and no orders.  So the silence was not a new texture.  It was the note of one lamp lit and everything still to be made, and she had it already, in her own hand, in pencil. 

A tree can carry an invented grandmother; a silence cannot.  She declined the pamphlet.  Instead she pulped the last of the azalea pollen with fiber from the shed's discarded grafting tape and made a single sheet, thin enough to read a lamp through.  On the card for the youngest plum she wrote only what was known: cut from a surviving tree, the year the town went quiet. 

Then she did what she had never done for a true record.  She sized the edges of the battered notepad, breathed on them, and laid the gold.  It took, and the book shut with a small bright seam along its side, like a lamp left on at the center of a maze.
