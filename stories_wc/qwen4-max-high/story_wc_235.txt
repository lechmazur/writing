The stone said my name and a date three winters back, which was rude, since I was still using the name.  I brushed the lichen off the letters, decided the mason had been drunk or early, and walked up the ridge to the ranger cabin where I learned to write.  Thirty-one volumes I have filled since, all of them dreams, all of them caught while I was awake inside them.  A chronicler of lucid dreams is just a clerk who has agreed to be haunted.  The job is to distill chaos into narrative: take the flood, find the spine, write the sentence that makes a night mean something.  My trouble is that the volumes refuse to stay in order.  Volume nine describes this cabin's stove with the door I installed last spring.  Volume twenty-two is dated a year that has not happened.  I stopped correcting the dates around the time I stopped being certain which self was writing.  The grave was only the latest entry filed out of sequence. 

The cabin smells of cold ash and mouse.  I pried the loose board behind the bunk and took out the first volume, which is not paper.  It is a birch bark scroll, peeled the summer I was nine, rolled tight and bound with a bootlace, the pencil letters gone brown.  "I DREAMED I WAS A WOLF AND I DID NOT WANT TO COME BACK," it says, and nothing else: no date, no index, no apology.  That is feral nostalgia, the kind that does not want a photograph.  It wants the animal back, and it will eat the present to get it. 

I have a method for it, and the method is ugly: I get jealous of my past selves.  Envy is the only compass that works down there.  I read the old pages until I resent the girl who wrote them, then I sleep, and the resentment walks me straight to her.  At nineteen I could soar.  Volume four has the whole technique in three lines: run, refuse the ground, keep refusing.  Now I get a foot above the floorboards and hover there like a moth with a paperwork problem. 

That night the dream gave me the cabin, the stove, the bunk, and the two girls I used to be.  The nine-year-old sat cross-legged with the bark across her knees, writing.  The nineteen-year-old stood in the open door with the whole black valley behind her, bored, magnificent, waiting for me to notice the sky.  I knew I was dreaming, which changes nothing about how much you want what they have.  I told the child to give me the scroll.  She held it against her chest, and the cabin went the color of a bruise, and I felt the dream start to tear along its seam.  Jealousy had brought me here and jealousy was going to burn the whole archive down. 

So I did the clerk's work instead.  I sat on the floor and told her about the stone in the valley, the lichen, my name, the date that came too soon.  I gave the chaos a spine: I have seen my grave, and I am still the one holding the pencil.  The bruise went out of the walls.  The child handed me the bark, because a nine-year-old will trade anything for a story that scares her.  The nineteen-year-old laughed at me from the doorway, then stepped off the porch and soared, no run at all, just refusal, rising until she was a scratch on the stars.  I have watched that a hundred times and it still takes the top of my head off.  Then I ran, and refused the ground, and kept refusing, and got maybe thirty feet, high enough to see the cabin's roof and the white scar of the cemetery below it.  Thirty feet is not the sky, but it is a vantage, and a vantage is all a narrative is. 

In the morning I wrote the grave on the first page of volume thirty-two, not the last.  An ending is only a placement, and I am the one who places things.  I added one line to the birch bark in pencil, rolled it, tied the bootlace, and wedged it back behind the bunk for whatever woman comes up here next to get jealous of.  Let her be feral about me for once.  Let her read that I saw the stone, went up, flew thirty feet, and came back down to write it.
