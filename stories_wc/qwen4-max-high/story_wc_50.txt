The mountain cracked in the first week of autumn, and after that the season lay wounded and still.  In the lull between the quake and its aftershocks, the marble cavern beneath Vessa let go of its echoes.  They came loose from the stone the way heat leaves a cooling loaf, faint and silver, visible only at the edge of sight.  Once a generation the town went down with copper jars to harvest them. 

Odo the locksmith was the best harvester in Vessa because he could not look straight at anything.  A fever at nine had burned a hole through the middle of his seeing, and the world arrived to him sideways, in the margins.  Echo-light lived in those margins.  Where other men stared and saw wet rock, Odo glanced aside and caught the whole shimmering crop. 

But the harvest gate had a timer, and the quake had broken it.  Without it the sluice stayed shut while the echoes rose and thinned and went back into the stone, unharvested.  Brannoc, who built the thing, had been dead forty years and had left no drawings, only a brass drum, a falling weight, and an escapement with three teeth filed away.  The town gave Odo nine days to reverse-engineer a dead man's mind. 

He could not read the small work by looking at it.  So he set a tin mirror behind the drum and spent the lull studying reflection angles at different times of day, while the shaft above the cavern let down its single blade of sun.  At noon the light struck flat and told him nothing.  Late in the afternoon it raked across the brass, and every file mark stood up out of the metal in shadow.  He kept a ledger of hours and angles, the way he kept everything. 

On the fourth evening the low light showed him what noon had drowned: a spiral of hair-fine scratches inside the drum's lip, polished bright at three points by long rubbing.  A steel tongue had ridden that spiral.  The escapement had never counted seconds at all.  It had listened.  Brannoc had tuned the tongue to the note the far wall gave back, so that when the cavern answered itself the tongue shivered, skipped the three missing teeth, and let the weight fall.  The gate was not a clock; it was a courtesy, waiting for the mountain to finish speaking. 

Odo sat back on his heels and laughed, because this was exactly why he kept a cellar.  Beneath his shop stood eleven shelves of jars, each labeled in a plain hand with date, hour, and the height of the sun: rain on the tin roof; the year the bells rang wrong; his wife's laugh, caught the winter before she died.  Wonder spoils if you leave it floating.  You store it in the cellar of reason, he told his apprentices, so it keeps until you are hungry. 

He filed a new tongue from a clock spring, tuned it against the far wall's note, and hung the weight.  Testing cost him three days; two days of the lull remained, and the town could feel the mountain gathering itself.  They carried four hundred copper jars down into the marble dark and set them under the sluice.  Odo turned his face toward the wall, watching the door frame out of the corner of his eye, and waited.  The cavern spoke, and far off the marble answered, and the tongue shivered, and the weight fell.  Silver came through the sluice like fish through a gate, and every jar in the gallery filled at the edges of his sight. 

They sealed the harvest before the first aftershock, which took the church steeple and nothing else.  All winter Vessa came down to Odo's cellar with requests.  A girl who had lost her mother in the crack asked for something from before, and he read his labels and chose a summer morning: a scythe, a river, a woman calling a name twice.  He told her to hold the jar a little to the side, not straight on.  Then he looked at the shelves and let her weep in the margin of his seeing, where he could bear it, and where everything worth keeping had always been.
