The glade went cobalt at dusk, and Maren's roots drank the color.  Eleven years ago she had knelt here to take the covenant, and the ground had taken her back, closing over her calves like water closing over a stone.  Root-bound, the village called her, as if it were a diagnosis.  Visionary, her mother had called it, and had said the word the way other people said lottery. 

The bargain was simple: the glade would let its dreamers meet, one dream crossing another, and from those crossings the village would read what was coming.  The Confluence, the great crossing, was scheduled for her granddaughter's old age.  It came instead in her thirty-first year, the spring she rooted, forty years early, and it came wrong.  Two futures slid across each other and stopped there, one in front of the other, and the light behind them went out.  An eclipsed covenant, Maren wrote in her book, because broken was not the right word.  Nothing had been torn; something had merely been stood in front of.  Since then the eleven rooted dreamers of the glade had dreamed alone, and the village had planted its fields by guesswork, and the children had stopped being told what to expect. 

Maren was curious the way a locksmith is curious.  She asked the glade its questions in order, four each dusk, and wrote down which ones made the blue go pale.  It had taken her nine years to learn that the crossing was not gone but shadowed, and one more to learn what could move a shadow.  Tonight she had the answer in a jam jar on her knee: a clipping of maze topiary, four inches of yew her mother had cut from the hospice labyrinth the week before she died.  The labyrinth had been grown as a map, every hedge a dreaming path, every turning a place where two sleepers might brush.  In the jar the clipping had put out white threads, and Maren's hands shook so badly the water rang against the glass.  She was terrified and she was laughing, and she had stopped bothering to tell the two apart. 

Grafting cannot be done cleanly.  You cut the stock, you cut the scion, you bind wound to wound, and if the scars take to each other the two lives become one life.  Maren opened the old seam above her right ankle, where the glade had first come into her, and set the clipping's cut face against it.  She bound them with a strip of her own sleeve.  Then she lay back in the cobalt grass and let the dusk take the top off her mind. 

The maze came up around her instantly, the way things do in sleep, yew walls to her shoulders and the smell of cut green everywhere.  Every wall was stitched with pale seams, and she understood that she was walking through scar tissue, hers and not hers.  At each graft she found a dreamer: old Bettan with her hip like a knotted rope, the ferryman's boy with his burned forearm, each one alone in a separate corridor until she touched the seam and let them through. 

Ahead a wide bright crossing opened, five paths meeting in a floor of white light, and every dreamer turned toward it like moths.  Her mother's rule, the only one she had ever bothered to repeat: heed the ache, not the light.  Maren stood still and listened to her ankle.  The bright crossing did not hurt.  The narrow way to her left, choked with yew, pulled at the graft until she gasped, and she heeded it, and went in.  It cost her; the hedge took skin from both arms as she pushed. 

Then the walls fell away into a small dim clearing where two paths crossed over an ordinary brown scar in the dirt.  Not the Confluence, not the great shining one, only the place where two dreams touch and can be read.  Bettan knelt beside her and put her scarred hip against Maren's scarred ankle, and the crossing widened by a hand's breadth.  That was the whole method; that was all the covenant had ever asked.  The early future stayed where it was, eclipsing what it eclipsed, and they simply grew around it, the way yew grows around a wire. 

Maren woke with her cheek in the blue grass, her ankle blazing, and ten voices in her head arguing about rain.  Above her, out of her own wound, the clipping put out its first new leaf.  She lay there shaking, afraid of the size of it, and so happy she could have bitten the earth.
