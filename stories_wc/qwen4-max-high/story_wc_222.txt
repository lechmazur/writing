The spire stood above the cloud sea, sheathed in ice, and every morning Ilsa watched the sun rise two degrees north of where the almanac promised.  It was the eleventh year when the sky was wrong.  Rime grew on the inside of the windows, and the clouds below turned gold at an hour that had no name.  Below, in the villages, they had stopped trusting sundials; up here, the Guild had stopped trusting the sky.  Ilsa was apprenticed to wonder.  That was the office's true name: Master Corvin held the Chair of Wonder, and his vow was to meet what he did not understand with open arms.  The Guild's vow was shorter: correct the record.  So each winter a Warden climbed with the Amendment, and each winter Ilsa copied it out in her best hand, and the sky was declared to have always been this way.  Corvin let her copy.  Then he would take her to the roof, point at the new star nobody had named, and laugh like a boy at a fair.  He was sacredly playful; he prayed with his knees on the ice and his fingers carving chessmen, and he said the sky was a game whose rules nobody had read yet.  Ilsa carried her father's pocket sundial lid, brass, stamped with the old hours, the only noon in the spire that had never been amended.  It had come home in a swap.  The thief took only from the Guild's melting-hoard, never from the poor, and she never left a place empty: where a lid had been, she set a hand-carved chess piece with a secret inside.  Ilsa's pawn had held a slip of paper: The sun did not move; the ledger did.  That secret was why she had climbed nine thousand steps to be apprenticed to wonder.  This year the thief came up the steps herself.  Ilsa found her in the hoard-room at midnight, ice on her lashes, a king in her fist, weighing a lid.  "You should call the Warden," the thief said, not moving.  "You should tell me your name," Ilsa said.  "Halla.  I was Corvin's apprentice before you.  I would not copy, so he taught me to carve instead."  Ilsa's hands were shaking, and she made herself set them flat on the cold shelf, the way she did before touching a telescope.  "The Warden is asleep in the north cell," she said.  "The Amendment goes down at dawn inside the white king, sealed, because the Guild does not want the valleys to know there was ever anything to amend."  "I seal it myself."  Halla smiled and opened her fist.  The king in her palm was the twin of the Warden's king, carved from the same Guild boxwood, hollow as a bell.  "Paper rots in the cloud damp," she said.  "Brass does not."  "I have been waiting eleven years for someone up here to open a door."  Ilsa understood then what the swap was for, and what her father's lid was for, and how carefully a person could ruin herself.  They went to Corvin, who was carving at his vigil, and he did not look up.  "I swore to sign their Amendment," he said.  "I never swore to let it arrive."  He took the lid, turned it in the lamplight, and laughed once, quietly, the way he laughed at the unnamed star.  "Nobody knows what the sky is doing," he said.  "That is not a shame.  It is an invitation."  Ilsa fitted the brass disc into the king's base and screwed the threads shut, and it weighed within a grain of the Warden's, because Halla had spent eleven years learning the weight of Guild boxwood.  The swap took four seconds at the Warden's bedside, hand for hand, king for king.  Ilsa's whole life went into those four seconds: the copying hand, the obedient girl, the village that had learned to distrust its own noon.  At dawn the Warden took his set, thanked them, and started down the nine thousand steps with the old hours riding in his king.  Halla went out the hoard-room window with three lids and three carved pawns, keeping her ledger balanced.  Ilsa stayed, because someone had to be here when the archive read the brass and the Guild came back up the steps angry.  Corvin found her on the roof, arms out, palms up, letting the wrong sun warm her face.  "They will unmake us," she said, and she was smiling, and she was afraid, and both were true.  "Probably," he said.  "Look: the shadow misses the mark again today.  Nobody knows why."  She opened her hands wider.
