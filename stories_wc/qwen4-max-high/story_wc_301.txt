The ledger had two hundred and nine entries, and every one began with a map reference.  Grid, easting, northing, then the truth: where the Ouse had undercut a bank, where it had handed back a shingle bar, where it had taken someone.  Entry two hundred and ten had been waiting eleven years, a ruled page she kept at the front of the book.  Whenever she came to it she alluded instead: the incident at 614 227, the crossing, that August.  Four years of sertraline had made the allusion bearable, a stone smoothed flat by a tongue.  Six weeks after stopping the medication, the flatness was gone.  The doctor had stretched the taper over five months, and still grief came back like blood into a slept-on arm: pins, then heat, then the whole weight of the limb.  Nell could feel again, which meant she could finally do the work. 

The old station at Marsh Halt stood at 612 229, ivy over the gutters, the platform edge furred with moss.  Trains had stopped in 1968; the viaduct still carried nothing across the water, beautifully.  In her rucksack, wrapped in a towel, was Toby's accordion.  He had patched its bellows with insulation tape at eleven, badly, proud of himself, and the seams still leaked.  She had carried it here to drop it off the viaduct.  She was not looking for absolution, she told herself, only for a path to forgiving herself.  The truth she had never written: at fifteen she had studied the 1961 survey sheet, seen the ford marked at 614 227, and told her brother the water would be at his knees.  It had been at her chest, and then it had been over his head. 

The station toilets were the only room with a roof left, and the tiles were a hundred hands deep in writing.  She had come in out of the drizzle; she stayed for the conversation.  Someone in blue marker had written JUMP HERE, DEEP, with an arrow and the reference 614 227.  Beneath it, in biro, older: NOT DEEP NOW, 1994, SORRY JAMIE.  Beneath that, a third hand, felt-tip, patient as a teacher: THE MAP IS OLD, THE RIVER IS NEW, GO AND MEASURE IT.  And a fourth, small, near the cistern: it moved, not you.  She sat on the cracked pan and cried in a way the tablets had not permitted for four years, ugly and loud under the ivy.  Then she did what she always did with a claim: she went to check it. 

The catchment office paid her to keep the river's record, and their word for her was daringly methodical.  She waded in at 614 227 until the October current gripped her thighs, then sounded three transects with a ranging pole, tape pegged to the bank, pencil on waterproof paper.  One point eight metres at the ford's old centre.  The channel had walked forty metres north since 1961; the sheet she had trusted at fifteen had been true for a river that no longer existed.  It did not make her innocent.  She had still hurried him, still laughed when he said the water smelled wrong.  But she had been fifteen, holding a map, believing that a printed thing could not lie, and the river had not been consulted.  Two truths, both hers, both finally on the same page. 

Back on the platform she unwrapped the accordion, meaning still to throw it.  She pulled the bellows and the taped seams hissed, air bleeding out of her brother's bad repair, and a chord came anyway, thin and sour and alive.  It leaked and it played.  She sat with it until the light went orange through the ivy.  Then she opened the ledger at entry two hundred and ten, wrote 614 227, and did not allude.  Toby Alder, eleven, drowned here on the fourteenth of August because I told him the ford was shallow, and the ford had moved, and I did not know that rivers move.  She added the depth, the date, the forty metres, all of it checkable.  Then she took the marker from her bag and went back to the toilets and added a fifth hand under the fourth: THE SHEET WAS 1961, I HAVE MEASURED IT, IT MOVED.  Not forgiveness, not yet, but the first honest survey of the ground it would have to cross.  She carried the accordion home along the trackbed, and every few steps she pulled the bellows, and every time it hissed and sounded, hissed and sounded, all the way to the road.
