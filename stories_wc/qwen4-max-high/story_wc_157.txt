The Thought Levy passed in April, and by June my greenhouse was the last room in the city where anyone could think undeclared.  The auditors' meters listened for subvocal speech, that small inner voice we use to rehearse a sentence before we risk it.  Bees have no inner voice, and forty thousand of them under glass made nonsense of every reading.  I had already paid a month's wages for thinking about my mother's hands.  So the club came to me, the district entomologist, because my glass was the only honest roof left. 

Marek arrived in a coat too good for him, carrying a gourd rattle and a suspension letter from the Institute of Lexicon folded into a paper bird.  He had been selling declensions in a wine cellar, and when the board interrogated him he confessed everything, fully, in a dialect they could not transcribe.  They cannot tax what they cannot write down.  "Candidly cryptic," he said, shaking the gourd.  "I never lie; I only decline to be legible."  One seed knocked inside it. 

The club had eleven members and a language with no word for ownership, and they needed my hives.  Their grammar conjugated for soil instead of tense: a thing said in clay meant something else in sand.  Beautiful, useless, untaxable.  But a language nobody can hear is only a hobby, and Marek wanted a sentence the whole district could read. 

So we joined the community gardens.  Nine allotments, nine fences cut in one night, beds realigned until the rows ran north without a break.  We warped the district like a loom, and my bees would fly the weft.  Marigold for one glyph, borage for another, phacelia for the stroke that turned a question into a refusal.  From the survey balloons it would resolve into one word: tended, in the case that admits no owner.  Eleven seeds from the gourd gave us the key, the shift in the rows that told a reader where to begin.  Marek planted them himself, walking the beds at dawn with his cuffs turned back, charming the allotment wardens out of their own rules. 

The twelfth he kept.  It rode in the gourd against his ribs and rattled when he laughed, which was often.  I asked him why, and he said, "Because a man who spends everything has nothing left to guard." 

By August everything arrived at once.  The levy office raised the assessment on untranscribed thought, which meant they had admitted the existence of thought they could not transcribe.  Demolition notices went up on three of the nine gardens.  A survey balloon dropped low each morning, my hives swarmed twice, and the heat warped the greenhouse panes until the world bent like a spoon.  Marek loved it.  He stood among the bees, paper bird in his teeth, and said chaos was a sentence nobody had finished reading.  That night he put the gourd in my hand and said, "You keep bees; you already know how to guard something that stings." 

On the morning the phacelia opened, the auditors came down the row with their meters and their cameras.  The beds were blooming glyph by glyph; from the balloon the word would read clean if you knew where to start.  The chief auditor photographed everything and asked Marek, politely, what it said.  Marek told him.  He told him the truth, at length, candidly, in a grammar conjugated for clay, and the meter recorded a noise like rain on a tin roof.  He also told them, truthfully, about the twelfth seed in my hand; a truth they could not use was his favorite gift. 

Then the chief auditor turned to me and named a price for the key: exemption for my glasshouse, forever, in writing.  It sat in my palm now, warm as a small animal.  One seed, one beginning; without it the sentence had no first letter, and no reader could enter.  Around us the district converged: the balloon descending, the mowers starting on the condemned beds, the swarm pouring out of hive nine like smoke going the wrong way. 

I shook the gourd once and said, honestly, that it was a rattle, that I used it to settle swarms.  The swarm folded down into the borage.  Both things were true.  The meter heard a woman being candid and wrote down nothing it could bill.  They mowed three gardens, logged the rest as ornamental, and Marek told them the whole truth again until they stopped listening. 

The sentence stays unfinished, which is to say it stays ours.  I am not building a revolution; I am standing guard over one seed's worth of defiance, and it rattles when I laugh.
