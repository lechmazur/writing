After the shutdown, the town called me blessed and would not sit beside me.  For sixty years the station had sold slow hours to men who wanted their fortunes to outlast their rivals, and the town grew fat on the trade.  Then the field buckled, the inspectors came, and the whole apparatus was shut down in a single night.  The field had let go of me unevenly, and now my words reached a listener half a beat after my mouth finished with them, so talking to me was like calling down a well.  They said God had touched me, and they said it from doorways, with the door mostly closed. 

Once a year I climbed the tower to the time dilation observation deck, where the evacuation lamps still burned amber over chairs nobody had sat in since the alarms.  The dome gave my footsteps back a second after I made them, so I learned to walk in the rhythm of my own echo, and the deck and I kept a silence together that was never quite empty.  I always brought the family sky-book, its cover worn bald around the gilded compass rose applique my great-grandmother had stitched there when she still navigated by stars and paper.  The gilt had been rubbed off the north point by four generations of thumbs.  My mother used to say the rose was not decoration but a promise: that somewhere a direction holds. 

Our custom was plain: on the anniversary of the night our family came to this valley, whoever was alive drew the sky exactly as it hung above them, dated the page, and offered no explanation.  The date never changed; only the place did, from our porch to that deck.  My grandmother's page put the Hook low over the ridge; my mother's lifted it a hand's width; my own pages, one each year, showed it climbing like something in a hurry.  For years I believed I was simply drawing badly. 

Then, in the black glass of the oculus, I caught the glimpse I had been refusing since I was eleven.  My mother had stayed at the lever to drop the field, and the field, dying, had stretched her instead of releasing her.  She stood temporally stretched across eleven seconds at the console, a pale smear of woman with her hand just beginning to rise.  Each year that hand was a fraction higher, and each year her last word came up through the floorboards as a vibration too low to be called sound. 

That was the winter I stopped asking why and began asking how much.  I laid the book open beneath the rose and compared the Hook's climb with the almanacs the town still printed, and the arithmetic was merciless and kind: eleven of my years, ninety-one of theirs, steady as a pulse.  The custom's ban on explanation turned out to be the first rule of measurement: record what you see, and when, and keep your grief out of the columns.  Confusion, it turns out, is only a bearing nobody has taken yet.  The deck's needle was useless, dragged sideways by the corpse of the field, but true north has never cared what pulls at iron.  True north is what stays fixed while everything else swings, and the only way to find it is to watch, patiently, what moves.  So I used my own bad drawings as data, and the drift gave me the axis, and the axis gave me north, and north gave me the deck's true orientation at last. 

Then, from the crawl of her hand between my pages, I found the anniversary when it would finish: forty years for me, three hundred for the town.  By then the town would be a rumor and the tower a stump, and I would still be slow, still shunned, still blessed.  No one else could bear the waiting up here; I had been made for it.  I drew the sky as it hung, and dated the page, and under the date I wrote a bearing instead of a question.  Then I set my thumb on the bare north point of the rose and told the floor that I would be here when her hand reached the top of its rise.  A second later the dome handed my voice back to me, agreeing.
