The last fish was a sand lance no longer than a finger, and the harbormaster rang the trawler's bell for it anyway.  He rang it the way his mother had, hard enough to mean it, and the bronze split from lip to shoulder.  After that the town had nothing to ring and nothing to catch, and the strait went still.  Pell carried the broken bell up the sapphire viaduct at dawn, because that was where the ledger lived. 

The viaduct crossed the strait on two arms, one over water and one over land, and the old tidewrights had built it as a balance.  Whatever the town took from the sea, the stone was meant to give back, drop by drop, into the seaward pan.  Sapphire weeps only in first light, and only if something warm touches it. 

Pell was the warm thing available.  He was a lukewarm mage, which is a precise description and not an insult, though the town used it as one.  His hands could bring any object to blood heat and no further.  He could not boil a kettle, light a lamp, or close a wound.  He could keep a sick child comfortable through a winter night, and he had, eleven times. 

The town had kept the ledger devoutly for three hundred years, which is to say it had kept the ceremony.  When the catches thinned, the priest decided the debt could be settled in one night of fire, and hired a mage who could burn.  Sapphire does not forgive heat: the feed channel cracked, the balance jammed, and the sea arm rose a hand's width and stayed there.  The tide had not turned in forty days, and the weed on the pilings had dried to paper.  Devotion like that is glass, flawless right up until it is asked to bend. 

Pell set the cracked bell under the fracture, mouth up, over the cistern's open throat, so it became a cup instead of a voice.  Water leaked out through its crack at a rate he could count, which made the bell a clock and a valve as well.  For nine dawns he lay on the cold stone with his palm against the sapphire, counting drops into the bell and drops out of it.  Nineteen minutes of sunrise gave him four hundred and six drops, and the cistern under the seaward arm wanted nine million.  He did the arithmetic twice on the back of a fish tally, then a third time to be sure.  Nine million drops was not a miracle; it was a schedule.  Sixty years, if the sun rose every day and he never missed a dawn.  He was forty-three. 

It never occurred to him that a debt older than the town might be too large for one pair of hands.  He had warmed eleven children through eleven winters, and it seemed to him that warming a strait was the same work, only slower.  So he began to rebuild the channel.  He packed the fracture with crushed sapphire and lime, warmed it to blood heat, and held it there while it set.  Blood heat was the only temperature the stone would accept, so the only mage who could mend the ledger was the one the town had laughed at.  He was naive enough to find that funny, and earnest enough to keep going. 

The harbormaster climbed up on the twentieth dawn and asked what he expected to accomplish.  Pell said the tide would come back, and the shad with it, and he said it the way other men say next spring.  "Not in your lifetime," the harbormaster said.  "No," Pell agreed, and lost his count, and started the hundred over. 

The harbormaster stayed to hold the bell steady, because his hands were idle for the first time in his life.  By the third week the priest came too, and the girl who had gutted the last fish, and Pell taught them to count.  The priest who had wanted one night of fire learned to say forty-one, forty-two, forty-three.  Pell wrote the rate on the pier in charcoal so it would outlive him: four hundred and six drops, nineteen minutes, every dawn.  The imbalance would be righted; the ledger had never asked whose hand finished the paying.  Below them the strait lay empty and patient, and the bell that would never ring again filled and emptied, filled and emptied, and the stone gave back what it owed one lukewarm drop at a time.
