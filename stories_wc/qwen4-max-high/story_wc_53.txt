The carousel halted on a Tuesday, or a Thursday, or forty years ago.  Nen kept all three dates in her pocket, the way she kept everything else she could not sort.  Above the cloud floor, the lagoon had been kinetic for as long as the ride turned, its water dragged in a slow bright ring by the carousel's spokes.  Now it was settling.  That was why she worked.  Stillness dropped things.  For years the current had held the lost stuff aloft: buttons, teeth, a brass compass, other people's sentences.  Nen waded out at dawn and took what the water could no longer carry.  The others had gone down through the cloud years ago, or last week. 

The camera came up on the ninth day, or the ninth minute.  A disposable thing, plastic, its film long fogged, but the wheel on top still turned.  Click.  She had been waiting for a clock like that.  Since the halt her hours came loose: she kept booking her own death for next week, and remembering the carousel's stopping as a thing she had not done yet.  The wheel gave her order back, one click at a time.  Click, then wade.  Click, then eat.  Twenty-seven frames, twenty-seven befores and afters. 

She collected the sentences too.  Riders used to lean over the rail and talk, and the lagoon kept what they said the way it kept buttons.  Nen skimmed them into a tin: a man telling his brother he never wanted the boat anyway; a woman saying she would learn to swim in the spring; a boy asking if anyone down there was waiting for him.  Fragments only.  She had learned that whole stories sank too fast to reach. 

Once she had meant to go down.  She had drawn the cloud floor as a coastline on the back of a ticket, marked the places where the light thinned, planned a descent by rope and patience.  That was before the scavenging paid, before the water gave her enough each morning to make one more morning bearable.  The map had worn soft in her pocket, then worn through.  Ambition did not break here; it eroded, grain by grain, like the rail under her palm. 

On the last still day she saw her ghost standing in the lagoon.  Not a reflection.  It faced her though she had turned away, and it was older, and it was dry.  The woman she would become if she stayed: dry, old, still on the platform.  Nen had come out here for this, every dawn, with her tin. 

You can be talked to, she said.  Everything in this lagoon can be traded.  She opened the tin and read the fragments aloud, offering them like coins: the boat nobody wanted, the swimming lesson postponed to spring, the boy's question.  Take these, she said.  Let me have the map back.  Let me be the one who went down. 

The ghost listened with her own face.  Then it said, in her voice, that it had heard all of these before, from her, on the platform, every year, in the spring.  It said the fragments were not payment.  They were the receipts. 

Nen stood in the flat water and felt the whole shape of it: not one refusal, but a thousand small delays, each reasonable, each the size of a grain.  Regret arrived then, late and out of order, the way everything came now.  She was sorry for the rope she never braided.  She was sorry she had learned to love the settling. 

What do you want, then, she asked.  The ghost held out its hand for the camera.  Of course.  The wheel was the only thing in the lagoon that still went forward, and she had been using it to stand still.  She laid it in that other cold palm, which was hers.  The ghost wound it, all twenty-seven frames at once, a small dry ratcheting, and the sound was every spring she had promised herself.  Then the wheel would not turn anymore.  The ghost sank, satisfied, into water that no longer moved. 

Nen was left with the tin, the compass, and no clock at all.  She scratched a coastline on the lid.  She took the guy-line off the halted carousel and began to braid.  It would take days, or a minute, or forty years.  She did not need the order now.  She needed the rope.  Behind her the lagoon lay flat as a held breath, and somewhere under the cloud floor the light thinned where she had marked it, and she went on braiding.
