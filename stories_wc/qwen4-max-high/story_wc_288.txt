The library rode the mist tide above the clouds, and twice a day its lower stacks drowned.  Mera timed her work by a brass sundial, because up here the sun was the only honest clock.  When the gnomon's shadow touched the ninth hour, the mist fell, the stacks breathed, and the stone circle in the floor began to hum.  Nine standing stones, hauled up from the drowned world below, older than any book she shelved.  She was the last person alive who had heard the sounders singing.  Sixty years extinct; she had been a girl on a fishing skiff when the last pod passed beneath her, and she had thought the sea was clearing its throat.  Everyone else in the library knew the sounders only as cylinders of recorded hum.  The reincarnation memory had come to her in the spring, and since then her mornings were what the librarians called brittle awakenings.  She woke thin as blown glass, holding a life that was not this one, and any voice before the tide turned could crack her.  In that other life she had rung the hunting bell at Kessle Point.  She had called the boats out to the last pod, and been paid in salt and silver.  So the rule of brittle awakenings suited her: no one may speak to the newly remembered until they choose to speak first.  Isolation was the medicine, and she took it like medicine, bitter and on schedule.  The boy Odo brought her tea anyway and set it outside the door.  He wanted to learn the sounders' language, and she kept telling him the hum could not be taught.  It was a lie she needed.  If only she could hear them, then only she was necessary, and being necessary felt almost like being forgiven.  At the ninth hour she went down to the stones with the last unopened cylinder and the sundial.  The sundial's plate was atomically reflective, flat to the width of a single atom, and that was the whole trick of translation.  It gave her back her own face grain by grain, which she preferred to any softer mirror.  Set it among the stones, let the hum rise, and the mist above the mirror stood in ridges and lattices, the shape of a sound too low for ears.  The sounders had learned their language by the hum of an ancient stone circle, and only that hum could unfold it again.  She set the phonograph horn against a standing stone and played the cylinder into the ring.  Lattices bloomed on the brass, and she wrote what they said, and what they said was a roll call.  Names, a whole pod of them, and then a gap where the answers should have come, and then the same names again, slower.  They had been calling the pod that never came back, and they had kept calling for eleven years.  Mera sat down on the wet stone with her hands over the mirror, ruining the figures.  She had spent forty years as the only room where that sound still existed, and she had called it loneliness, which is a thing you wait inside.  Independence was different; independence was a thing you built and then handed over.  She had translated everything else in this room; it was time to translate her own solitude.  She thought of the whales calling into water that gave them nothing back, and of herself calling into a door she kept shut.  Odo was on the stair with the cold tea, pretending to shelve.  She said, Come down.  Her voice cracked on it, the way ice cracks, and she did not shatter.  She taught him to lay the plate level, to wait for the ninth hour, to read a lattice from its center out.  It took a season of tides.  He was slower than her and better at the vowels, and the hum did not care who was listening; that was the mercy of stones.  The bell-ringer and the girl on the skiff and the old woman with the brass had to coexist in one body now, and she stopped trying to wake as only one of them.  That was the cure for brittle awakenings, and no book in the drowned stacks held it.  On the last morning of the season she left the sundial in the ring and went up early, before the tide turned, and knocked on Odo's door.  She was still thin as glass; she spoke first anyway.  Read me the names, she said, and I will tell you which ones I heard from the water.  Below them the mist rose, the lower stacks drowned, and the stones hummed on for anyone.
