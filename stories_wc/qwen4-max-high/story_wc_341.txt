The gondola smelled of cold grease and pine, and its scratched window turned the valley lights into dusty starlight.  I ride it up every dusk, to the summit station, because that is where the string broke.  In my pocket is the spool: four hundred meters of linen line, wound tight, one end frayed white. 

I am the aviation meteorologist for the strip on the valley floor; I tell pilots what the sky intends.  In March I brought my daughter Nell up here to launch a sounding balloon, because she wanted to know where weather comes from.  She tied a card to the tail with her name and our address, so the sky could write back.  The line went up singing, and then it snapped, and the blue took all of it.  Nell laughed; she was nine, and losses were still funny to her.  She died in April, quickly, of something I had no instrument to forecast. 

Since then I have kept two logs.  The official one uses the proper codes, cumulus and altocumulus and lenticular, and goes to the pilots.  The other sorts clouds by emotion: hopeful, forgiving, patient, unwilling.  The second log is the accurate one. 

The station at the top is closed now, but the cable still runs for me, and inside the shuttered room stands the old brass telescope we used to track pilot balloons.  Its barrel is green at the seams, and the lens still has Nell's fingerprint on it, a smudge of oil and dust. 

Tonight the valley is calm, capped by warm air, the day's heat locked underneath it.  We call that quietly explosive: nothing looks wrong, and everything is loaded.  I have been quietly explosive since April. 

I set the telescope on the bearing the balloon took, elevation forty degrees, and I look at nothing.  Through her fingerprint, the nothing has grain, like a photograph of a room someone has just left.  This is my work now: to prove that empty spaces remember.  A pilot would say the balloon burst and the card fell into some field and rotted.  I say the air is not empty, only unread.  Every balloon I ever released left a hole in the air shaped like itself, and the air closed around that shape and kept it. 

I cannot prove it with instruments.  So I do it the way my trade does everything: I write it down, in a book that will be archived for a hundred years.  No letter ever came to our address, so I am writing the reply.  Under emotion I enter a category I have never used: remembering.  Beside it, the sky condition: clear.  Beside that, her name.  That is how I memorialize her, in the only ledger the world is obliged to keep. 

Then I take the spool out to the platform where the cable hums and the wind comes up the slope cold.  For months I have thought of the break as an amputation.  Tonight, with the wood warm in my palm, I think instead about winding.  Nothing was cut off; it was paid out.  Somewhere above forty degrees the rest of that line is still running, unbroken, wound on something I cannot see.  In the second log I have given this a name, the way I name everything: spooled wholeness.  A thing can leave your hands and stay entire. 

I hold the spool and let the wind take the frayed end, paying out line, turn after turn.  Four hundred meters goes up the dark like a slow white thought.  When the last turn slips off the wood I do not grab for it.  The spool is empty now, and light, and I put it back in my pocket. 

Below, the strip lights come on, dusty stars in a bowl of haze.  The radio in my coat asks for the evening observation.  A pilot is coming down the corridor from the north and wants to know what the air is doing.  I tell him the truth in the official language: calm surface, cap aloft, unstable beneath, expect nothing and then expect everything.  He thanks me and flies on.  In the second log, under a clear sky, I write: patient, forgiving, remembering. 

Then I ride the cable down with the spool in my pocket, watching the place where the line went.  It is not empty.  It is holding everything, quietly, the way the air holds a summer day until it lets go.
