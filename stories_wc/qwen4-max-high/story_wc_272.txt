The lighthouse kept its library the way a drowned man keeps his pockets: full of things nobody would ever ask for.  Mira climbed to it every evening, because it was the only room left where faces stayed put.  She had been the coast's portrait artist for eleven years, until the night the lamp cracked and the world came apart along its seams.  Now every sitter arrived in pieces.  A fisherman's jaw belonged to his brother; a bride's eyes were her mother's at forty; and Mira, who could once mix a cheekbone out of ochre and patience, painted facets and was paid in apologies.  Crestfallen was too gentle a word for it.  She had stopped signing her work. 

In the library she found the box again, where she always found it, on the shelf of tide tables.  A tarot box, its hinge snapped, its lid hanging like a jaw.  It had belonged to Sena, the keeper's daughter, who used to sit for Mira and ask what the cards meant.  Mira never answered her, because Mira only ever asked questions that needed no answer: Isn't the light lovely tonight?  Hold still, won't you?  Sena had gone out in the boat the night the lamp broke, and the sea had returned her in fragments too: a shoe, a name, a laugh someone swore they heard off the rocks. 

Inside the box were seventy-eight cards, and every one of them was Sena's face at a different age, painted by hands Mira did not remember owning.  Nine, nineteen, eighty, drowned, unborn. 

The walls were closing in.  They had been for a month: an inch a night, the shelves pressing toward each other, the lighthouse narrowing around its own forgotten heart.  At this rate the room would shut like a book on Thursday.  Mira had three nights. 

What good is a lighthouse that shows you everything at once?  The cracked lens did not scatter the beam; it scattered the seen.  Every night it swept the coast and dealt the world like a deck, and every night the cards landed face up and out of order. 

So she would redesign it.  Not the lens; she was no glazier.  The box.  A hinge is only a decision about what stays joined. 

The library's books were sewn, not glued, each signature bound with linen thread the color of salt.  They did not hold stories; they held versions, one per signature, stitched shut so they could not wander.  She began unpicking them.  She sat on the floor with a sail needle and untangled the threads, book after book, following each strand back through the knots until it ran free, and the versions it held came loose in her hands: Sena at nine, Sena at nineteen, Sena in the boat, Sena on the rocks, Sena grown old and tired of the sea.  It took two nights, and her fingers bled, and the walls came on.  She threaded the strands through the broken hinge and re-strung the cards in order, seventy-eight small windows on one linen spine.  A box that could hold a whole life instead of scattering one. 

But a redesigned box is still only a box under a broken lamp.  Up here the beam would keep shuffling her.  She had to go down.  Down the ninety stairs, past the sleeping engine, into the oil room at the root of the tower, where the lamp's light had never reached and never could.  She had spent her whole life climbing toward brightness because she thought seeing was the same as being shown.  Who taught her that?  The dark was necessary; she understood it now the way you understand a hand on your shoulder. 

Behind her the library shut with a sound like a held breath.  She did not look back, because what is a closed book but a finished one? 

In the oil room she opened the box.  The linen threads glowed.  Sea-light, faint and patient, the ache the water keeps after a body has gone through it.  By that small glow the seventy-eight cards lay in one line, and the line was a face, and the face was Sena at twenty-six, the age she was, the age she stayed.  Mira painted her there in the dark, badly, weeping, correctly.  Not a resurrection.  A portrait. 

When she came up at dawn the lamp was still broken and the world still came in pieces, but she carried one whole face under her arm, and she signed it.  Isn't that what light is for?
