The locksmith kept no shop.  He appeared where seven alleys met in shadow behind the saltworks, under starlight, with a coat lined in envelopes.  Each envelope held a secret someone had paid him to keep, and he never opened one.  Nobody had ever seen him arrive or leave. 

Mira had watched him for thirty-one years from the seawall.  She was a copyist, gray-dressed, apologetic in doorways, the sort of woman people forgot while looking at her.  In her satchel she carried an envelope of her own, waxed against the salt air.  Inside it: a lock of hair tied with ribbon, and nine pages of drawings.  The hair was hers, cut the morning she decided to stop saying what she thought.  The drawings were the reason. 

At twenty-three she had understood that the tide gates could be reversed, that the dead salt flats behind the town could be made to bloom.  She had said it once, at a meeting of the harbor board, and the laughter had been so warm, so fond, that she felt foolish for ever wanting it.  So she enveloped the idea and lived inside the envelope.  That was the thing about secrets, she had learned: they do not sit in your hands, they close around you. 

Tonight she stepped down into the nexus.  The alleys did not stay where she left them.  Doors stood in the walls of other doors; the saltworks chimney rose ahead of her, then behind, then not at all.  It was meant to disorient.  The locksmith said the nexus was the place where every unchosen thing waited, and that a person walking it would be turned around by their own reluctance. 

"This is not a night," he said.  "It is the hush between what is and what might be, and it lasts as long as a thing stays possible."  "You are not lost," he said.  "You are being argued with." 

She asked how anyone found their door.  "The first star you see at twilight," he said.  "Not the brightest but the first, and it marks the bearing of the thing you would do if you were not afraid."  "Walk that line and the doors stop lying." 

She had seen that star ten thousand times and never once followed it.  She had marked it at twilight from the seawall, low over the flats, and now she set her shoulders toward its bearing.  The alleys stopped folding.  At the end of the seventh stood a plain door, salt-scoured, with a lock that took no metal. 

"I made it myself," she said, surprised at her own voice.  "I locked it on a promise." 

The locksmith examined the lock and did not touch it.  "Then the key is the promise, and you have been carrying it in wax paper for thirty-one years." 

She opened the envelope.  The ribbon had gone brittle; the hair still smelled faintly of the soap her mother used.  She laid the lock of hair in the locksmith's palm, because a promise you take back must be given to someone, and he was the only one who had ever waited for her.  He closed his fingers, and the lock opened without a sound. 

Behind the door was not a garden.  It was the hush she had lived in all along, the pause before the universe commits, wide and starlit and unfinished, and in it her idea stood waiting, patient as a heron.  It had not aged.  It wanted the gates turned; it wanted green over the white flats; it wanted, absurdly, to be said out loud in a room full of men who would laugh. 

"Will it work?" she asked.  "I open locks," the locksmith said.  "I have never once been told what is on the other side." 

She thought of the laughter, warm as bathwater, and how it had cost her a life.  Then she said, quietly, the way she said everything, "Tomorrow I will go to the harbor board."  It was an unassumingly bold thing, and the locksmith inclined his head as if she had shouted. 

The nexus began to let go of her, alley by alley, the way a hand opens.  At the seawall she turned to thank him and found only the first star, higher now, and the smell of salt.  In her palm lay a ribbon, still knotted, holding nothing.  She kept it. 

Some evenings, years later, when the flats were green and the board had stopped laughing, she would look for that star and feel the old improbable longing, not for the garden she had made but for the thirty-one years the envelope had been shut.  Then she would go inside and write the next idea down.
