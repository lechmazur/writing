The statue had already told her the secret, and Mara still wanted to break it.  "I opened the gates," it said again, inside her skull, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.  The drowned garden lay around them in the lamp beam: rose trellises furred with silt, beds of grey lavender, a sundial wearing an anemone like a small pink hat.  Mara's grandmother had been born in this city, three years before the water took it.  The family story was a stone of its own: "the gardener flooded us."  She had brought a hammer down forty meters to settle it.  The statue was marble, a woman in a gardener's apron, and she was alive; roots ran through the seams of her like veins through a leaf.  Where the roots entered, the stone moved.  "You have come to sentence me," the statue said.  "That is your right."  "But before you swing, do one small job for me, because my hands cannot hold it anymore."  From a niche in her apron she worked a glass ampoule, no longer than a finger, stained purple as a foxglove bell.  Mara knew the flower; it grew in the harbour pots above, and her grandmother had kept it in a brown bottle by the stove.  "For the heart," her grandmother said.  "A little keeps it beating."  "A little more stops it."  "There is a crack under my collarbone," the statue said.  "Thread it through the split, into the hollow where the roots knot."  "Why me?"  "Because the flaw is the only door I have, and you are the only one here with hands."  Mara put the lamp on the sundial and knelt.  The crack was old, a hairline fault the quarry had never meant, widened by a century of salt.  She turned the ampoule, found the angle, and threaded it in.  The roots took it like a mouth takes a wafer.  "Now listen," said the statue, and the truth came apart in Mara's hands like a knot pulled from the wrong end.  First: the sea wall was already failing, a seam of bad limestone under the east bastion, and the water was coming in whether anyone opened the gates or not.  Second: a breach would have taken the lower streets in ninety seconds, and the lower streets were where the old people slept.  Third: she had opened the gates at the shallow end to let the city fill slowly, ankle, knee, chest, so that everyone had four hours to climb.  Mara's hammer hung at her hip, suddenly a stupid weight.  "Then everyone lived," she said.  "Fourth," said the statue, and here her voice in Mara's head went careful, the way a surgeon's hand goes careful.  The climb was long, and eleven hearts in the lower streets could not manage it.  "I gave them foxglove from this garden, measured in drops."  "Nine lived to the ridge."  "Two I miscounted."  Her grandmother had been carried up those stairs by one of the nine.  The certainty Mara had carried down forty meters cracked, and something moved in the crack.  "You wanted the story simple so the grief would be simple," said the statue, and it was not unkind, only exact.  "You want me to say you were right," Mara said.  "No," said the statue.  "I want you to say I was a gardener who counted drops in the dark, and got two wrong, and would do it again, and would count better."  "That is a harder thing to hold than a murderer," Mara said.  "It is heavier, and it is alive."  Inside the statue, the ampoule broke.  Seeds and one last measure of tincture went into the knot of roots, and the garden began to wake.  Silt lifted off the beds like dust off a table someone has finally decided to use.  Lavender stood up.  The marble woman's fingers closed, once, on nothing, the way a hand tests whether it still can.  "Come back in spring and count with me," she said.  Mara left the hammer on the sundial, where the anemone could consider it.  She went up slowly, letting the light in by degrees, so that nothing in her would break from the change.  Above, in the harbour pots, the foxglove would be ringing purple, medicine and poison on the same stem, and she would tell the family story with the two names in it.
