The first letter arrived in March, three lines in her brother's hand.  "I found the mist."  "I could not pay the toll."  "Do not follow."  She would remember everything afterward as the interval between two letters.  Mira read the warning twice, then folded it into the lining of her veil, where she kept everything she had never said.  She had been a veiled dreamer since childhood: she slept with silk across her mouth so her sleeping words would settle in the cloth instead of escaping.  Nine years of silk, and not one word spent.  Her mother had died with a sentence unfinished, and Mira wanted the library of unspoken words the way other people wanted heaven.  Anselm had gone into the marsh after it and come back only as far as the post office.  The toll was the problem.  She owned nothing rare except a watch bought off a confidence man in the capital.  He had promised it kept the time of whatever you were looking for, and it did not.  Its hands ran clockwise for one minute, then counterclockwise for the next, cyclically reversed, so it never told the hour.  It told only the difference between going and returning.  A swindler's instrument, useless for clocks, perfect for lags.  The mist over the marsh was not water but unspoken language, and language slows whatever crosses it.  Mira went out at dusk with a lantern, the confidence man's watch, and a blank ledger.  She lifted the lantern, and far out in the white a shelf answered, glowing faintly, the way rotten wood glows.  The answer came late.  She wanted to run; she counted instead.  She measured the delay on the reversing hands: four beats out, four beats back, and the reversal told her which was which.  Four beats meant the fourth letter, D, unless the cycle had turned, and then it meant the fourth from the end.  The cipher unfolded as she used it: every letter it gave her became the key to the next, so no one could read faster than they could listen.  She documented each lag in the ledger, and the ledger became the sentence.  The first night gave her four letters: TOLL.  The second night the alphabet ran backward, and the shelves spelled YOURS.  Mira sat down in the reeds and laughed with her hand over her veil, terrified that the sound would spend something.  She had expected a price of gold, or years, or blood, and instead the library wanted deposits.  So she began to document.  She wrote what she had not said at the funeral, when the sentence her mother stopped inside of had stopped inside of Mira too.  She wrote what she had not said to Anselm the morning he left: that she had been jealous of his courage, and that she had let him go alone because of it.  Each page she filled made the mist thinner, and the glow came back sooner.  Four beats became three, then two, and the cipher unfolded faster as the lag collapsed.  On the eleventh night the delay was almost nothing, and the shelves stood close enough to touch, warm as bread, glowing along a hundred spines.  Anselm sat on the threshold stone with a blank ledger on his knees.  He had said everything he ever felt, out loud, immediately, all his life, and so he had nothing left to deposit.  He had been waiting eleven days at a door that would not take him.  Mira kissed his forehead through the silk and wrote the second letter.  It was addressed to their mother, and it said the thing she had swallowed at the funeral: that she had been relieved, and that she was sorry, and that she had loved her anyway.  She did not read it aloud.  She shelved it, and the watch, which had never once told the truth about the hour, swung forward and did not swing back.  Zero delay: she was inside.  Anselm came in behind her on the strength of her silence, already talking.  The library glowed around them, mist still moving between the stacks, every spine a sentence somebody had carried to the grave.  She found her mother's volume with her hands shaking, carefully, the way you lift a sleeping child.  The unfinished sentence was not an accusation, and it was not forgiveness.  It was: I was afraid too.  Mira laughed again, one hand at her veil, and did not spend a word.
