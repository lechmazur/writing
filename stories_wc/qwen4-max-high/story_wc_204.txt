After reality became negotiable, the library stopped lending books and started lending facts.  It floated still above the swirling sea of clouds, a glass orchard where books hung as fruit, and every amendment to the world climbed its roots and ripened there.  Quire kept the letterpress in the graft-house, and he spoke the way he set type: backward, in slugs, so you had to hold his words to a mirror before they meant anything.  When the clerks came down with fresh amendments, he would only say, "mind your p's and q's," and print what they paid for.  A clerk once asked him why he stayed at the press, and he answered, "a page is a room you can carry," which the clerk logged as nonsense. 

The Concordance upstairs was perfect: every fact consented to by every party, cross-checked, sealed, without a single loose thread.  Perfection was the point; a negotiable world needs one book that nobody is arguing with.  But each amendment rustled the orchard.  The glass leaves knocked together, and in the hammocks between the trees the long readers woke, revised.  Rustling awakenings, the clerks called them, and logged the changes, and went back up. 

Nell had signed the Long Read eleven years ago, and she had woken forty-one times since, and forty-one women had opened her eyes.  Two of them had known him.  None of them had been the wife who, on the last night before the Accord, whispered something into a bottle and stoppered it and handed it to him.  He had never opened it.  A whisper spent is a whisper gone, and it was the only thing left in the world that no one had agreed to. 

Quire wanted one moment.  Not a longer life, which could be renegotiated overnight, but one instant with everything in it: all forty-one Nells, the orchard, the whole amendable eternity, pressed flat into a page that could not be revised because nothing was left outside it.  To set that page he needed a word nobody had written. 

So each dawn he went out along the glass branches with a lamp and read the frost.  Frost was the last unauthored script; the cloud sea breathed it onto the panes at night, ferns and feathers, no party's hand, no consent, nothing to bargain with.  For eleven years it had said nothing.  That morning, on the pane above Nell's hammock, the frost had grown letters, mirrored, the way type must be.  Three words.  He copied the pattern into his composing stick, letter by letter, and the frost melted behind his lamp as if it had only been waiting to be read. 

Down in the graft-house he locked the forme, and then he did the thing he had saved for eleven years: he unstoppered the bottle over the press bed.  The whisper came out thin and cold and lay on the ink like breath on glass, and it was the sentence the frost had spelled.  He laid the master sheet down and pulled the bar, and the whisper went into the paper the way breath goes into a cold pane.  Then he carried the wet sheet up and bound it into the Concordance, the one book nobody was arguing with. 

Three words that no party had consented to, sitting inside a perfect system like a stone in a clock.  The Concordance tried to reconcile them and could not, because a whisper cannot be renegotiated; there is no one left to bargain with.  Every fact in the book leaned on the whisper, and the whisper would not move, and the leaning became a strain, and the strain became a rupture. 

It went through the orchard at the speed of sound in glass.  The fruit burst; eleven years of amendments came off the branches all at once; the cloud floor tore open and the sea poured up through the gap.  The leaves did not rustle.  They rang, once, all together, and in that single sound every waking that had ever been divided among forty-one mornings arrived at the same instant.  Nell sat up in her hammock, all of her at once, every version folded into one woman, and she looked at him and said the three words the frost had spelled: "I was here." 

It was not a long moment.  The library was falling, the Concordance was ash of glass, and nothing in the world was agreed anymore.  But the moment had everything in it, and so there was nothing outside it to revise it with, and it stayed.  Quire, who had never explained himself to anyone, held his empty bottle and did not explain himself now.  Eternity had been negotiable; this was not.
