At the Reach, twilight lasted eleven hours, and the iron sand stood up in small black hairs whenever the field breathed.  Three magnetic lines braided above that nexus, and no compass could tell you which way was out.  That was why they sent exiles here.  Odran had taught chess to the heir of Aum for nineteen years, recording not the moves his students made but the length of their thinking between them.  He believed a lost position could still hold one beautiful move, and that finding it was the whole of an education.  The keepers of the covenant called that heresy, loaded him onto a cart, and gave him to the Reach as the city's difficult gift.  He had kept one thing: a polished basalt worry stone, dented where his thumb had pressed it through ten thousand lessons.  Basalt remembers the field it cooled in, so here the stone swung in his palm like a slow needle, its dent always turning toward the braid.  In the ninth year of his exile, the sand began to lie flat.  Ships out of Aum missed the channel and broke on the shoals, and the aurora thinned to a smear.  A bird came at dusk with a ledger strapped to its leg, copied in the hand of the woman he had taught to lose well, now Archon of Aum.  The covenant had been sworn under an eclipse, and the field could not hear words, only intervals, so its terms were written as rests, measured silences between drumbeats.  One clause had fallen into shadow, and the keepers called the whole thing the eclipsed covenant and shrugged and went on taking gifts.  Her note said: the next phase begins at moonrise; tell me what we owe.  He had four hours.  Odran laid the ledger on flat rock and set the stone at its corner.  He knew this notation; he had invented it in a cold schoolroom for a child who thought too long and hated herself for it.  He translated silence as he had taught her to, not as absence but as the other side's move, and the rests became a game he could read.  The stone gave him the beat.  Each time the dent swung toward the braid he marked a pulse, and he correlated the written pauses against those pulses until the shadowed clause rose out of the dark like a piece developed late and perfectly.  The gift must be given, not taken.  Seven words, and nine years of a man's life inside them.  They had not given him; they had thrown him away, and the field knew the difference and had been waiting, politely, for someone to ask.  He laughed once, alone, in the eleven-hour dusk, because it was a beautiful move in a lost position.  Then he wrote on the ledger's last page, thoughtfully precise, the way he had written comments on a thousand bad games: ask me.  He tied the page to the bird's leg and opened his hand.  The bird went out over the shoals by the braid, as birds do, needing no compass.  Somewhere beyond the horizon a woman he had taught to lose well would read seven words and understand that she must give her teacher away on purpose, and that he must be asked.  It would cost her more than it cost him.  He already lived here.  The answer came before moonrise, as a drum across the water: one beat, then a measured pause, the question put properly in the old notation.  He answered in the only language the covenant had, sitting down with the stone in his palm and letting a silence of the correct length go by.  The dent fit his thumb.  Under him the iron sand rose, hair by hair, and stood.  The braid overhead thickened into three green roads, and far off he heard a ship's bell find the channel.  He would never see Aum again; a gift stays given.  But there were keepers' children at the Reach who had never been shown that a losing game can be played beautifully, and he had a stone, a notation, and eleven hours of light.  Tomorrow he would begin with the endgame, which is where the difficulty is, and where the beauty hides.
