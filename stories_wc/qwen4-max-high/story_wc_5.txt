For eleven days the ash had covered the sky, and the world held its breath.  In the withered orchard deep in the subarctic crater, a thousand feet below the rim, Ilse knelt in the snow and did not move.  The vent's warmth had failed in the spring, the trees had died standing, and then the ash came.  She had been a flash-paper alchemist, which meant she had spent her life teaching light to leave quickly.  Theaters bought her sheets for their ghosts; the survey corps bought them for signal flares.  Now she worked the reverse art: forty sheets of safekept starlight, folded in a tin against her ribs.  Enough light to read a map by, if there had been a map.  Her husband Tomas had gone out over the rim on the first dark morning to find whether the north pass was still open.  He carried a pulse key, as all the surveyors did, tapping the wire in time with his heart so the station would know he lived.  The smoked drum had drawn his heartbeat for nine miles, then stopped.  Compasses lied in all that iron rock, and no one would walk blind into the crater's throat.  So Ilse had set herself the only work she knew: to translate heartbeats into maps.  A beat was a pace; a long gap was a climb, a rest, a place where a man leaned on a rock and breathed.  She needed the ground beneath her hands to reason against, so she built the crater out of what the orchard had thrown away.  Bottle glass for the ice fields, tin lids for the ponds, wire for the trail, pruned apple wood laid in ridges, and the shriveled fallen fruit for the boulder fields.  Discarded things, arranged into a pattern that told the truth.  She had been kneeling six hours; the snow had melted and refrozen around her knees, and she let it hold her there.  Patience, in her, was not a mood but a posture.  She laid one tin lid, waited for her fingers to stop shaking, and laid the next.  Then she read the drum against the model, beat by beat, and found where Tomas had stopped: below the north pass, on the scree shelf, where the wire ran out.  Now she needed something to carry the map in the dark.  In the house, in a drawer, lay a copper storybook illustration plate her mother had engraved: the orchard in bloom, forty rows, every leaf cut by hand.  It was the only place the trees still lived.  Ilse carried it out, set it on her knees, and scraped the blossoms away.  She cut contour lines where the branches had been, and the scree shelf where her mother had cut a child reaching for an apple.  She did not hurry, and she did not let herself weep onto the copper, because water pits the metal.  Then the alchemy: flash-paper pulp, soaked in the safekept starlight until the fibers held it the way a wick holds flame.  She filled the grooves with it, grain by grain.  One tremor, one warm breath, and the whole plate would flash white and leave nothing: no ash, no map, no Tomas.  So she stopped breathing in the ordinary way.  She let her breath out thin across the snow, held her wrists against her thighs, and counted her own heart to keep her hands from hurrying it.  Frost grew on her lashes; the orchard did not stir; somewhere above, the ash waited to decide whether to fall or lift.  At the four hundredth beat the last groove took, and the plate began to glow like a window seen from a road at night.  Ilse sealed it under glass and wax, so the light would keep for the walking.  The searchers came at a dawn that was not dawn, and she put the map into their hands, and told them the shelf, and the count of paces.  They went out into the dark with the plate glowing between them, and she stayed kneeling among her tin lids and glass.  She could not stand; the snow had made a cast of her.  She waited, breathing at last, easy and slow, while the world went on holding its breath with her.  Near midnight a small white flash answered from the north pass, quick as her old paper, gone before it was fairly seen.  A signal sheet, her own recipe, burned by a living hand.  Ilse laid her palm flat on the cold model of the crater, on the little shelf of apple wood, and let the orchard exhale around her.
