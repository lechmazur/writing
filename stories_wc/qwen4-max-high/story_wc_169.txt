The grotto had been polished by ten thousand tides until its walls held light like standing water.  Mira launched her trial balloons from that smooth throat of stone, one each dawn, while the village above her slept out its long disappointment.  The fish had gone, the wells had turned salt, and the young had taken the road inland.  Her father had been a balloonist; his altitude gauge still hung from her wrist on a braided cord, its needle trembling over a dial of brass clouds.  He had used it to know how high he stood above the sea.  She used it to know how far she stood from the other worlds.  Pressure fell in the grotto whenever the parallel timelines drifted apart, and the needle climbed as if she were rising.  Mira was a logical dreamer: she wanted a livable future badly enough to write it down in columns.  Her notebooks were full of attempts to puzzle out new equations for hope.  The village called her the balloon woman and left paper scraps at the grotto mouth, indulgent and unconvinced.  Each balloon announced one small change she had made in the village, and each change wore a decoration.  They were trial balloons in both senses: instruments, and proposals.  A blue spiral of paper ribbons meant a mended net; a chain of red paper fish meant a shared well; seven green lanterns meant a school kept open through winter.  She was creating patterns with decorations because patterns were the only language that survived the crossing.  Facts changed from world to world; the shapes people loved did not.  In the tide-polished walls she could read the reflections of a hundred other grottos, a hundred other dawns.  She stayed lucidly watchful, noting which garlands hung in which world, while the sea breathed in and out beneath her boots.  On the fortieth dawn the needle swung hard toward the dial's high clouds.  The grotto did not shake; it simply began to empty of versions.  Reflection by reflection the walls went dark, and in each darkening she saw the village above her gone: roofs fallen, the road inland choked with dust, the school's green lanterns unlit.  Nothing screamed; the world simply became less plural, the way a chord becomes a single note.  Water rose around her ankles, warm and unhurried, and she kept writing.  Panic would have cost her a column.  By the time the tide touched her knees, only nine reflections remained, and the gauge hovered at a height her father had never come down from.  In all nine, one pattern held.  Not the ribbon spiral, not the green lanterns.  A chain of red paper fish, repeated hand to hand, hung in every surviving world, and in each of them it had been made by many people, not by one.  Mira understood then that she had been solving for the wrong variable.  Hope was not the size of the change but the number of hands that repeated it.  One woman launching balloons was a single, fragile decoration; a village stringing garlands was a pattern the timelines could not lose.  She wrote the equation in the wet margin: hope equals repetition times the number of willing hands, and it had no upper bound.  She climbed out of the grotto with the gauge quiet against her wrist and the water falling behind her, and she went door to door.  She did not preach; she asked for paper, string, and scissors, and she taught the old festival pattern to anyone who would sit down.  The widow made fish; the boys made fish; the man who had stopped mending nets made forty and hung them along the well.  By evening the pattern ran from the harbor to the schoolhouse, and the village began to revitalize itself the way a tide returns: without drama, without permission.  The net-mender mended, the well was shared, the school stayed open, not because a balloon had told them to, but because their own hands had already agreed.  Mira went back at dawn and found the walls full again, a hundred grottos, a hundred dawns, red fish swimming in all of them.  The needle rested low, near the sea, where her father would have wanted it.  She launched one more trial balloon, unmarked and plain, just to see.  Then she sat lucidly watchful in the polished light, doing the arithmetic of a place that had decided to stay.
