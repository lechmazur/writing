The shop ticked from every wall, and none of the clocks agreed.  Chaplain Amos Teague stood inside the door with rain running off his coat, holding a shoebox against his chest like something rescued from a fire.  Nadia Ferrante did not look up from the music box on her bench.  Nineteen years at Rockview Penitentiary had taught him how to enter a room where he was not wanted.  You waited, you kept your hands visible, you let the silence belong to the other person.  Her brother had taught him that, indirectly, across eleven years of Thursday visits.  "Dov made this," he said, and set the shoebox on the counter.  Inside, in a nest of cotton, lay a brass escapement shaped like a heart, no bigger than a plum.  A clockwork heart, Dov called it: a governor he had cut and filed in the prison hobby shop for a music box older than the state itself.  Nadia's hands stopped.  Outside the storm leaned on the street, and inside the shop held its bright, breathless hush, gilt and rosewood glowing, forty escapements whispering under the thunder.  "He wrote that you told the board he had no remorse."  Teague had rehearsed an explanation for nine days and could not find its first word.  What he had written was true: the man sat dry-eyed through his hearing and said he was sorry to the family once, flatly, like a fact.  The board denied parole, and two more years opened in front of Dov like a hallway.  "I told him I was sorry," Teague said.  "When?"  "In the corridor, after."  She wiped the screwdriver on her apron and set it down parallel to the bench edge, aptly deliberate, the way her brother used to lay his files in a row.  "Sorry is a sound," she said.  "He wanted you to fix the letter."  "I came for a second opinion," Teague said, and heard how ridiculous it sounded, a man asking a stranger to overrule his own verdict on himself.  He wanted her to say he was not the small, careful man he had been in that room.  She lifted the heart, turned it once between finger and thumb, and set it on the bench between them.  "You don't get an opinion," she said.  "You get work."  She found the old cylinder box in a drawer and a key that fit the heart's arbor.  "Wind it slow," she said.  "Listen for the limp."  He wound, and the heart began to beat, uneven, insistent, a small brass animal refusing to be quiet.  The other clocks went on around it, and for a while the two of them simply listened, the way you listen at a door you are afraid to open.  The rain slackened.  He thought of Dov's hands shaking so badly he had to sit on them, and of how he had written that stillness down as coldness.  "The nerve damage," Nadia said, not unkindly.  "He wrote you about it twice."  "That was your second opinion, and it came with a stamp on it."  Then the light came through the transom the way light breaks through storm clouds, one long bar of it, gold and sudden, laying itself across the bench.  In that bar he saw the seam.  Dov had cracked the mainspring bridge, soldered it, filed it smooth, and left the line visible where the two pieces met.  It kept time anyway.  The limp was the seam.  Something in Teague went quiet and then rearranged itself, the way a room does when you finally see where the door has been all along.  "I keep thinking about what I deserve," he said.  "Don't," she said.  "Deserve is for endings."  He took his copy of the recommendation from his coat, nine days folded in the pocket, and turned it to the blank side.  He wrote that he had mistaken a damaged body for an unrepentant soul, that his recommendation was wrong, that he would say so under oath on Monday, and that the chaplaincy could do what it liked about that.  Nadia read it, folded it once, and handed him an envelope with the shop's address printed on it, so the reply would come here, where she could make him keep his word.  "Bring the heart back when it plays clean," she said.  "Saturdays," he said.  "Saturdays," she agreed, and the clocks went on disagreeing, and the brass heart kept its imperfect, stubborn time until the spring ran down.
