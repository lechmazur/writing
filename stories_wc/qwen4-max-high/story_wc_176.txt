Forgiveness came first, and then the thaw.  I had spent eleven winters under the aurora punishing myself in the language of my trade: cold molds, cracked wax, bells that would not ring.  The morning I forgave myself, meltwater began ticking off the sanctum's ribs of ice, and I wanted, suddenly and badly, to work.  I am a lost wax caster.  My whole craft is a courtesy to absence: you shape the wax, bury it in clay, burn it out, and pour bronze into the hole it leaves.  In the icebound sanctum of Sarn, absence is the archive.  Here the Naming Rope hangs, nine hundred years braided, flax and horsehair and threads of glass drawn from meltwater under this same green sky.  Every knot is a syllable of a name; every crossing carries the aurora's light a little farther down the cord and into the bells' hammer rack.  All winter the hammers sit frozen in their rack; only light running the glass threads can loose them.  Braid it true and the sanctum illuminates, ice going blue-green from floor to vault, and the tongues speak the drowned of Sarn in the order they were loved.  At nineteen I was strong and quick and not yet gentle.  My tongs slipped, the crucible went over, and I snatched the ceremonial rope to sling it clear of my foot.  Bronze ate forty names out of the braid and fouled the forty tongues beneath it.  The rope burned dark there, and for eleven years no one could say those forty aloud, because in Sarn a name is not a word but a sound.  I kept what could be kept: a scrap of punched card from the street organ my mother cranked on the quay, eight bars of the drowned, her own name punched in later by me.  Cardboard gone soft as cloth, a torn corner, the holes still true.  It is a transcribed memory, and I read it the way I read everything, backwards, by its voids.  This spring I pressed the scrap into a slab of wax and let the holes tell me lengths.  Then I burned the wax away and poured bronze into its absence, forty tongues, each one the shape of a hole where a name used to be.  I tuned them on the whetstone with the card held up to the aurora, matching pitch to hole until the metal said what the cardboard remembered.  Gentleness, I have learned, is not a feeling; it is a technique.  I am technically gentle now: two fingers, a breath held low, the pressure of a moth on glass.  You cannot bully a nine-hundred-year-old cord.  I hung the tongues in the stripped rack, then opened the burned place and began to reconfigure the braids.  Nine strands, and the card's holes for a pattern: over, under, cross, hold.  Where the old flax crumbled I spliced new glass thread from the meltwater barrel, and each splice I laid in with the patience of pouring.  Water ran everywhere, off the vault, down my wrists, a hurry in it, an ardor, the whole sanctum melting toward the sound.  Aurora light came into the top of the rope like heat into a mold.  It crept down through my crossings, stopped where I had not yet finished, waited.  I did not apologize to the rope; I had already done that for eleven years, and apology is a way of holding on.  Forgiveness had given me back my hands.  I only worked, gentle and exact, forty crossings, forty small permissions.  The last knot closed.  Light fell the whole length of the cord and the sanctum illuminated, ice lit from inside like a lamp of green water, and the hammers came down.  Forty bronze tongues said forty names in the order they were loved.  I heard my mother's among them, cranked out of torn cardboard, out of wax that no longer exists, out of a hole I made and then filled.  The ice wept it back at me, drop for drop, and I stood in the ringing with my hands open, which is the only way to hold anything I cast.  Tomorrow I will braid in this year's drowned, and my own name after them, so that someone gentle can ring me when I am only a hole in the record.  The water is running, the sky is green, the wax is gone, and the bronze is speaking.
