The ridge bloomed once in a lifetime, and Sevi had nine days to check whether the island's memory was true.  After that, the monsoon.  Rain would strip the white flowers off the ancient basalt, and the pollen that carried the island's shared remembering would go into the sea with everything else.  Between blooms the island forgot whatever it had not planted, which was why the office existed at all. 

Sevi was the island's reality checker, licensed third class, and her instruments were a brass tally wheel, a spoon, and a jar of moths.  Her mother had called the method empirically whimsical: count everything, and count the ridiculous things too.  So Sevi slept in the bloom huts among the dreamers, where the air tasted of burnt sugar, and gathered at dawn what the pollen had put into them. 

Nine ships, said forty dreamers, sails white as the ridge.  Nine ships, and ancestors stepping ashore with harps and seed grain, founding us in glory.  Old Teu, who had dreamed the harps most loudly, asked her whether the fleet would be kept.  She said she kept what she could measure, and he spat, politely, into the flowers. 

Then she went to measure the harbor.  She rowed it at low tide, counting oar strokes, and the spoon held seawater she tasted for silt.  Nine ships could not have turned in that water.  One leaking boat could have.  She released the moths above the bloom, because moths fly toward the thickest scent, and the thickest scent is the oldest memory.  They went not to the harbor but to the north scar, where nothing had grown for two hundred years.  Under the ash there lay a stump, wide as a table, of the knowledge grove the songs did not mention. 

In the ledger office, wrapped in her mother's certificate, Sevi kept the last knowledge fruit seed.  A seed like that will not sprout in soil.  It must be wakened by an interpreted dream spoken over it, and once wakened it grows into whatever the island will remember forever.  That was the whole purpose of her office: not to invent a past, but to hand the seed a true dream. 

Her mother's certificate verified nine ships.  Sevi read the signature forty times, as if repetition were an instrument.  Perhaps her mother had lied to keep the island proud.  Perhaps the pollen had drifted, the way pollen does, carrying glory because glory travels better than hunger.  She could not prove either, and the not knowing stood in her bright as the ridge at noon.  She loved her mother, and love was not an instrument either. 

On the seventh day she climbed to the north scar with the seed and a lamp.  Below her the clouds were stacking in the south, purple and patient, two days early.  She had one dream of her own to offer, and she had checked it as she checked everything.  In it, one boat came in low and leaking, and hungry people burned the grove to cook what they had salvaged, and a girl took one seed out of the fire.  The moths had gone to the scar.  The harbor was too small.  Her mother's handwriting slanted the same way in every year, honest or not.  That was all the evidence there would ever be. 

So she interpreted the dream aloud in the ledger's grammar, where a boat means a beginning and a fire means a debt.  She spoke the nine ships also, and the doubt between them, because the doubt was measured too, and she would not amputate what she could not resolve.  To protect the island's memory, she had come to believe, was to protect its right to keep asking. 

The seed split in her palm with a small dry sound, like a page turning.  She pressed it into the ash and counted her heartbeats, which was whimsical, and wrote the number down, which was not.  Two leaves came up unequal, and she did not pinch either one off.  The first rain reached the ridge at dusk, and the blossoms began to fall toward the sea, and the seed held.  In the ledger she wrote: unverified, kept.  Then she went down to teach the children the spoon, the moths, and the wheel.
