The fort on the storm-tossed ridge took weather the way a tooth takes cold.  I had climbed three days to reach it, carrying a surveyor's chain, a plumb bob, and a commission nobody in the capital believed in anymore.  They had sent me to map the night.  Not the land under it; the night itself, which had begun, eleven months earlier, to take the stars away.  One by one, from the edges inward, like a marble floor losing its veins.  My sister called me mildly obsessed, and she was right in the mild way: I counted the losses each dusk, sharpened my pencils twice, and could not stop. 

The garrison kept the pebble-shore oracle in the granary, under guard, because a general had once hoped she would predict his battles.  She had told him nothing, and he had left her here anyway, the way you leave a tool you cannot use but hate to throw away.  She was small and salt-worn, and she asked me what I wanted before I asked her anything. 

"A map of an endless night," I said.  "For the roads, for the ships, for whoever is left walking when the last one goes." 

She nodded as if I had reported the weather.  Then she took out the booklet.  It had been gilt once, gold leaf laid over every page, and the gold was flaking now, coming away in scabs at the corners, leaving the paper beneath it pale and scarred.  The words had gone with the gold. 

"Forty years of the shore," she said.  "Every hush I ever kept." 

I turned the pages and found them blank, and I said so, rudely, because I had climbed three days. 

"They are not blank," she said.  "They are summarized." 

She explained it once, plainly, the way a mason explains a joint.  A hush is not emptiness; it is amber.  It closes over whatever has stopped, sound or light, and holds it entire, the way resin holds a wasp.  On the shore she had learned to listen to that ambered hush between waves and say what was inside it: the boat that had passed, the girl who had stopped singing, the bell buoy torn loose in the night.  She did not invent; she retold, by summarizing silence, and the fishermen had trusted her more than they trusted the sky. 

I asked her to do it here, where there was no sea.  She said the ridge was only a slower shore, and that the storm broke on the fort the same way. 

We went up to the rampart between gusts, into the marbled hush, gray silence veined with the white of failing light.  She stood with her eyes shut and told me what the quiet was holding: a horse dying in the lower stable, a man weeping without sound in the gatehouse, the wind's shape around the broken merlon.  I drew it.  Not the walls, not the stars: the boundaries of the hushes, where each silence began and where it gave way to the next.  It came out looking like a coastline.  It came out looking like something you could steer by. 

We worked eleven nights, and the sky kept thinning, and when my paper ran out I drew on her blank pages, over the scars where the gold had been.  On the last night there was one star left, low over the western notch, and the whole garrison came up to watch it, quietly, as if watching would help.  I had my chain and my plumb bob and no use for either.  The oracle opened the booklet to its last gilt page and let the gold go; the flakes lifted off and went out over the wall like a small, useless currency. 

"Now," she said, and the star went out inside the hush, and she told me where it had been.  She summarized the silence it left, and I marked the place, and that was the last fixed point on the map.  Then the dark was complete, and the fort was a ridge of listening men. 

She died before the thaw, and I kept the booklet, its once-blank pages a coastline now, and I read it nightly, mildly obsessed, learning to retell what the dark holds.  They copied my chart onto sailcloth, and in the spring the ships came up the estuary by it, sounding their way from hush to hush, and none of them struck the bar.  The night is endless, and it has a coastline, and I am drawing it.
