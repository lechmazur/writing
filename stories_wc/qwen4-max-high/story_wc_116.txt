The library had no roof left, only roots, and the roots had learned to hold the ceiling up.  Sela was its chronicle keeper, and the overgrown library lay hidden in the catacombs of lost knowledge, where the shelves had gone soft with moss.  She wrote what the river brought her.  A drowned cart.  A wedding ring full of silt.  Ash from orchards burning upstream, which she recorded as ash, and not as cruelty. 

That was the whole of her work: to witness change without judgment.  Judgment was noise, and noise did not compost.  Plain witness fed the silence, and the silence grew back over the fallen plaster, keeping the peace soft.  It was a crumbling peace, and she loved it that way. 

Then the river stopped sending anything.  Seasons drifted past untold, uncounted, unnamed; the roots flowered and rotted and flowered, and no cart, no ring, no ash came down.  Whatever had happened upstream was not hers to name.  Sela wrote the empty water nineteen times, then stopped.  With nothing new to witness, the silence thinned.  It went brittle, like a wasp nest, and where it cracked she could hear her own thoughts, which was the worst sound she knew.  If it went entirely, the shelves would go to powder, and she would go hard, and stay. 

So she took out the swan.  It was old origami, paper gone furry at the creases, folded and refolded by every keeper before her, and its folds were instructions.  She opened it flat and read the cryptic geometry: a valley fold for the flooded stair, a reverse fold for the shaft where the roots grew thick as a wrist.  At the center of the paper, where the body had been, a small square of creases meant the obsidian refuge along the river. 

She had never seen it.  She was not certain she had not built it.  Sela gripped reality loosely; it was the qualification for the job, and the price of it.  Names slid off her, including her own, and some seasons she was a woman and some seasons she was a sentence someone had once written. 

She burrowed.  Through the flooded stair, through the root shaft, on her belly in wet silt with the swan held in her teeth, she burrowed the way water burrows, patiently and without opinions.  The catacombs let her pass.  Above her the world held its breath, and holding is not silence. 

Then the shaft opened, and there was the river, wide and brown and new.  It had moved.  The obsidian refuge stood where the swan promised, black glass walls, a doorway shaped like a cupped hand, but the river now ran through it.  Silt filled the room to the height of her knee.  A heron had nested in the window notch, and the black walls held the shape of every flood that had come and gone. 

Something in Sela wanted to write loss.  Wanted to write ruined, wanted to write they should have moved it, wanted to write the river was wrong.  She stood in the cold water with her hand on the glass and let the wanting pass through her, the way silt passes through a flood. 

Then she wrote what was.  The river moved.  The refuge holds water now.  A heron lives in the window, and the walls remember floods, and the door is still open. 

Around her the silence began to grow back.  It came off the wet obsidian like breath off a mirror, thick and cool, seeding itself in the silt, in the heron's feathers, in the hollow of her wrist.  This was what the refuge was for: not to keep her safe, but to change where she could see it.  She carried the silence home in her cupped hands, and it did not spill. 

She burrowed back.  In the library the brittle places softened under her breathing, and moss crept over the crack where she had heard her own thoughts.  Before she slept she refolded the swan, and added one crease of her own: a small mark for the heron, so the next keeper would know the doorway was occupied. 

She did not write that it was better this way.  She did not write that it was worse.  She wrote the date as she always did, which was: still drifting.  Outside, the roots held the ceiling, and let a little more of it fall.
