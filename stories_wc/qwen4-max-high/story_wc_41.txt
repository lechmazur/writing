My mother read the future in other people's garbage, and the town called her the junkyard prophet, and she never once corrected them.  Her gift ran backward.  She did not see what was coming; she forgot it, days ahead, and the shape of the hole told her what to expect.  People brought her their broken toasters and she held them until something went missing.  When she lost the word for kettle, the kettle broke.  When she lost my father's face, we buried him that Friday.  I was eleven, and I decided that if forgetting was the weather, I would become a machine that kept things.  Those were the years of the unwatched videotapes.  My father filmed birthdays, tide pools, my mother sorting copper wire, and then the tapes went into a shoebox under the stairs, and the house held its breath around them.  Nobody pressed play; we were afraid of what we would have already lost.  I bought a recorder instead, and I went after the sound of growing older.  Not words: the grain under words, the way my mother's voice had begun to sand itself down at the edges, the half second she needed now before she said my name.  Behind the yard, past the crushed cars, lay the fog sculpture garden.  A man from the city had bolted iron armatures to concrete pads out there, frames and ribs and open hands, and left them for the sea fog to fill.  On clear days they were nothing, scaffolding for absence.  In fog they became a woman, a heron, a wave curling, all of it gray and breathing and gone by noon.  My mother loved that garden because it worked the way she did, by losing its outline on schedule.  She kept a piece of plain chalk in her coat, the only thing in the yard she refused to read.  Everything else meant something; chalk was just chalk, and it washed off in the wet air, and she liked a mark that could not outlive her.  With it she numbered the concrete pads, and between the pads she laid driftwood, long sticks pointing the way to the next, so a person could cross the garden blind.  She could not trust her memory to hold a route, so she built the route outside her body, in wood.  In October she stopped knowing the word for daughter.  She said it went like a coin down a grate, and then she looked at me with the polite curiosity of a woman at a bus stop.  I had my recorder running before she finished the sentence.  That night I understood the prophecy: she would not lose me, she would lose knowing me, and I did what I always did, I clung to the echo.  I played her voice back until the tape of my own patience wore thin.  The next morning the fog came in thick, and she walked out past the crushed cars, and I went after her with the recorder and her chalk.  The garden had swallowed itself.  I could not see the pads, so I did what she taught me: I knelt, and I laid driftwood as I went, stick after stick, each one aimed back at the last, a chain of arrows leading home.  At every pad I found, I wrote the next number in chalk on the wet concrete, and the numbers were already softening as I wrote them.  I located her at the seventh pad, inside the iron woman, where the fog had made a body around the frame and my mother had stepped into it to be filled too.  She was breathing the way the tide breathes at the turn, in, and holding, and letting go.  "I know I am forgetting something," she said, "and it is enormous, and it is kind."  I lifted the recorder and then I lowered it.  The sound of growing older was not in her voice; it was in the hush around it, the same hush that sat over the shoebox under the stairs, all those years of tapes we never watched because we wanted the voices to stay unspent.  I pressed record on nothing, and let it run.  We walked out along my driftwood, and the fog came apart behind us, and the chalk went back to being weather.  She asked who I was, and I said nobody you have to remember, and she took my arm anyway, the way you take a rope in the dark.  Later I would play that nothing back and hear her breathing inside it, and want her the way the shore wants the water: not to keep it, only to be reached again.
