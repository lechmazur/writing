After the resuscitation, light stopped leaving things.  A cup set down kept burning its shape into the air for minutes, a green ghost of a cup, and my ribs ached where the lifeguard had cracked them pressing me back into the world.  The doctors called it lingering luminance and wrote it down as a symptom.  Photographs disagreed.  In every picture of me taken after that afternoon, something tall stood at my shoulder, and the emulsion around it bubbled like burnt sugar.  My mother stopped photographing me.  The tall thing talked.  It had come up out of the water with me, and it whispered advice: which stairs to avoid, which friend was lying, when to hold my breath.  Its voice was warm honey poured over something dead, and I hated how I leaned toward it.  It was the only one who had been where I had been.  It said: never look behind you.  It said: never look at the glow, only at the thing that made it.  So I understood that the glow was its footprint, and that my fear was the floor it stood on.  My grandmother's opera cloak came to me that winter, moth-eaten velvet, with a clasp of polished silver shaped like a moth's spread wings.  The clasp was convex, a small curved mirror, and in it the whole room bent and watched.  She had drowned in 1961 and been brought back, too.  Nobody in the family had a photograph of her after that.  In the cloak pocket I found her dream dictionary, a cheap paperback swollen with damp, its margins crowded with her handwriting.  Under MIRROR she had written: a door that opens only when shut.  Under DROWNING: the water is dry, and I am the one who is wet with air.  Paradoxes, dozens of them, and beneath the last one: it cannot hold two truths at once; make it choose, and it must speak with its own mouth.  She had left me a weapon and no instructions, which is a grandmother's way.  I wrote my own notes for a week, sharpening them.  Under ADVISOR: the friend who loves me is the stranger who never leaves.  Then I went to the funhouse at the abandoned carnival out past the reservoir, where the advisor had been telling me, nightly, not to go.  Rot and candy floss, wet plaster clowns, a corridor of mirrors silvered with mildew.  It smelled like the inside of a mouth that had eaten something sweet.  I pinned the clasp at my throat, tilted my chin, and there it was in that little curved silver: tall, patient, wearing my lifeguard's face like a coat it had not buttoned.  Beautiful, and I wanted to be sick.  It whispered that we should go home, that I was tired, that it forgave me.  I had not come to be brave; I had come to trick my fear into showing where it was thin.  Forgave me for what, I asked, and then I unbosomed myself before it could answer.  I told the mirrors that I had not fought down there.  I told them I had let the water in on purpose, because the quiet was kind, and that every day since I had missed it the way you miss a lover who was cruel to you.  Then I read her dictionary against it, my own note in the margin: the friend who loves me is the stranger who never leaves.  Fear eats what you swallow; I had handed it my whole secret and it had nothing left to chew.  It chose, and its own mouth said: I am the stranger, and a stranger cannot stay where he is looked at without fear.  So I looked.  In a hundred mildewed mirrors I turned and looked at the glow instead of the thing, at the lingering light it left in me, and I did not flinch, and I did not stop wanting it.  It thinned like sugar in hot water, still whispering, still kind, and the afterimages went out one by one, the mirrors going dark behind me.  In the booth outside, the old automatic camera still took quarters.  The photograph showed one woman in a moth-eaten cloak, and nothing at her shoulder, and no burn on the film.  I keep it in my wallet, and some nights I miss the voice so badly I hold the clasp up and whisper to it myself.
