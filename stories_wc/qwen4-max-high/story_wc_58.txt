The last ship of the season carried the palace carpet away, and the harbor chain rose behind it, and I stood on the wrong side of the water.  I write obituaries for the living, a mainland trade; people pay to read their endings while they can still correct them.  Stranded until spring, I climbed to the palace to find whoever had money and a reason. 

Only Marit was left, sixty years a carpet mistress, kept on to sweep the rooms her work had emptied.  Above the garden the state rooms stood open to the weather, chandeliers sold, gilt frames holding nothing but the smell of old smoke.  Her garden lay under the north wall in permanent shadow, and that October it was magnificent: quinces rotting gold on the bricks, maple leaves lacquered crimson, a drained pool holding one black coin of rain.  Every empty place carried a stake with writing on it.  "Fig, cut in the siege winter for the bread ovens."  "Pool, drained the summer the governor's son drowned in it."  "Loom house, burned for warmth in the fever spring." 

"An absence you can explain is a room you can walk through," she said.  "The other kind is a wall." 

I had come north for exactly that.  Four hundred obituaries behind me, and some sitters rose from the reading whole while others never rose, and I wanted the geography of healing: its passes, its distances, which griefs a person can cross on foot and which demand a winter.  My own brother vanished at sea when I was nine, and no one ever explained the hole, so I have been labeling other people's ever since. 

Marit commissioned hers before the snow, on one condition: every absence in it explained.  I never work from records; I am hired for description, because the gone are only real to us in details.  I seat the sitter, learn them by heart, then send them out of the garden and write the shape of the hole, projecting their presence into it until the vacancy stands up and answers.  So I learned her: warp-knotted hands, lanolin and cold tea, a habit of testing the air with her thumb as if the whole world were wool to be graded. 

When I asked what she wanted said, she drew a single thread from her sleeve, brown, coarse, a hand's length.  Weft, from the great carpet: the pattern had gone aboard the ship, and this had stayed.  "Nobody looks at the weft," she said.  "It runs across under the knots and holds everything, and no one has ever thanked it."  "Say I was that." 

She wound it three times around my wrist and tied it with her thumbs pressed to my pulse, counting the beats the way she must once have counted picks to the inch, her breath quince-sweet and close.  Then she went out through the gate as the work requires, and I sat alone in the shadow with the wet gold smell and wrote her into the empty chair. 

Halfway down the page I understood the stakes.  She was doing what I do: naming each hole precisely so the fig, the boy, the looms, the carpet could go on standing in it, explained, unbearable and bearable at once.  The map I had traveled for was not a country at all.  Healing runs under the picture like weft, over and under every knot, invisible, holding a pattern that has already been carried away; you do not arrive anywhere, you only keep crossing. 

She read it by lamplight in November and corrected one line.  "Not she was left behind," she said.  "But she let go of the thread's far end."  It was the only vanity she allowed herself, and I set it down exactly. 

She died in February, when the chain was still up and the garden was iron.  I read her obituary aloud at the drained pool, to no one, and then I did the only thing my trade permits: I added a stake.  "Marit, carpet mistress, weft: gone in the winter the harbor was chained, aged eighty-one, having explained every absence in this garden but one." 

In April the chain came down and the first ship stood in.  I sailed with the thread still knotted at my wrist, going south to tell the living what their emptiness will be shaped like, and how to walk through it.
