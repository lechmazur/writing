The canyon held forty thousand abandoned dreams, and under the moon they glowed like quartz in a wound.  Ilsa Venn had helped bury them.  Now the company was gone, the lace network was being unwoven from every skull on the continent, and the world had spent two years relearning how to sleep.  They called it the Unweaving, and nobody's hands were steady.  Hers shook worst at dusk, which she had come to understand as growth, not damage. 

Ilsa was an engineer, and her mind ran in helices: every problem returned to its starting point one turn higher, paired strand against strand, never the same and never separate.  It was a good mind for building periscopes.  For nine years she had ground the mirrors that let technicians watch a sleeper's dream without touching it, light folded down a tube and into a stranger's dark.  The shard in her coat pocket came from rig twelve, from the first week of the Unweaving, the night the coupling failed.  Her brother Teo had been in the chair, she had been in the booth, the safety mirror cracked, the rig fell back on direct coupling, and their two channels bled into one.  Entangled memories, the clinicians said, and they were not being poetic.  Every time she recalled his face, the recall rewrote it; she had loved him so often that she had worn him away.  Teo had walked into the desert during the first month of the Unweaving, and she could no longer say whether that was something he did or something she dreamed. 

So she stopped recalling, and started excavating.  That was the ambition she had confessed to no one: to become the archeologist of abandoned dreams, to read the strata of what people had stopped wanting.  The company had dumped its unsold inventory here, cores like river cobbles, each one holding a night some sleeper never claimed.  By moonlight the walls showed their own layers, ash and flood and ash, chaotically precise, every crooked seam laid down to the millimeter by ten thousand years of water.  She wanted to dig the way the river dug, without lying about what she found. 

The obstacle was her own head.  Any dream she viewed would entangle with hers, and her hunger would collapse it into a story about herself.  So she set out to modify two things: the broken mirror, and the woman holding it.  On a flat stone she ground the shard against canyon grit, and her working hands trembled, and the tremor scrawled a thousand wandering scratches whose sum was a curve true to a quarter wavelength.  Chaotically precise, like the walls.  She scraped the silvering from half the curve until the broken thing became a splitter: one path for the dream's light, one for her own.  Two images side by side: the thing itself, and her hunger for it, so she could tell them apart. 

The harder modification was interior.  She could not delete her hunger, but she could grow a second strand beside it, through synaptic plasticity: nightly, for an hour, she rehearsed wanting nothing.  She held a core in the beam and let the images come and refused to name them hers, and the shaking in her hands was only the labor of a new path being cut.  Eleven nights, and the tremor deepened and steadied into something like a hand learning a language. 

On the twelfth she opened a stranger's abandoned night.  A woman she had never met stood in an orchard that was also a stairwell, counting pears that were also steps, and the dream had been dumped because nobody would buy a desire so plain.  Ilsa watched it through the split mirror and did not reach.  The orchard stayed an orchard.  She wrote in her ledger: layer nine, female, unknown, wanting only to climb toward something sweet; recovered intact. 

Then, because the new strand was strong enough to carry weight, she let one memory of Teo rise without chasing it.  He was laughing at something off to the left, and his face was not her face, and it was not the face she had been manufacturing for two years.  It lasted a second and a half, and she let it go the way the river lets go of a stone.  The moon crossed the canyon rim, the buried dreams went dim and ordinary, her hands quaked, and she kept working.  There were thirty-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine nights left to dig, and she intended to leave every one of them exactly as strange as it had been.
