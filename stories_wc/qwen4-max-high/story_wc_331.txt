The Scientific Poetry Library kept two collections: proofs and poems, shelved side by side because the founder believed both were ways of saying the same thing with fewer words.  The third collection had no catalog at all.  It was the analog memories: wire spools, wax cylinders, glass plates, letters written on the backs of calculations, the leavings of people who had trusted paper to hold them. 

Margit worked the odd hours.  One, three, five in the morning, by choice, because the building's temperature settled then and the old media stopped swelling and shrinking.  The reading room stayed open all night for the students, who came at three to be alone together.  Her colleagues called her sanely eccentric: every strange thing she did came with a reason you could check.  She wore white cotton gloves and counted her steps aloud, and she could show you the arithmetic behind both.  The night patrons thought of the odd-hour librarian as part of the furniture, a woman-shaped lamp at the desk, and she preferred it. 

The founder had built the deep stacks on a hyperbolic plan.  Every aisle branched into five, and each five into five again, the shelves narrowing as they went, so a finite floor held more frontier than any index could follow.  You could not search it; you could only travel it.  The founder's answer lay in a drawer at the desk: a tattered silk scarf, crocheted with too many stitches in every row, so it would never lie flat.  It ruffled and curled like a lettuce leaf, a hyperbolic plane in silk, and one gray thread ran through the ruffles in a curve that was, by the geometry of that surface, perfectly straight.  A geodesic: an address written as a path.  The scarf was tattered at its edge because forty years of hands had followed that thread to the fringe, where the surface frays into infinity. 

In the founder's notebook, the last line read: find the place where stories live.  Margit had copied it onto her wrist in ink, renewed each shift.  Her mother had been a poet who deposited ninety-one wire spools here the year her memory began to go, and then had gone too, and the deposit slip listed no location, because location, in that library, was a curve.  For nine years Margit had walked the branching with the scarf over her arm, matching each junction to a row of stitches, turning where the gray thread turned.  She had found other people's mothers that way, and played them back, and shelved them again. 

Tonight the thread ran out at a carrel no wider than her shoulders.  Ninety-one spools in a tin box, and a hand-cranked player with a horn like a morning glory, because the founder had refused to let a memory depend on electricity.  Out on the floor, patrons browsed: a student photographing citations, a man asleep on a journal, a woman reading standing up.  Margit threaded the wire and turned the crank at the speed her hands had learned, the way you reawaken a sleeper: slowly, and by name.  She said her mother's name aloud, twice.  The horn gave a hiss, then a woman counting stresses in a line about snow, then laughing at her own mistake.  Margit did not cry; she listened with her palms flat on the table, the way you hold a boat steady.  Spool ninety-one ended in the middle of a word. 

She understood then that the place where stories live is not a shelf but the small curved room between a voice and an ear, and that it must be entered again and again or it closes.  She wrote the path on the deposit slip in pencil, so the next person would not need nine years.  Then she took a needle from her cuff and lengthened the gray thread by one stitch, out toward the frayed edge, because a place is only findable if someone keeps extending the way to it.  She left the spools where they lay and carried only the slip back through the branching, out to the light of the reading room.  The student was still photographing citations.  The man slept on.  Nobody looked up; the library went on browsing around her, and Margit sat down alone at the desk with the scarf across her knees, its ruffles rising and falling like something breathing, and began the next hour.
