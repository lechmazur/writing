The letter from the clinic came on a Tuesday, and I folded it into the smallest square it would make.  Ninety-one days until the appointment, a date I had chosen myself, in a country that would let me choose it.  My daughter had a phrase for people like me: euthanasia tourist.  She said it the way you would say someone had booked a cruise instead of staying home to help.  I did not argue, because the thing growing in my spine was mine and so were the ninety-one days.  My grandmother came from a hot country and owned one beautiful thing, a lace parasol, and she used to tell me about the north, where she had worked one winter, where the sea froze and came walking.  Ice arrives with a sound first, she said, before you can see it, like rice poured slowly into a bowl.  She died badly, over eleven months, and nobody told her when it was coming.  So I went north to learn the sound of approaching endings.  The marina at Skarvik had been ruined three weeks before I arrived.  An early surge of drift ice came in on a northeast gale, snapped the pilings, and put eleven boats and half the fish house into the water.  I had come to wait, and instead I found a signup sheet taped to the door of the school gym.  Disaster relief, it said, and underneath, in pencil: cooks, haulers, listeners.  I wrote my name under haulers, because my back could still lift a crate if I was careful, and because waiting is worse with empty hands.  The harbormaster was a woman named Bergit, seventy-eight, who slept in a chair beside the radio.  The surge had taken her brother, and she never spoke of him, but she spoke of the ice constantly, the way you speak of a neighbor who has wronged you.  In my second week we went out to trawl for the mooring chains.  Two boats, a weighted line dragged between them, crawling the harbor floor while a boy named Jonas watched the depth sounder.  We brought up a bicycle, a hymnal swollen to twice its size, forty meters of chain, and one rubber boot.  Bergit set the boot upside down on the dock, gently, the way you would set down a cup, and went to splice the chain.  With the chain we could tie the fleet down before the next surge, which is the only argument I know that answers grief.  Bergit taught me to listen at night, when the wind came off the ice field forty miles out.  I had brought one thing of my grandmother's: the tip of the parasol, a bone ferrule and a triangle of lace no bigger than my palm, all that survived the moths.  Held up in the wind it made a small dry hiss, and Bergit approved: anything that catches air will tell you what the air is doing.  Bergit grips reality loosely, which is why she is good at this: she never insists the ice be where the chart says it is.  On the fortieth night I heard it, not the ice itself but the wind changing shape as it crossed the floes, a hiss with grains in it.  I woke Bergit, she listened for nine seconds, and then she rang the harbor bell twice.  The fleet moved to the far moorings before dawn, and nothing was lost.  I slept until noon and woke up changed: the same spine, the same folded letter, but the waiting had creased over on itself.  Bergit has a phrase for that, a fold in patience, the place where waiting doubles back and the end lies against the beginning so you can hold both without dropping either.  I have not changed the date.  Fifty-one days now, and I am still on the hauling list, still a tourist, but no longer only passing through.  I gave the lace tip to Jonas, whose hands are steadier than mine and who already holds it up without looking at it.  Tonight the wind is northeast, and I sit on the fish house steps with my collar up, listening.  It sounds like rice poured slowly into a bowl.  It sounds like my grandmother, telling me kindly, from a long way off, that it is nearly time to come in.
