The shooting star tracking station kept its own weather: cold iron, colder sky, and the smell of ash that came down every night like slow gray snow.  Ivo had swept it for thirty-one years.  They called him the caretaker on the payroll and the cosmic janitor in the town, and both names were true.  The telescopes had gone automatic before he was thirty; the catalogs were finished; the sky was solved.  Nobody needed a man to watch falling stars anymore, so the funding dried, the road cracked, and the hush of an overlooked era settled over the dome like silt.  He stayed because he was spiritually curious, which is a poor thing to be if you want money and the only thing to be if you want the truth. 

The machines recorded where each star burned.  They never asked what it felt like to spend yourself entirely in one bright stroke and then be dirt.  Ivo asked. 

He had a tool for it: a shard of yellow lantern glass from the old signal lamp, cracked loose the winter the generator died.  Held low against the floor, it turned the invisible visible; in that amber light the sweepings glittered, grains of iron and olivine, the ash of things that had been fire an hour before.  For years he had brushed it into the drain and hated himself a little each time.  His hands had begun to shake by then.  He hid them in his pockets when the supply truck came, ashamed of the tremor, ashamed of the useless dome, ashamed of asking the dark questions it would not answer. 

Then he learned to read the falls.  On the glass he drew their arcs with a grease pencil, tracing the path of falling stars backward from the burn to the cold, and forward from the burn to the ground.  Every arc ended the same way: not in failure, but in arrival.  That was how he came to empathize with them.  He would stand under the open slit of the dome with his hands out of his pockets, letting them tremble in the cold, and tell the sky he was burning too, only slower. 

The arcs told him where the ash would land, so he stopped sweeping it away.  He carried it out in a tin cup to the courtyard, where the concrete had split into a grid of cracks, and he dusted the cracks like a man sowing.  Iron feeds moss.  By the fourth spring the courtyard was green in every seam, thick and wet and breathing, a garden nobody had planted and nobody had come to see.  This was the thing the machines could not have recorded: a flourishing stillness, the quiet doing work.  In the loud years the yard had been swept bare every morning.  Only the hush had let it grow.  He had spent his life looking for strength in things that could not be hurt, and found it at last in a green crack. 

The auditor came in November with a shutdown order and shoes wrong for the road.  She was young and tired and had closed nine stations that year.  Ivo's hands shook as he took her coat, and he did not put them in his pockets.  He said he was afraid, that he had been afraid for thirty years, that the fear had never once stopped him from opening the dome.  She looked at him the way people look at a thing that has said too much. 

Then he put the yellow glass in her hand and knelt and showed her the floor.  In the amber light the dust rose out of the dark like a spilled sky.  He showed her the arcs on the glass, thirty years of them, layered until the shard looked like a net thrown over the night.  He walked her out to the courtyard and let her stand in the green seams.  She crouched and touched the moss with two fingers, and something in her face came loose.  She said she had been telling herself for years that being good at closing things was the same as being strong.  Ivo said the stars were not strong either; they were only willing to be seen going. 

She signed the order anyway, because the money was gone and she was not a god.  But she stayed until dawn, and he taught her to trace one fall from fire to ground, and she wrote in the margin of the form that the station was not empty.  Afterward the dome stayed shut and the moss went on eating iron in the dark, unhurried, unwatched, alive.
