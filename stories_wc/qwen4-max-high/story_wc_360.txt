The workshop is cut from topaz, and it keeps the stars the way a ledger keeps debts.  Ninety-one facets still burned above my press; a month ago there had been four hundred.  Each time one goes out, something falls.  I keep eleven of them in a type drawer: curved slivers, warm as a held breath, thin as the rule I use to space a line.  One for each year since I stopped printing my own name.  Halo fragments, the astronomers would call them, if any astronomers were left.  Hold one, and your grief goes visiting.  It leaves your chest for the nearest chest, and it arrives wearing the wrong name.  That is the beginning of my science: feeling can be transposed, like a chord played in another key.  I want the rest of it: the ratio, the conservation law, the arithmetic underneath sorrow. 

Sela climbed the stair at the ninth hour with a drowned wife and an order for memorial cards.  She had the flatness of a woman who has carried one weight too long.  A clean instrument, I thought.  I cannot use myself.  I borrow personalities the way other men borrow coats: the fisherman's shrug, the widow's clipped vowels, the boy's laugh that comes before the joke lands.  Whatever grief lands in me gets filed under a stranger's initial.  Sela was all one woman, and her sorrow had one address. 

I gave her the fisherman's shrug and asked what she wanted printed.  "Her name," she said.  "And that she is gone." 

Names are the trouble.  A named feeling stays put; it knows whose it is, and it will not travel far enough to be weighed.  So I disorient them.  It is the only honest cruelty in my trade.  I set her card with no names at all, a line of pronouns with nothing to hold them: SHE LEFT ME HERE ALONE WITH IT.  She could be the wife, or the sea, or the star that went out on Tuesday.  IT could be the body, or the grief, or the empty house.  Ambiguity is a door left open on purpose.  She read it twice and her mouth came apart.  "Whose is this," she said, not as a question. 

I put the halo fragment in her palm and pulled the press.  The gaps between vanishings shorten by a fixed ratio, and I had computed this one at four hundred pulls of the bar.  I was on ninety.  When the next star goes, the fragment clouds and every borrowed feeling snaps home; the experiment had to fit inside one gap. 

Her grief came across the bench like water finding a low place.  It entered me and put on the fisherman's coat, and I wanted to weep for a net I have never owned.  She, holding the fragment, took my emptiness for her own and said she felt strangely light, and hated herself for it.  Transposition, clean as a swapped signature.  I measured what I could: breaths per pull, sorts spilling from shaking fingers, hers and mine. 

Nothing was conserved.  The sorrow grew in the crossing, and it grew in proportion to how little of the receiver was named.  I was the error in every measurement I had ever taken.  I had not been borrowing personalities to study grief; I had been borrowing them so that nothing would ever arrive addressed to me. 

I was on one hundred and forty.  Sela reached for the drawer where the other ten slivers lay.  Lightness is the most addictive thing I have ever printed.  I could have let her keep it and owned a cleaner number than any man alive.  Instead I took the fragment from her hand and set one more line, and this time I set the names.  HER NAME WAS IDRA AND SELA LOVED HER AND SHE IS GONE.  I pulled, and the grief went back to its owner the way type goes back into its case, each sort to its own box.  She sat down under the ninety-one facets and wept correctly, horribly, her own. 

Then, with the gap closing, I set my own name in the composing stick.  Above the roofless wall a facet dimmed, and the sliver in my palm clouded, and something came into me with no coat on at all.  It was mine, it was not larger than itself, and I stood in the amber light and let it weigh exactly what it weighed.
