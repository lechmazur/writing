The lime factory shut down in March, and by June the pumps had stopped, so the water under the cliffs began to remember its old shape.  Ines Vardo collected petrichor.  She was sensibly eccentric about it: rubber boots resoled twice, a ledger ruled in half-millimeter columns, jars numbered and dated, each entry noting wind, temperature, and the barometric pressure at the moment the first drop struck dry stone.  Four hundred and twelve jars, and every one of them explained.  Geosmin, she wrote, actinobacteria, oils released from clay; the rain was only a key, and the lock was ordinary chemistry.  She had not started collecting to confirm the world.  She wanted the one place where explanations failed, and she had spent thirty years failing to find it.  Everything in her life had been accounted for: the factory's wages, her father's silicosis, the exact year the town began to empty. 

At the auction of the factory office she bought a lot of surveying junk for nine pounds because her hand had gone cold three feet before she touched it.  Inside the crate lay a brass compass, lid cracked, stamped HALLORAN EXPEDITION 1911.  Three men had walked into the cliffs to map the water and never walked out; the quarrymen found the compass in 1938 and kept it on the foreman's desk as a paperweight, which is how a thing survives. 

Ines read objects the way other people read weather.  Bare palm on metal, breath held, and the thing's history came up through her wrist like cold through a boot sole.  The compass gave her limestone sweating in lamplight, a man named Halloran laughing at a joke she could not hear, then a passage opening, then the laugh stopping.  Under the stopping there was a hush so full it rang, the sound a bell keeps after it quits.  And a smell she had no column for. 

Her private theory was that nobody wakes gradually.  Awakenings are hinged: they swing on a door, a threshold, a first drop on dust, and afterward you are in a different room of your life.  She had catalogued four hundred and twelve hinges and every one had opened onto the same room. 

So she delved.  She went down through the old adit while the lagoon was still rising, three weeks of dry weather behind her and the pumps silent above.  She carried empty jars, two lamps, a rope, and a notebook, and she told the last quarryman exactly where she would be, because eccentric is not the same as careless. 

The passage narrowed, then let go.  The lagoon lay beneath the cliffs like something held in cupped hands, black and unmoving, the ceiling reflected so exactly that she could not tell which way was down.  Her breath did not echo; the hush took sound the way the water took light, and gave back a low ringing under everything, the note of a room that had been waiting.  The compass needle, which had pointed north for a hundred years, swung and held on the water. 

She knelt at the edge and opened jar four hundred and thirteen.  The smell came up: rain on stone, yes, but also the smell of a door standing open in a house where nobody lives, of a held breath, of being expected.  She reached for her explanations and found the shelf empty.  No bacteria, no clay, no oil; the water had never seen rain, and there was no dust here to wet.  Halloran had stood here and stopped laughing, and she understood that he had not been frightened.  He had simply set the compass down, the way you set down a tool when the work it was for no longer exists. 

She could seal the jar and carry the smell home and spend the rest of her life failing to name it.  Instead she tipped it out.  She let the water have its own smell back, and closed the empty jar, and wrote on the label the date, the depth, the temperature, and nothing else.  In the ledger, under cause, she left the column blank, and the blank was the door. 

She set the compass on the ledge and climbed out through the hush, and at the adit mouth the first rain in a month was falling on the quarry dust, and she did not stop to collect it.  She had found the hinge, and she was on the other side, and for once she let the room stay unnamed.
