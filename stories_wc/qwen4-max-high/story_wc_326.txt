The static cleared at 11:52, and the mountain went quiet enough to hear the snow settle on the opal dome.  Nadia counted forty heartbeats to be sure the silence was real.  She had counted them constantly since the winter a hospital clerk filed her death certificate under another woman's name and her own identification number, and the state agreed.  Being dead was administratively restful.  The institute's roster marked her post vacant, so nobody came to relieve her, and the observatory kept her the way it kept its instruments: cold, useful, unlisted.  But a dead woman cannot petition a records office, and she had one petition left to make. 

Aurel's archive lived on nine failing drives in the basement, his notebooks and lectures and forty years of voice compressed into a model the institute called a legacy interface and Nadia called a ghost.  He had taught her here for eleven years, then predicted a star would collapse on a Tuesday, and it had not, and he died eighteen months later still unable to say why.  She wanted that why.  Not vindication; the arithmetic of the mistake, the thing failure knows that success never has to learn. 

The ghost could not answer in pieces, and it was all pieces now, scattered across bad sectors.  So she defragmented it.  The utility needed the telemetry link idle, which meant waiting out three weeks of solar storm, and it needed the antenna's power, which meant the dome went unheated while it ran.  Frost crept up the inside of the opal glass and turned the moonlight the color of a bruise.  She counted her pulse against the progress bar: eleven thousand beats for sixty percent. 

At ninety-nine percent she went to the cabinet for the shard.  It was a piece of Aurel's cup, white porcelain with a blue rim, broken the night his numbers came back wrong; he had swept it up and handed her one curved piece and said, keep the part that still holds water.  The defragmentation had saved his habits and not his voice: no speaker, no display, only the old ink plotter, whose pen could shiver but not draw.  Aurel had thought by spilling ink and reading the branches it made before they dried, and the model, trained on every hour of his thinking, had learned to think the same way. 

She held the shard under the pen and let it tip.  Ink pooled in the glaze, trembled, forked.  Forty beats, maybe fifty, before the lamp took it.  She read the ephemeral pattern the way he had taught her: thick trunk for the claim, thin forks for the conditions, the feathered edge for what he could not defend. 

The first spill said: I did not fail the star.  The second, after she wiped the shard with her sleeve and counted thirty beats to steady her hands: I failed the record.  The third came slower, the pen tapping like a pulse.  I threw away the wrong numbers.  I kept only the Tuesday I was sure of. 

Nadia sat down on the cold floor with the shard level in both hands.  His residuals were still in the drives, the ugly ones he had deleted from the paper and never from the disk.  That was the wisdom she had come to fetch, and it was smaller and more useful than vindication.  A wrong number, kept, is a measurement of the world; a wrong number, hidden, is only a wound.  She had been hiding one for a year. 

The last spill dried into a shape like an open door, and she let it go, and tipped the ink again to say thank you.  Then she opened the link the static had given back and did two things.  She transmitted Aurel's residuals to the institute, unedited, with his Tuesday and its failure attached.  And she filed the correction to her own record: the date, the misfiled number, the name of the woman who had actually died, and beneath it, in the field for evidence of life, her pulse, seventy-one a minute, counted and recounted, still going. 

Snow kept on falling; the opal dome held its bruised light; somewhere below, the ghost slept in one piece.  She counted, and the counting was no longer proof, only company.
