Nine thousand years dead, the shell still sheltered everybody.  It was gargantuan, a fallen cathedral on the reef, and both clans ensconced their children in its innermost whorl when the storms came, then spent the rest of the year arguing over whose children had the right to be there.  Mira, the shell's poet, could not sleep, so she was the one who kept the truce.  Four hundred years ago her ancestor and a boy from the other clan had written the terms on the back of a galaxy postcard, the only paper either had, and the spiral of stars on the front matched the spiral of the shell, which they took for permission.  The terms were simple: while the night blossom stood open in the shell's crown, nobody killed anybody.  They called it the blossom armistice, and it lasted three nights a year, and the rest of the year was the war.  The blossom opened only for the song.  It grew in the shell's shade, fed by a pale algae that lived on salt water and grief, so Mira wept where its roots could reach and the algae made a small cold light, and the plant drank that light and made buds.  That was her whole craft, really: to photosynthesize pain into beauty, and hand the beauty back to people who had not asked for it.  The song was the trouble.  The ancestors had written it in halves, one for each clan, so that neither side could sing the armistice alone.  The wind came in through the shell's mouth and went up the whorls and came out flat and wrong, an off-key wind, and it carried the half-sung lullabies down the corridors so that each side heard only its own half, over and over, forever.  Only in the middle chamber, on the three nights the ancestors had dared to allow, did the halves meet, and the flower opened, and the killing stopped.  This year the other clan's singer had died in the spring, and no one had taught his half to anyone.  Mira waited two nights.  She was shyly unstoppable: she could not look a stranger in the face, and she had never once stopped singing.  On the third night she took the galaxy postcard from its nail and walked down through the whorls toward the enemy's fires.  The nacre threw her small light around in green ripples, and the joy she felt was the reef-shadow kind, the kind that only grows where something has been broken.  In the middle chamber a boy sat guarding the cold hearth, maybe eleven, with a spear he did not know how to hold.  She could not look at him, so she put the postcard on the stone between them, front up, the spiral of stars.  Then she turned it over so he could read what his own people had promised.  "That's ours," he said, and then, uncertain, "Half of it."  "The other half is mine," Mira said.  "Neither one works alone."  She sang her half, four lines about a boat coming back, and stopped where the ancestors had stopped.  The off-key wind took it up the whorls and mangled it and brought it back.  The boy did not know his half.  So she taught it to him, reading from the postcard's margin, where his half was written small in a hand four hundred years dead.  He learned it in eleven tries, and the eleventh time they sang it together, her lines and his, the halves closing like two hands.  Above them, in the crown, the night blossom opened, and its light came down the whorls like water.  The boy laughed, and it was a strange sound in that place, and Mira laughed too.  She gave him the postcard.  "Keep it where your children can read it," she said, "and teach them both halves, so it never matters again who dies."  By summer the shell's children were singing themselves to sleep with the whole song, all year, badly, in the flat wind, and the blossom did not close, and nobody could find a night that belonged to the war.  Across centuries of longing, that was all the shell had wanted: not peace, exactly, but somebody awake enough to carry half a song down a corridor and hand it over.  Mira still did not sleep.  She lay in the green dark and wept a little for her brother, drowned nine years ago, and let the roots drink it, and listened to the light come on.
