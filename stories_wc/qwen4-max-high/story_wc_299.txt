The quarry office trailer smelled of wet paper and diesel, and Del Vance liked that, because neither smell asked her anything.  Three weeks earlier she had stood under television lights at the appraisal fair and told a widow that her husband's 1965 amplifier was a fake.  She had said it in her appraiser's voice, flat and kind and certain, while her stomach swung like a dropped cable.  Her colleagues called that tone confidently uncertain, and until the clip went around it had never cost her anything. 

For eleven years she had lived inside facts: serial stamps, weave counts, the year a maker switched from cotton cloth to polyester.  Facts were insulation; a woman could handle a dead man's guitar all day if she was only counting frets.  Her brother Toby had died in the band's van in 1996, and she had not said his name aloud since the funeral.  Before that she had been Hollow Bell's roadie, coiling cable in the dark; afterward she became a woman who could price anything and feel nothing.  Any roadie will tell you the real work begins after the applause, when the hall empties and somebody still has to carry what is left. 

The quarry job was punishment work, an estate clearance nobody wanted: Harrow Pit, flooding in nine days, site office contents to be valued and gone.  She had known the name when she read it, the way you know a song from two notes.  Hollow Bell had played the pit floor in August 1994, four hundred people on the stone benches, the walls throwing every chord back a half second late.  Toby had loved that delay and played into it, chasing his own echo up the valley while the crowd clapped in ragged time. 

Inside the trailer she worked fast, because fast was safe: ledgers, a truck scale, forty-one hard hats, a coffee tin of bolts.  Then a speaker cabinet jammed under collapsed shelving, and she had to wrest it free, and the grille cloth was wrong.  Wrong exactly as she had said wrong on television, from the cloth alone, never the serial: loose polyester where 1965 should have been tight cotton.  She knew the weave because she had bought the roll, in 1994, and re-clothed every cabinet the band owned on a plank stage in this pit, Toby's amplifier included. 

In the coffee tin, under the bolts, lay a scrap of speaker grille cloth, tight cotton, brittle with age, folded around a torn ledger page.  The owner's handwriting said Hollow Bell, cabinet repair, August 1994, cloth kept for the auction file, and her own pencil initials sat in the margin where she had marked the cuts.  The amplifier she had condemned under the lights was Toby's, sold at the pit auction to the widow's husband when the owner cleared the band's stored gear, and the cloth that condemned it was her own repair.  She had called her brother's amp a fake in front of a crowd because she had once fixed it for love, and she had been so certain, and everyone had heard. 

She sat down on the trailer steps with the scrap in her palm and did not count anything.  Every fact she had was still true; they simply added up to a person, which was the thing the facts had been built to keep out.  She called the producer from the trailer and left the appraiser's voice behind: "I was wrong, I can prove it, and I want to say so on the record."  Then she telephoned the widow and said that the amp was real, that it had belonged to her brother, and that she would bring the cloth and the ledger page herself. 

Nine days of water were coming for this office, and there were forty years of other people's leavings here that somebody would have to tell the truth about, slowly.  She could be certain of the weave and uncertain of everything else and keep working anyway; that, she thought, was a purpose, or the load-in for one. 

She clapped once, the way four hundred people had clapped in the pit, and waited.  "Toby," she said, and the far wall gave it back a half second late, thinner, but it gave it back.  The applause had ended in 1996; she was still here, carrying what was left.
