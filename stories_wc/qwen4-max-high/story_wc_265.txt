Bel had been a cartographer of conclusions until the maps began mapping him.  That was the trouble with the sixth sense: eleven years after it woke in everyone, thinking had become a place you could arrive at.  Every mind hummed when it found something true, and the hum pulled the sleeping body toward whatever it had understood.  Bel understood the same nine things every night, so every morning he woke in the same brass plaza, among colleagues who smelled of lamp oil and certainty.  So he resigned, and became a stray philosopher, which is only a philosopher who has stopped arriving.  He wanted the wild frontier of his own mind back, the part no survey had ever reached.  The guild paid by the arrival; Bel had begun to suspect he had never once thought anything of his own.  The rainforest had grown up out of all that hum, the way coral grows out of the labor of small animals.  Its trees were mechanisms: gear-trunks, cable lianas, leaf-plates of hammered tin that ticked when the oil rain came down.  Each species was a family of ideas, and Bel had learned to navigate by the sound of wind through them.  Wind in the axiom-oaks sounded like applause; wind in the doubt-ferns hissed; the great theorem-palms rattled their proofs all night.  He walked east for two weeks, listening for a sound he could not name.  Where the canopy was well understood, the forest was solid, bolted, smug.  Where the maps thinned, the trees began to unravel.  Bel found a cable liana loose at its end, unspooling into bright wire, and above it a tree coming apart politely into its components, dropping them into the moss.  That was how the frontier looked: not empty, but unassembled.  He picked a small gear out of the litter.  It had nineteen teeth, an orphan count, and it fit nothing he knew.  Miracles were probable out here; that was only arithmetic, since nothing had been decided yet.  Bel sat with the gear and did the thing his guild had forbidden: instead of following a thought to its conclusion, he unraveled it backward.  What is a gear for, he asked, and then, what is 'for,' and then, who said a thing must be for anything at all.  Each premise he pulled came loose like a thread from a hem, and the hum in his skull dropped to a note he had never heard.  The sixth sense was not hearing truth anymore; it was hearing room.  Wind came through the unraveling tree and made a sound he could not name, and Bel laughed out loud in the empty green light.  Then he lay down in the moss with the gear in his fist and slept, which was the dangerous part.  Sleep was the vehicle: a body went where its understanding pointed, and he had just spent an evening unpointing his.  He might wake in the plaza, or in a lake, or nowhere anyone had bothered to be.  The odds of a miracle were good, and the odds of drowning were also good, and Bel had made his peace with both.  He woke at dawn in the moss where he had lain down.  For the first time in eleven years, his mind had not carried him anywhere, because there was nowhere else it was pointing.  His fist had opened in the night.  The small gear stood on edge in the moss, and out of its bore rose a stem of soft brass, and on the stem, four tin leaves, unfolding.  It was not a tree yet, and it was not a thought anyone had finished.  Bel put his ear close and waited for the wind.  When it came, the sapling made a sound he could not name, and could not stop listening to, and did not want to name.  Somewhere behind him the mapped forest ticked and applauded, and the hum in his skull went on ahead of him into the unassembled green.  By evening, he thought, there would probably be a whole grove of things nobody had decided yet.  He took out his notebook, and left it shut, and walked east.
