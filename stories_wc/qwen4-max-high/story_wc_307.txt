The sycamores held the hush the way a cup holds water.  Under their pale shedding bark the pond lay black and still, and a hand's breadth above it floated a slab of grey rock.  Hessa Vane called the slab the platform, and she called her work listening.  The ledger said she was seventy-one; her face said thirty-four, and the spring before it had said forty.  She was aging backwards, and she had made herself the instrument.  The subtraction was speeding up, and she recorded the acceleration with great satisfaction.  I came to her at twenty-two, after my mother's funeral, to learn how to measure loss.  She said the world subtracts things constantly and leaves no picture behind.  Her whole ambition, she told me, was to teach vanishing to appear.  I thought that meant a good photograph of an empty chair.  She let me think it for nine years. 

Beneath the pond there is a grotto of magnetite, and it wakes only under starlight.  Clouded nights, the rock sits on the bottom like any stone.  Clear nights, the stars comb the iron into one direction, the field rises through the water, and the platform lifts and hums.  You sit on it cross-legged, and the field holds your attention the way a bowl holds a marble.  That is the trance.  Then you throw a stone, and you read the rings.  Ordinary eyes see circles widening and dying.  Inside the trance the rings cross the field and snag on whatever is missing, and the interference draws it.  A drowned dog.  A demolished house.  A word a stroke took.  The first thing I ever read there was my mother's hand. 

Nine winters I read for her, and she wrote down what I called out.  She never lied to me, not once.  She wove the truth carefully, giving me each true piece out of order, so the pattern would close only when my hands were steady enough to hold it.  The last page she kept.  One number, she said, and she would withhold it until I could read it myself.  I was angry about that for a long time. 

Meanwhile her sleeves grew long.  Her handwriting rounded, lost its slant, became a girl's careful print.  By autumn she was fifteen and had to stand on a stone to see the water from the platform.  She still corrected my counts.  That night she was nine.  She put a stone in my palm, grey and warm from her pocket, and said it was the first one she had ever thrown.  Then she said: read it alone. 

The stars came out over the sycamores, the grotto woke, and the rock rose under me with its low hum.  I threw her stone and went down into the trance.  The rings went out as rings do, struck the field, and came back carrying her.  I expected the usual shape of a vanishing: a widening, a thinning, an edge going soft.  Instead the interference ran inward.  Every ring closed on the centre of the pond, tightening, brightening, the way water gathers before a thing breaks the surface from below.  I had read a hundred losses and never once seen one arrive.  The pattern gave a place, which was the water under my feet.  It gave a weight, which was seven pounds.  And it gave the number she had withheld: a date, eleven days off. 

She had not been studying how to keep herself.  She had been teaching vanishing to appear, using her own subtraction as the lesson because it was the only one large enough to read.  Backwards is not away.  She was coming toward the beginning, and the beginning had a door, and the door was this pond.  If she had told me the date in the first year, I would have spent nine years counting instead of learning.  She withheld it so that when it came I would not need to believe her.  I could read it.  All of it arrived inside the trance, in the time one ring takes to cross the pond. 

I came up out of the trance near morning.  The girl was asleep against a sycamore with her shoes off, small enough now to carry.  I had come to learn how to keep what was gone.  She had taught me to be waiting when it came back.  I put my coat over her and sat down at the edge of the water to wait for the eleventh day.  The stars were still up, the grotto still humming, the rock still hanging its hand's breadth above the pond.  I held her stone, and I listened, and the sycamores kept the hush.
