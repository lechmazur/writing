The forest above Kettle Ridge sang too high for ears, so Sefa read it with her hands.  She was the district's last blind mapmaker, and the only one who could tell you where the loud trees stood.  Language came into her through skin.  Bark, throat, palm, page: whatever touched her poured its grammar in, and she could not close the door.  In the ultrasonic forest that meant ten thousand voices a minute, all of them speaking, none of them hers. 

Since Teodor died in the spring, one more voice had joined the crowd and would not leave.  She had held his hand while he went, and taken his last sentence straight into her blood, where it circled like a swallowed bell.  She could not remember what grief felt like underneath it.  That was the country she had never mapped. 

The old charts called it a stillpoint: a hidden place where the forest's chorus cancelled itself out and nothing spoke.  Sefa had copied those charts for forty years without believing them. 

Now the constellations were shifting.  Every nineteen years the sky's figures came apart and reassembled, and between the old shape and the new one there was a pause, three nights wide, when the stars held still and the forest changed key.  Only in that pause, the charts said, did the forest hold one key long enough to be answered. 

She went out on the first night with her staff, her satchel of blank vellum, and the platinum compass ring on her thumb.  Platinum keeps no magnetism and tells no north; it is the honest metal, the one that stays at zero.  Teodor had cast it for her so she could dowse for absence instead of direction. 

She hung it on a horsehair and let it swing above the needles, dowsing the way he had taught her.  Where the world spoke, the ring spun; where the speech thinned, it slowed.  By midnight it had stopped spinning and begun to tremble, and she found the first note.  It lived in a split trunk, a tone her palms translated as a single syllable: here.  Twenty paces on, a stone under moss gave her the second: still.  Then a root, then a hollow antler, then a spring that stammered one clean pitch.  A trail of notes, each one colder under the ring than the last.  Every place the ring slowed was a place where something in the wood was already singing against the chorus.  She wrote them in raised wax, the only ink she could read. 

Each note also cost her; to take a pitch she had to lay her hand flat and let the forest's whole vocabulary in with it.  By the second night she was carrying the grammar of moths, of fungus, of a dead surveyor's carved initials, and Teodor's sentence still ringing over all of it.  She kept walking. 

On the third night the trail turned back on itself and the notes began to repeat, and she understood she had been reading them wrong.  They were not directions.  They were a melody, scattered, waiting to be put in order. 

She sat in the needles and set the wax notes side by side with her fingertips, while the ring turned slowly, waiting for an answer.  Ordered, they made a tune she knew: the four-bar thing she used to hum over her drafting table, wordless, hers.  The forest had been keeping it for her, the way it kept everything. 

She hummed it back.  The trees took the melody and turned it against themselves, crest to trough, and the shouting folded shut.  The ring hung dead still, and the silence had edges. 

In it, Teodor's sentence loosened and went out of her like breath.  What was left was unlabelled and enormous, and it was hers, and she let it come.  She wept without translating it. 

Above her the pause closed; the sky's figures snapped into their new arrangement and the forest found its key again.  She did not hurry.  When the noise returned she took out a fresh sheet and pressed the stillpoint into it: a small blank circle, wax-rimmed, at the center of everything she had charted.  No coordinates, no legend.  Only the tune, in raised wax, walking toward the blank, so that any hand that touched the map would know the way in was a song. 

She slid the ring back onto her thumb.  Then the blind mapmaker stood, and went home through the shouting trees, quiet as a coal that has decided to burn.
