The silo had been built to hold one thing pointed at the sky, and now it held books, which point at nothing.  Ottoline Brack came down its spiral every morning with a bronze rod in her pocket, and the librarians called her the odd historian, because she catalogued walls.  Books kept what people had chosen to say.  Walls kept the rest.  She had come to collect the stories walls had heard, and no one had ever asked her to stop.  Nobody argued with her; it had been eleven years since the last human dream was dreamt, and argument needs a picture of how things might otherwise be.  Her gift was small and useless: she noticed everything.  The grain of a handrail worn hollow by one particular thumb.  The half-degree by which a corridor's air changed where someone had once stood weeping.  The rod was her probe, a listening needle she pressed to plaster until the room came up through her wrist.  That winter her map began to lean.  Every wall she probed spoke as if tilting toward a point that was not the shaft.  The shaft was the library's axis, nine levels of shelves wound around the empty column where the missile had stood, and every catalogue number began from it.  The walls disagreed.  They leaned toward something off-center, low, and warm.  She spent forty days triangulating the reclaimed silo, following the tilt the way a root follows water.  It brought her to a door on level seven that the catalogue listed as ballast.  Behind it lay a wardroom, a table, and a fireplace with a fragment of medieval tapestry nailed above the mantel.  Wool gone the color of weak tea, and in it three dragons, wings half-unravelled, chasing something the weaver had left unwoven.  In the fireplace lay half-burned diaries.  She read them the way she read walls, slowly, with her hands.  The name inside was Marit Solvang, the silo's last dreaming librarian, and she had burned her diaries one page a night, in order, from the beginning.  The early pages were ordinary: quarrels, weather, a bad tooth.  The later pages counted the dreams going out, house by house, like lamps.  Marit set down what she could hold of her own while it lasted, and at the end only this: the dragons again, over the fire, the same three, the same unfinished thing they chased.  I have looked at them since I was a girl.  I think they waited in the wall for me to be quiet enough.  The last page was scorched to brown lace; its one legible line said the dream had come the night before, and would not come again.  The fire had gone out somewhere in that last page, and she had not lit it again.  Ottoline sat back on her heels and understood the lean.  The axis had been displaced.  Not the shaft, not the sky: the dreaming of this place had turned around a hearth on level seven, around a woman watching wool dragons until the wall watched back.  Every wall in the silo had heard her tell it afterward, on the stairs, at the rail, in the dark of the corridors, and had kept the telling, and leaned toward its source ever since.  She could have carried the fragment up to the climate-controlled cases, where the tapestries were numbered from the shaft.  Instead she opened her catalogue and gave the wardroom the number zero.  Let the shelves keep their old center; a building may be measured from its weapon and still turn on its fire.  She hung her lamp on the mantel hook and set the bronze against the chimneystack.  The wall gave it back the way bronze gives back a struck note, long after the striking: a girl, a fire, three wings, and the sound of someone saying them aloud to whoever was awake.  She wrote it in the bronze quiet, in the hand she used for walls, which was slower than her hand for books.  Nothing new had been dreamt in eleven years.  Nothing had been lost either, only displaced, and it was her work to write down what the walls had heard.
