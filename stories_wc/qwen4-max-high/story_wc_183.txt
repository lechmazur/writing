The carnival ship Merryweather had drifted in the fog of Kettle Bay for eleven years, engineless, unanchored, and never once aground.  Marta Voss surveyed wrecks for the harbor authority, and she had learned to distrust anything the fog kept afloat that long.  Still, she went aboard on the third of November with a lantern, a clipboard, and an emerald swatch card folded in her breast pocket.  Her mother had chosen that green for a nursery forty years ago, and on the back she had penciled two names.  One was Marta.  The other had blurred into a graphite smudge, the way a word does when a thumb worries it for decades.  At nineteen, a blood typing had told Marta why her mother would never speak that second name: she had absorbed her twin in the womb and carried her still, two blood types in one body, a green eye and a brown one.  The doctors called it chimerism; Marta, who had discovered the sister living in her own marrow, called it company.  Blissfully aberrant, the cells were, thriving where they should not have been, and Marta had never felt alone a day in her life.  What she felt instead was the absence of a name, and the salvage men's rumor of a garden of forgotten names growing in the Merryweather's hold.  Below the carousel deck, where the fog had rotted the canvas awnings, she found it.  Thousands of pale shoots rose from the ballast gravel, each one strung with a single filament, and on every filament hung a name like a drop of dew.  The green of them was exactly the green of her card.  She held the swatch against a leaf and felt the first cold comfort of the evening: the color matched, and nothing else would.  When she brushed a filament, it spoke in her mother's voice, and the name on it went out like a spark in water.  She understood the rule then, the way one understands rules in dreams: the names were held by narrative threads, and a name untold came loose.  To keep one, you had to give it a life.  She found her sister's filament by its smudge, the only blurred name in the bed, and the name slid loose as she reached.  It drifted up toward the deck grating, a mote of dew with her sister inside it, and Marta began to talk.  She told the life the girl never had: the crooked tooth, the winter she would have spent hating swimming lessons, the emerald kitchen walls, the dog named Pilot, the argument at seventeen about a boy, the ordinary Tuesdays.  Each detail spun a thread out of her mouth, thin and green, and the threads crossed the hold and caught the fleeing name and drew it back.  She had recaptured it, and the filament hung heavy, and she read what her thumb had spent forty years erasing.  Iris.  The name went into her mouth and did not warm.  Iris did not stir in her marrow, did not answer, did not become a sister who could be missed properly.  This was the solace the garden sold: unkindled, a match head damp in its box, comfort that would never catch and never quite go out.  Above her the carousel turned a quarter inch, the way it had every tide for eleven years, and the fog came down the companionway to collect its fee.  As memories alter, the bay takes the difference.  She had come aboard remembering her mother's pencil moving twice; now she remembered it moving once, and a smudge that had always been a smudge.  The dog named Pilot became a dog named Biscuit, then no dog at all.  Every invented Tuesday she had spun was true somewhere and nowhere, and she felt them leaving her, paid out into the threads.  She could go.  The ship had no lock on her, the fog no wall; the dinghy waited, and the town lights were a smear she could row toward in forty minutes.  But a name untold comes loose, and telling it once had bound it for only one tide.  Marta wrote IRIS on the emerald card in pencil, pressing hard enough to score the paper, so that no thumb of hers could ever smooth it away.  Then she climbed to the carousel and rode the painted horse that faced the stern, going nowhere at the speed of the tide, and began again: the crooked tooth, the winter, the walls.  Below her the green threads tightened, holding a girl who would never be born and never be lost, and the cold comfort in Marta's chest stayed cold, and stayed.
