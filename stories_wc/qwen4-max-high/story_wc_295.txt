The celestial mapping observatory was the kindest room I knew at two in the morning.  Osip measured photographic plates at one desk, and I slept at the other, on purpose, with a lamp burning.  The stove ticked, the dome turned once an hour, and nobody in that room expected me to be cheerful.  My title in the director's ledger was lucid dream chronicler, which meant I could hold a dream steady, walk its rooms, and copy what the walls said. 

For nine years the rubbings from the drowned temple had defeated every linguist who tried them.  Two columns ran side by side down a corridor of basalt, and that corridor now lay under sixty feet of reservoir water.  Everyone assumed the second column translated the first, and everyone failed, because the words almost matched and then swerved.  I had come to decode those inscriptions the way you come to a bedside. 

In my ledger, pressed flat between the pages for eleven years, was a telegram with a daylily inside it.  The flower had gone the color of weak tea, and the message said, THE LILIES OPENED TODAY COME AND SEE.  My mother sent it from the hospital garden, and I did not go, and she died on the Thursday.  I could enter any dream I liked and I had never once entered that one.  I wanted the temple read because I did not know how to answer her, and I thought the dead might have left instructions. 

The break came from Osip's plates.  Each entry in the temple began with a phrase we had been calling a god's name: the lamp in the Swan, the breathing coal, the slow blinker.  They were variable stars.  The temple had dated its nights by stars that brighten and dim on a fixed period, and Osip had thirty years of those periods measured to the hour.  We could finally put the dreams in order.  They were dreams, that much was certain; the corridor was a sleeping house where the sick came to lie down and be told what their nights meant.  Ordered by the stars, one woman's nights ran through four months, her drowned son rising in each of them, saying less, then nothing.  By the last night she dreamed of a garden. 

Understanding came at the hour the sky went gray over the reservoir, when Osip put down his eyepiece and rubbed his face.  I read the second column aloud and heard, for the first time, that it was not a translation.  It was the same dream told back to her in the second person.  You dreamed of your son; you were afraid; I sat with you until the lamp in the Swan turned.  Two hands cut the columns, and the second hand added one small line every time, a line the linguists had thrown out as a scribal error.  It said, now it is carried by two.  That was the whole grammar we had missed.  The temple did not cure anyone; it witnessed them, and the witnessing was the medicine, words laid on the wound in the right order until the wound could bear its own weight. 

Osip was quiet a long time.  Then he said his wife's name, which he had not said in that building in six years, and told me the dream he kept having about the stairs.  I wrote it in the first column, in his words.  Then I wrote the second, in mine: you dreamed of the stairs, you could not reach her, I am sitting with you until the lamp turns.  He read it twice and his shoulders came down from around his ears.  I had always healed with words, slowly, the way a variable star mends the dark by coming back. 

Then I took the telegram out of the ledger and the dry daylily with it, and I read my mother's message to him.  I told him the dream I had refused for eleven years, the one where I am always packing to go and the train has already left.  He took the pen and wrote the second column while I spoke, in his slow measuring hand.  You did not come; you have carried the lilies alone; I am here, and now it is carried by two. 

Outside, the reservoir held the temple and the sky held its slow blinkers, and the lamp in the Swan was due to brighten at ten past five.  We stayed to witness it, the two of us, and I slept at the desk with the ledger open and dreamed of a garden.
