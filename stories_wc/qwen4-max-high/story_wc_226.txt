In the canyon of endless echoes, silence was not a thing you found; it was a thing the wardens made.  They forged it every morning, hammering the noise flat, quenching it in the cold air, until the whole gorge rang with a hush that had seams in it.  The towers of bone stood along the walls, pale as knuckles, grown from every word the canyon had ever refused to let die.  At the head of it, in permanent mist, rose the pale spire.  Once a year the town fed it a confession, and the spire drank the sound and gave back forgiveness.  The price was simple: one truth, spoken once, by one mouth.  Afterward, no other version could be remembered. 

Corvane ground glass for a living, lenses and prisms, and she had been called defiant so often that the word had worn smooth.  She etched a line of verse into every lens she sold, which was why the wardens had never liked her.  Her brother Tam had drowned on the lower terraces with forty others when the wardens opened the floodgates to save the granary.  At dawn the Warden would climb to the spire and say: the water rose.  Three words, and the hand on the wheel would belong to no one. 

Corvane could not stop the ritual.  But she knew what a prism does.  It refracts.  A single beam goes in; many come out, each one true, each one bent a different way.  The spire could swallow one truth.  It had never been asked to swallow a hundred.  She had no lie to tell; she only wanted to refract one truth into many. 

So she started, that evening, in the low lanes, with impromptu poetry sessions.  No stage, no permits, no poems written down.  She would point at a woman mending a net and say, give me Tam, one line, from where you stand.  The woman said, he owed me for the net.  A boy said, he could swim, he could swim, he could.  An old man said only, the granary was full that year.  Every line was true.  None was the same.  And the canyon, which forgets nothing, took each one and passed it up the towers of bone, hand to hand, until the walls hummed under the forged hush like a struck bell held still. 

The wardens came, of course.  They could arrest a speaker; they could not arrest a listener who had already heard.  They fined her twice and took her grinding wheel, and the sessions moved to the cistern steps, then to the rope bridge, then to the queue for bread. 

It was a child who made it unstoppable.  Pell, six years old, innocently mischievous, turned the lines into a skipping rhyme because counting was boring and Tam's name fit the rhythm.  He did not mean to defy anyone.  He meant to jump higher.  By midnight the rhyme was in three districts, and no warden could find its author, because it had none.  Corvane sat on the cistern steps with her empty hands and listened to the truth split and split again, and she was afraid, and she was glad. 

Near dawn the Warden came to her, alone, without his guards.  He was old; his own son had been on the terraces.  He said, I want the quiet as much as you want the noise.  He said, the price of forgiveness is one truth, and I have been paying it for eleven years.  Corvane said, you have been paying it with our memories, which is cheaper.  He did not argue.  He asked her to stop, and she said no, and he walked up toward the mist. 

The ritual would begin at first light.  She had one hour left, and she spent it teaching the rhyme to the men who would carry the Warden's chair.  They learned it easily.  That was the thing about a truth with many faces: it was light enough for anyone to carry.  When the Warden opened his mouth at the spire, the towers would answer him in a hundred voices, and the pale thing in the mist would find nothing single to swallow.  There would be no forgiveness this year.  Corvane understood what that cost her: no absolution for her defiance, no quiet, ever, and Tam still cold in her chest every morning.  She paid it before the ritual, sitting on the steps, listening to children skip in the dark.  The hush held its seams.  Under it, the canyon said her brother's name, and said it, and said it.
