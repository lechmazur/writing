The saffron veil was not a decoration; it was a filter, nine folds of dyed silk that caught the dust of the dead before it reached her lungs.  Nira had worn one since she was eleven, and in nineteen years she had never once taken the same stair down into the archive.  The Order's historians called this discipline; her mother had called it survival. 

Below the salt plain lay the archive of Hess, a civilization that had learned to store memory in matter and had died of the learning.  Its shelves held no books, only sealed clay jars of gray powder.  Each grain was an atom of nostalgia, indivisible, indestructible, holding one moment of one life at full strength forever.  Breathe one and you would taste a stranger's wedding morning, her joy exactly as warm as it had been four thousand years ago.  Breathe it twice and you would want nothing else. 

The Order taught that Hess had burned under an enemy's torch, and that the jars were relics to be catalogued and never opened.  Nira had counted the scorch marks on the walls at nineteen and found none.  She wanted the sealed shelf at the bottom, the one labeled in old Hessic: the End of Innocence.  Nine jars, the last nine days of a people, and the reason her Order forbade them.  To open them was expulsion; to want it aloud was worse. 

She could only read at twilight, in the bronze prism pagoda the Hessians had built at the archive's mouth.  Seven tiers of angled bronze caught the low sun and split it, and only that split light, sliding sideways through a jar's quartz window, could shake a single atom loose.  At noon the pagoda was only a roof; at dusk it was an eye. 

Her mother's rule was written on the veil's hem in stitches so small only a daughter would find them: purify by avoiding patterns.  Nostalgia atoms bind by repetition; a repeated route, a repeated hour, a repeated grief, and they lattice inside a living mind like salt in a drying pool.  So she took a different stair each descent, ate at no fixed hour, slept facing a new wall.  The trick was not chaos.  Chaos clumped; randomness left streaks a lattice could climb.  She had trained herself to be steadily unpredictable, varying at an even, unrelenting rate, constant in nothing but her refusal to repeat.  Met once, in a mind with no grooves to catch it, an atom leaves knowledge behind and no hunger. 

On the first evening of her trespass she set the first jar in the pagoda's split light and lifted her veil.  The atom came to her like a bee to a bell.  And she was a woman in Hess on the last ordinary morning, holding a warm loaf, laughing at something her son said, entirely certain that tomorrow would resemble today.  Nira wept inside a stranger's happiness.  Then the light moved, the atom spent itself, and the moment was gone out of the world forever, because that is what purification costs: an atom released once cannot be gathered again.  She had known this and had come anyway.  Her innocence had lasted thirty years; it took nine evenings to spend it. 

Eight evenings, eight jars, no order she could have predicted, no shelf touched twice.  She learned the truth in pieces out of sequence: the granaries full, the streets quiet, the archive's doors open day and night, a people choosing yesterday because tomorrow had begun to require work.  No enemy, no torch. 

The ninth jar held a woman in a saffron veil, standing where Nira stood, four thousand years earlier.  The last archivist of Hess, emptying her own city's memory jar by jar so that no one could sit down inside it and starve.  She had gotten through a tenth of the shelves before her own people shut the archive and called her a vandal.  Their heirs kept her veil and forbade her work.  Her mother had said the walls held no scorch marks, said it aloud, and been sent up the stairs for good, stitching the rule into her daughter's hem first. 

Nira laughed, then, alone under the bronze tiers with the sun going out, and the laugh hurt like a hand pulled from a fire.  Nine moments saved from the dark and nine moments destroyed, and both were hers, and neither could ever be hers again.  Below her waited four thousand shelves.  She wrote nothing down, because a page is a pattern, and she would need every evening to be unlike the last.  Tomorrow she would take the stair she had never taken, and grieve, and be glad, and empty another jar.
