The greenhouse had not seen morning in eleven years, not since the sky closed, and the orchard inside it had learned to fruit by lamplight.  Outside, the fields lay winter-cracked and hushed, fissures running under the snow like veins under skin.  Ivo slept in the potting shed at the orchard's heart, his breath shallow as a moth's, his hands still black with grafting wax.  I had put him there.  The cup I carried to our wedding bed was honeyed and lovingly venomous, three drops of the orchard's own distillate, because the blight had already eaten through his lungs and I could not bear the noise of him drowning in air.  The venom stopped the rot and his heart in the same instant, which the alchemists call a held note, not a death.  I drank the rest myself, and the burnt row's ash came down on my hair as an ashen veil, and I did not go where the dead are supposed to go.  The orchard keeps ledgers.  Nine years earlier he had breathed his own years into my mouth when the well-gas took me, nine of his for nine of mine, and such debts are not cancelled by dying; they cross lifetimes and wait.  I owed him a waking, and I had only a ghost's small change to pay it with.  Everything grown in that alchemical glass wakes at its own note.  Ivo taught me the resonant awakenings himself, walking the rows with a bow of rosined wire, drawing a tone out of the carved stone beads he strung on every branch until the sleeping tree inside the seed came up through the dark asking for light.  He had carved one for himself, too, a river pebble no bigger than a plum stone, grooved with the spiral of his own name-note, and it lay on the potting bench two hand-spans from his open fingers.  Two hand-spans is nothing when you have hands.  I could not lift it, or breathe on it, or tip the bench.  What I could do was appear.  Not haunt, which is staying, but ghost: a cameo in the black pane above the bench, a face in the frost of the propagator, a bride-shaped flare in the lamp, gone before the glass could finish holding me.  Each ephemeral appearance lasted one held breath, and in that breath I could sing.  A stone answers its own note by shivering.  So I sang his note at his bead, and it moved a hair's width toward him, and I was gone.  That was the first night of the winter.  Over the hush of the winter-cracked fields, while the fissures outside crept and ticked and the snow held its breath, I paid him back in hair's widths.  Nine cameos a night, then six, then three; the singing cost me veil first, then hands, then the shape of my mouth.  The orchard woke around me as I worked, because a note sung true does not travel alone.  Buds split in the endless dark; the quince row put out glass leaves; somewhere a seed he had planted the year we married cracked its stone and stood up green.  I was so glad I could have wept, if I had anything left to weep with.  Every hair's width was a grief and a festival.  Because I understood the arithmetic: the note that woke him would spend the last of me.  What wakes the sleeper unmakers the waker; he had written that in the margin of his notebook, in ink, underlined twice, the way a man writes down a price he intends to pay.  On the last night of the winter the bead lay in the crease of his palm, and I had one cameo left, no veil, no hands, only the note.  I appeared in the pane above him, ash-grey and bride-thin, and I sang.  The stone took the note and gave it back doubled, and his fingers closed, and the sound went into his bones like water into dry ground.  The blight came out of him in a grey breath that fogged the glass, and every tree in the orchard rang at once, and the lamps burned white.  Outside, the cracked fields kept their hush, but under the glass it was suddenly, impossibly spring.  He sat up coughing, alive, his hand full of stone, and said my name to the empty air above the bench.  I was already the ringing, and the ringing was already going out into the rows, and I had never been so sorry, or so paid, or so happy.
