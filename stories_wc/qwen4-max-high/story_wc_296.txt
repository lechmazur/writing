After the trick failed, Maren knelt at the shore of the cursed lake and gathered the pieces of her brother's mirror.  She had performed his last illusion exactly: the palm-flat pass, the breath held, the coin of light thrown across the water.  The lake had swallowed the light and given nothing back, the way it had swallowed him eleven winters ago.  Maren was a druid, and a guarded one; she kept her grief folded shut like a fist and her mouth shut over it.  Villagers came to her because she found lost things: rings in gutters, lambs in snow, a drowned man's wedding band.  Her method never varied.  She dropped a pebble at the place of losing and read the expanding circles, walking outward ring by ring until the water touched what was gone.  It had never failed her, except once. 

Now she rowed toward the heart of the lake, where the orchard turned.  Apple trees grew out of the whirl, roots gripping the spinning water, boughs sweeping the surface in slow revolution.  Blossom fell inward, petal by petal, and the fruit never ripened, and the whole grove circled the still eye at its center like soldiers around a standard.  Maren shipped her oars and let the current carry her in, calm as a woman laying a blade flat on a table.  In the eye, the water was glass.  A mirror, then, after all: it showed her own face, gray and closed, and nothing of what she had come for.  That was the whole of her quarrel with mirrors.  They gave back surfaces; she wanted what surfaces kept. 

Beneath the glass, a hand's depth down, hung a knot of ice with a shape inside it like a curled tongue.  The lake froze confessions there.  Every last word spoken at the drowning moment, held mid-breath, never thawed, never heard.  Her brother had been a showman of small vanishing acts, all mirrors and misdirection, and she had never once asked him what he was hiding behind them.  Now his sentence lay bent in the ice, and she meant to unbend it. 

She took the seed from the pouch at her throat.  Her order kept such seeds for the worst fields: a flesh-eating plant, marsh-bred, that would take root in warm meat and grow.  Its hunger made heat; heat was the only thing that could open a frozen confession.  The price was flesh, and the flesh had to be offered, not thrown.  Maren had kept her left hand closed since the funeral, as though it still held his.  She unbent it.  The fingers opened one at a time, stiff as a bow unstrung, and she set the seed in the pale center of her palm and pressed it under the ice.  It split, and rooted, and began to eat.  She did not pull away.  She had watched men die badly for less than a sentence; she held still and let the green mouth work, breathing slow, at peace the way a spear-wall is at peace. 

Warmth spread from her palm; the ice around the curled tongue softened and let it go.  The confession did not make a sound.  It made circles.  They rose out of the eye and widened through the turning orchard, and Maren read them the way she had read every loss of her life, ring by ring, outward.  The first ring said: there was never any magic in me.  The second said: I was afraid of the water.  The third, widening past the apple boughs, said: I went in because you had stopped looking at me.  The rings ran on into the dark shore and were gone. 

Maren stayed in the eye with her hand half eaten and the plant flowering white above her wrist.  She had found the last lost thing, and it was not a body or a ring.  It was the inside of a person, which no mirror carries.  So she gave the lake something to freeze.  She spoke into the water, plainly, the way a soldier reports: I closed my hand on you and called it strength.  I should have unbent it while you were warm.  The glass took the words and held them, a small curled knot sinking beside his.  Then the orchard slowed, one ring wider, and let her row out.
