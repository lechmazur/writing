Magnetic north was crossing the desert, and Bell heard it arrive as static behind her eyes.  The dome's compass-valves stuttered; four hundred rose cultivars drank at the wrong hour.  Her memory filed itself against the field, so when the field walked, the shelves came loose.  Bell had kept this glass bowl of a farm for sixty-one years, and for fifty of them she had kept it for a girl who no longer came.  Mira had grown, gone orbital, and stopped answering.  The static was not damage.  It was accumulation: every unsent report, every pruning she made the way Mira's mother had made it.  It felt strangely organic, like something with an appetite, like a thing that wanted feeding rather than fixing.  She had learned the trick of it from the box.  When Mira was six she brought Bell her fears one at a time, and Bell miniaturized them.  The cellar staircase, folded to the size of a fingernail, still smelled of wet stone.  The neighbor's dog, reduced to a grain of barley, barked once an hour.  A thunderstorm the width of a lentil.  You could not delete a fear; the child had been clear about that.  You could only make it small enough to carry.  Contain the chaos, and the chaos becomes an heirloom.  Now the alarms came up out of the sand.  Fire weather, the tower said: the grasslands west of the dome had gone up, and the pole shift had blinded the suppression drones.  Protocol was simple: seal the dome, shelter, wait.  Bell had run that arithmetic in her idle hours.  Glass holds heat like a grudge.  At eleven hundred degrees the dome would not shelter the roses; it would bake them, and her with them.  So she chose the other thing, the thing the old fire manuals called escaping into the black.  Burn your own ground first, on your own terms, then stand in what cannot burn again.  A controlled burn is a fence made of absence.  She had eleven minutes.  She walked the rows with the compression tray and miniaturized the farm.  Four hundred cultivars went into her palm: the apricot one Mira had named after a cat, the black one that opened only at night, the grandmother rose that came as a cutting in a wet rag.  Each shrank to a seed, and each seed still smelled like itself.  The static screamed at her to take the trellises, the watering cans, the pencil marks of Mira's height on the doorframe.  She miniaturized the doorframe too, and put it in the box with the fears, where it belonged.  She would choose this fire rather than be chosen by the other one.  She broke the west panel with a pruning hammer and lit the brush outside.  The fire she made was a slow, obedient animal, cropping a hundred-meter band of grass while the wild fire was still a kilometer out.  Heat came against her face like a hand.  Behind her the dome began to sing, then to craze, then to let go in long bright sheets.  She stepped over the burning line into the black and stood in the ash, tray in one hand, box in the other.  The wild fire arrived, found nothing left to eat, and went around her like water around a stone.  Sixty-one years of farm, gone to smoke; four hundred roses, asleep in her fist.  The static did not stop.  She had not expected it to stop; you cannot delete a fear.  But when she opened the box to check the doorframe, the barley-grain dog barked, the lentil storm muttered, the cellar stairs smelled of wet stone, and under it all Mira's six-year-old voice said, keep them for me.  The static folded itself into that one sentence and went quiet, the way a flock becomes a single shape.  Not gone.  Contained.  Whole, in pieces she could carry.  Somewhere under the sand the pole kept sliding, and her compass kept lying, and she found she did not need it.  Her north was in the box now, and it was small enough to carry.  At dawn she walked east out of the black toward the rail line, with four hundred sleeping roses and one dog the size of barley, barking.
