The fog had been eating the orchard district for three centuries, and it was still chewing.  Sefa made mirrors in the shell of a cider house, because a mirror is the one thing fog cannot swallow.  It hands the fog back to itself.  She kept forty pounds of orchard stone in her coat pockets and a rope from her belt to the doorpost.  Firmly grounded, the old glaziers called that, and Sefa had never once laughed at the phrase.  People who wandered light came back as weather. 

On the wall hung the astronomer's broken sextant.  Teodora had carried it up through the fog for eleven years, hunting a horizon she could measure.  A sextant does one thing: it brings a star down until it stands on the ground, and erases the distance between them.  Then the arc snapped on the ice, the silver flaked off both mirrors, and Teodora went out to fix her angles and did not come home. 

For a year after, Sefa dreamed the orchard whole.  Bloom on every wrecked branch, ladders, wet grass, Teodora naming stars with her mouth full of petals.  Then the dream stopped coming.  It had run out into the fog, the way dreams do when the sleeper stops being a place they can live.  Sefa heard it some nights, ringing far off like a spoon against a glass mountain.  A runaway dream thins every night it stays loose, until it is only fog with a habit.  She meant to catch it before it forgot her. 

You cannot net a dream; you can only build the room it recognizes.  So she would recreate the orchard, branch for branch, in the one material the fog respects.  She had until the shimmer of twilight frost, that half hour when the cold comes down and the fog sets into ranges of glass, blue and brittle and ringing, before the wind could rise and shake it down. 

She re-silvered the sextant's two mirrors first, tin and mercury, her breath slow over the hot glass.  Then she went out along the rows with a lantern and the sextant, and she breathed.  Every glazier knows the trick: glass takes the shape of the breath that works it, and two breaths in time become one shape.  The fog breathed too, in long swells against the ruined walls.  Sefa counted, found the swell, and matched it, in when it came in, out when it went out.  Where two breaths keep the same time, the boundary between them stops being a place. 

The frost came.  It came along her exhale and climbed it, and where she breathed out an orchard, an orchard stood: glass trunks, glass ladders, ten thousand blossoms thin as fingernail parings, chiming.  She kept her boots in the frozen grass and the rope taut behind her, forty pounds of stone at her hips, because a woman who erases a boundary must know exactly where she is standing. 

The dream came up the rows like a deer.  It had gone thin; she could see the fog through its petals.  It stopped at the glass ladder it remembered, and it did not know her, and Sefa understood that she had built a house with no one in it. 

So she lifted the sextant, its snapped arc held together in her fist, and looked through both mirrors at once.  In one glass hung the dream; in the other hung her own face, fog-gray, breathing.  She moved the index arm by hand, breathing, until the two images sat in the same place: the dream standing where she stood, her mouth full of its petals.  Zero degrees; no distance left to measure.  The dream knew her then.  It came into her chest the way warm air comes into a cold room, and the glass orchard rang once, all together, a sound like a mountain of glass deciding to be a bell. 

Then the wind rose and shook the rows down into powder.  Sefa hauled herself home along the rope, hand over hand, stones dragging, firmly grounded, laughing, full. 

She hangs the sextant by the door now, arc mended, mirrors bright, set at zero.  The fog still eats the district; the orchard is still ruin; nothing out there was healed.  But she sleeps, and the bloom comes back every night, and Teodora names the stars in it, and Sefa does not let go of the ground.
