The under-town only wandered during the fallow period, when the fields above lay unsown and nothing was asked of the ground.  Vesna had learned this at nine, the autumn her mother walked into a root cellar that had decided to be somewhere else.  The guild called it spatial mutiny: rooms refusing their coordinates, corridors slipping their moorings, the whole buried plan of the town turning like a restless sleeper.  Vesna became an explorer anyway, which surprised everyone who knew she could not breathe in a closed room.  She was prudently adventurous, with three ropes, chalk at both wrists, a lamp and a spare, and one unbroken rule: never go deeper than the last place you can still see sky.  That rule left her exactly one doorway: the collapsed rotunda where six corridors met beneath a hole in the world, a shadowed nexus under starlight.  Her mother's drafting compass came with her, brass legs and a steel point hard enough to score stone.  She wanted the vital key the guild only whispered about: the founders' pivot pin, lost to time, the one fixed thing that had once told every room where it stood.  Without it the plan had no legend, no still point, and her mother was somewhere in the wandering.  The guild's order was simple: find the socket, seat a new pin, nail the under-town shut forever.  Vesna had copied the order into her notebook and underlined nothing.  She went down on the third fallow night, when Teodor climbed to the empty church above the shaft and played the organ for nobody, as he had every autumn since his wife died.  Sound was the only surveyor the mutiny could not fool.  Through the reverberation of a lonely church organ, the low pipes rolling down the shaft like slow water, Vesna read the corridors: a room that shifted returned a sour, sliding echo, while a room holding true rang clean.  Better, the reverberation transfixed the mutiny, holding each corridor still for as long as the chord survived, which was eleven seconds if Teodor leaned on the pedal.  Eleven seconds was a great deal to a woman who counted her breaths.  She worked in those windows, chalk in her teeth, compass in her fist, scratching arcs across the rotunda floor from every corridor mouth.  Five arcs argued with each other as the stone drifted under her knees.  Once a corridor leaned in and her throat shut like a latch; she looked up at the torn hole of sky, counted four breaths, and waited for Teodor's next chord to push the stone back.  The sixth arc cut clean and did not move when the note died.  At its center, under a hand's width of grit, lay a socket the exact gauge of her compass point.  The guild's drawings had burned; her mother had copied the pin as a student, ground her compass point to match it, and then gone into the cellar to check.  Vesna knelt in the starlight with her heart slamming and the sky cold on her neck, and understood what the guild wanted her to bury.  A seated pin would fix every room where it happened to be standing tonight, including whichever room held her mother, three streets or thirty years away.  Order forever, and no way home.  Above her the organ began again, some old tune Teodor played badly on purpose because his wife had liked to laugh at him.  She thought of the guild's clean word, forever, and of her mother's handwriting in the margin of a blueprint: measure twice, but leave room.  So she did the prudent, disobedient thing.  She unscrewed the steel point from her mother's compass and seated it in the socket only as far as the first thread, pinning the nexus, the meeting place, the one room every wandering corridor must eventually pass through.  The six mouths steadied; beyond them she could hear the under-town still turning, free and unforgiven, doors opening onto doors.  A crossroads that stays put, she wrote in her notebook, is a place where lost things can arrive.  She climbed out under the stars and told the guild the socket was stripped, which was almost true.  Then she sat on the church steps beside Teodor until the last chord rolled away, and drew circles in the dust with her finger, because a compass without its point can only guess.  She was a woman who could not bear closed rooms, and she had left one open.  Somewhere below, in the fallow dark, a corridor came around again toward the light.
