The town forgot its library so completely that it paved a schoolyard over the roof, leaving one ground-level window to look up at ankles and sky.  Underneath, Ansa kept the shards.  The stacks smelled of cold tea and silver polish, and the shelves held no books, only wrapped glass, ten thousand reflections alphabetized by grief.  Every exile began with a mirror broken across a threshold and ended here, where the pieces of a person's reflection lay shelved, labeled, and reversible.  Anyone could undo an exile: join every shard in the correct order, then annotate the register before the hush ended.  In forty years, three people had tried. 

Wren came down the coal chute with her mother's teacup in a dishcloth, three years unwashed.  Her mother had told the water council what was in the reservoir, and the council had answered with a hammer.  "You're late," Ansa said, "and you're shaking, and I don't care about either."  The box was already on the table, labeled, the lid off. 

The exiled were given tea at the threshold, and the leaves dried into the geometry of the break, vein for vein, so that a cup was a map.  Ansa tilted it to the lamp and traced the angles with a needle.  "Nine shards."  "Long fracture, then a fork."  "She drank it fast; the leaves are crowded, which means she was afraid, which means nothing."  Wren said her mother had never been afraid of anything.  "Then she was cold," Ansa said.  "Hold the lamp." 

They worked through the afternoon while the swing above them creaked empty.  The hush was the only hour a join would hold: that slack minute after the children went inside, when the empty swing still drifted on a breeze nobody was pushing, still moving with nobody on it.  When it stopped, the seam closed. 

Wren fitted shards until her fingertips split, and Ansa let her.  "Don't bleed on the silver."  "It hurts."  "Yes," Ansa said.  "Keep going."  Nobody had ever told Wren she could bear something; Ansa simply arranged the work so that she did.  Between joins she made Wren annotate: name, shard number, angle, hour, weather, what her hands had felt.  "The register isn't for the town," Ansa said.  "It's for the next girl down that chute." 

Eight shards went together, and the glass showed the room, and not her mother.  "The ninth is the witness's," Ansa said, and took a small square from her apron.  Inside it lay an afternoon shadow caught in glass: Wren at six, stretched long across the schoolyard boards, the shape of a child waiting for someone who did not come home that day.  "The mirror kept it," Ansa said.  "You were standing in the glass when they broke it."  "I archive what the glass keeps." 

Wren asked why she didn't use her own.  "I spent mine on my brother, who came back and never thanked me, and I would spend it again."  "Yours is still yours."  "Set it, write your name beside it, and she comes back."  "And if the order is wrong?"  "Then you'll have a scar, a cup, and tomorrow." 

Above them the swing's arc shrank, the chains going quiet by degrees.  Ansa began walking backwards down the aisle, heel then toe, counting under her breath, the way she moved in the narrow stacks where turning your back on loose glass could cost you your reflection.  Wren had seen it a hundred times and had always called it an old woman's habit.  Now she knew: Ansa was frightened, and could not stop, and would not say so.  "Say something," Wren said.  "Set the shadow." 

Wren set it, and the glass took her afternoon and gave back her mother's face, mid-sentence, three years late and impatient, saying something about the rain.  She annotated the register in a hand that shook: name, hour, weather, what her hands had felt.  The swing stopped.  Out in the yard, a woman's shadow crossed the boards, walking home.  Wren looked down and found the floor took nothing from her, no shape at all, and she stood there anyway, light as a debt paid.  Ansa, still backing away, wrote two words in the apprentice column and turned the ledger so Wren could read them.  "Adequate."  "Thursday."
