The laboratory of lost sounds kept no clocks.  It kept the last ring of a demolished bell, the breath of a dog dead in 1974, and a piece of amber with a midge inside, wings unbroken.  Sounds went there to be held, not to be healed.  The place had the hush of a field after a meteor: something enormous had come down, and the air had not decided what to do.  Ilse came because a letter told her to, and because the letter was right about her. 

She had found it in October, under the horsehair stuffing of a secondhand nursing chair.  The chair was meant for the baby; the baby had stopped at four months, in the night, in the next room.  Ilse was a constellation tattooist: she dotted star maps into forearms and shoulders, ten thousand small punctures, each one placed by patience.  That night she had been awake thirty hours finishing a client's sky, and the monitor had translated the cry for her, as it always did: tired, settling.  She had believed the machine because she wanted to. 

Beneath the horsehair lay eleven letters, handwritten in 1911 by a woman named Ada to her sister, cataloguing her infant's cries.  "The hunger cry climbs."  "The pain cry waits first, a flat little silence, and then arrives."  Ada had lost that baby too, and wrote it all down so someone could learn her ear.  The twelfth letter was in different ink, undated, and gave its directions as a star map, which she of all people could read.  It said: come to the laboratory of lost sounds when time folds in on itself; you will know the hour, because you will hear her from the next room; bring the chip. 

The keeper met her at the door of a converted dairy and did not ask her name.  He socketed the chip she had brought, and its firmware began scrolling translations of a cry three years old: cold, tired, hungry, come, cold.  "It is not broken," the keeper said.  "It is haunted; it holds a sound with nowhere to put it, so it keeps asking."  Sounds need a tether: two anchors and a line between them, or they wander. 

He set the amber under the lamp.  Forty million years ago a midge beat its wings, and the resin took the song and held it, and the song was still there, whole, unafraid.  The tether needed one map plotted twice, once in the resin, once in living skin, by a hand that does not tremble.  That was why the letter had found her, in the stuffing of a chair she had bought for a child.  Ilse took up her needle. 

Then time folded.  The dairy was also Ada's kitchen in 1911, and also the hallway outside her daughter's door at three in the morning, one instant laid over itself like tracing paper.  The cry came through the chip, and Ilse heard what Ada had taught her to hear: the flat little silence before it.  Not tired.  Pain, and then a call, and the call meant come.  She had not come.  Guilt had been a dissonance in her for three years, one note held against every other note. 

She set the needle into the amber, dot by dot: the seven stars that had stood over the hospital the night her daughter was born.  Then she inked the same map into her own forearm, matching every point, drawing the lines that make separate fires into a single figure.  The scrolling slowed.  The cry crossed the line into the resin and lay down beside the midge's song, held, no longer asking.  The screen printed "held," and went dark. 

The fold opened out; the kitchen and the hallway slid away; the dairy was a dairy.  Two songs in the amber now, and a constellation pricked above them like a sky.  The keeper wrapped the resin in flannel and put it in her hands.  It was warm, the way a stone in a field is warm after something has fallen out of the sky.  At home she sat in the stripped chair, the amber against her forearm, and rocked.  The guilt was not gone; it was tethered, which is a different mercy.  The house held the hush that comes after a meteor: the whole sky listening, the ash settled, nothing left to brace for.  She rocked, and let the small old song keep her company, and did not need to be forgiven to be quiet.
