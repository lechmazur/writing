The canyon held its breath after midnight, and Sela sat against the cold rimrock, waiting for frost.  Above her the stars were thick and unbothered, the same stars that had watched her shed burn nine nights ago.  In her lap lay a tile of her own glass, no bigger than a hand, glowing the pale green of shallow water.  It was the last piece she had made before the attempt, and the only lamp she trusted now.  Slow light, Odric had called it: canyon sand, burnt bone, and a cooling so patient it bordered on prayer. 

Her hands were bandaged to the second knuckle, and the burn along her left side had taught her a new way of standing.  She was not brave about it.  She changed her grip, her stride, the hour she worked, and kept going, steadily adaptive, the way water keeps going around a stone. 

In her coat, folded in oilcloth, lay what the fire had left of Odric's testament.  Two thirds of a page, scorched brown at the edges, the ink gone rust-colored where the heat had licked it.  It gave her the kiln, the sand rights, the shed.  The middle lines were eaten away, and those were the lines that mattered, because Odric had been setting down something he was afraid to set down.  She had read the holes a hundred times: "To Sela, who understands that the light is in the," and then ash. 

The other thing in her pocket was a strip of paper two fingers wide, torn from a charcoal smuggler's ledger.  Columns of loads and nights in a cramped hand: eleven sacks, nine, twelve.  Then three nights with nothing written at all.  Then one line, a sum far too large for charcoal, and beside it the seal of the guild factor who bought every pane she made.  The man who dragged her out of the burning shed had pressed that strip into her hand and gone back into the smoke, and he had not come out.  She did not know his name.  She knew his handwriting, and she knew what the empty nights meant. 

Frost came an hour before dawn, and Sela walked the canyon floor with her tile held low.  Frost is a truthful witness: thick on untouched ground, thin over warmth, cracked along the line where a weight has been dragged.  She had learned to read it the winter she and Odric hid their firings from the tariff men.  Nine mornings had passed, but packed earth stays packed, and every frost since had redrawn the same map.  She read it backward now, retracing: a heel print feathered soft, then two long smears where a heavy sack had been hauled, the frost still splitting along its seams.  The ledger's pauses told her which nights to trust; the patterns in morning frost told her where those nights had gone. 

The trail left the shed and climbed the wash toward the old charcoal pits, and at the third pit the frost thinned to a gray whisper.  The coals underneath were still alive.  She dug with a shard of kiln shelf, because her hands could not close on a shovel.  A hand's depth down, wrapped in sacking, lay the ledger itself, and folded inside its cover was the missing leaf of the testament. 

Odric had confessed in ink what the factor answered in fire: that the slow light left the canyon in charcoal sacks, untaxed and unrecorded, and that Sela's name was on none of it.  The tally keeper had hidden the page, then come back for her, and the three blank nights were the nights he spent deciding.  She would set his mark, the cramped hand of his columns, into a pane, since she had no name to cut. 

Sela sat in the warm pit and read the leaf against her glowing tile while the stars went out one by one.  The leaf's last line was whole: "the light is in the waiting, and the waiting is yours to keep." 

Below the rimrock the town was waking, and at noon the factor's wagon would climb the road to ask, kindly, when she might be well enough to work.  She could meet him with the page, or with empty hands and let him talk first.  Glass takes nothing from the furnace but shape; it earns its light afterward, in the long dark hours when nothing seems to be happening.  She wrapped the leaf and the strip together, banked the pit, and went down to light a slow fire and to wait, purposefully, in the pause.
