The cobalt terrace kept the day's heat long after the sun went under, and my mother sat in it with her hands open on her knees.  Dusk came up the tiles like water.  She asked me again whether the doctor had called, and I said yes, and she nodded, and then there was the quiet I had spent eleven years learning to recognize. 

In my trade we call it a sieved hush.  It is the silence left when a memory has been lifted out and the hole has been sanded smooth: no grit, no grain, nothing for grief to catch on.  I am a genealogist, and no board has ever licensed me.  For money, in rooms behind the fish market, I build people better ancestors and kinder childhoods.  A pedigree on old paper, a christening spoon, a photograph with the right shadows, and the mind does the rest; it manufactures false memories the way a wound manufactures scar. 

Eleven years ago my mother paid me in cash to take my sister out of her.  The work took the payment from her mind too, which she had wanted, and which I had let her want.  My fee was the hand off my sister's watch, a small brass finger that had pointed at the minute of her drowning and now pointed at nothing.  I keep it in my waistcoat pocket, where it weighs less than a coin and more than the house. 

What I take from a client I carve into driftwood and throw into the sea.  That is the old rite of this coast, and it is also the only honest ledger I keep.  The water is a sieve.  Light things come back, the small lies, the softened birthdays, and they wash up on the forgotten shore below the terrace in a windrow of pale wood.  Heavy things stay down.  Every dusk I go down and sift the carvings, thumbing salt out of the grooves, reading names the tide has half eaten, trying to quell the hush in myself with the noise of other people's losses.  I am looking for one piece: my sister's name, cut deep, gone eleven years.  It has never come back, and I have told myself that this means the sea is keeping it safe.  But the men who taught me the rite said the water returns whatever has been spoken aloud, and holds whatever has not. 

For eleven years we have lived under the weight of unspoken words, my mother and I, and she has carried a load she cannot name.  What the work left in her is fog, and the coast road, and a gate of water closing over a car she believes was empty.  She does not ask who was in it, because I never said, and because I sold her a road that did it alone.  I wanted to know the weight of that withheld truth before I handed it to a dying woman.  That is what the sifting was for: to find the wood, hold it, learn whether it would sink her or hold her up. 

Tonight the windrow gave me a stranger's forged grandfather and a boy's dog and nothing of mine.  So I climbed the steps to the cobalt terrace, and the tiles were still warm, and I sat down where she could see my face.  I said, I was driving.  I said her name, which I had not said in that house since the funeral, and I said the water came in fast, and I said I got out. 

My mother did not forgive me, and she did not remember, not properly, not the way the false ones remember.  But she made a sound I had never heard from her, a low grinding sound, and the sieved hush broke, and grief found something to catch on.  It was terrible, and it was hers, and I had given it back. 

In the morning the carving lay in the windrow, waterlogged, black, heavy as a stone in both hands.  It had not been waiting for me to find it; it had been waiting for me to say it.  In the evening I carried it up to the terrace and put it in her lap, with the little brass hand laid on top, pointing at nothing.  She held them both, and the dusk came up the tiles like water, and neither of us asked for anything kinder.
