The clouds came up the mountain every afternoon like sheep returning to a fold, and Vera watched them from the mouth of the grotto.  Below them lay the valley, hidden, and the road where her daughter had died.  Above them lay her seven terraces of vines and the shaded cave where she had nursed the dying for thirty-one years.  The sun found the grotto's mouth for nine minutes a day; the rest was cool blue shade, good for fever, good for last things. 

Anton arrived in April, carried up by two nephews and a mule.  The nephews stood at the gate waiting to be judged, and she told them to bring him in before they had finished asking, because a man who cannot breathe does not need a debate.  He was the driver: brakes gone, curve taken wide, a child on the verge gathering fennel.  In March she had walked down through the cloud to his door and forgiven him, kindly, without letting go of the doorframe.  Forgiveness had been a decision, like pruning; she made it once and did not reopen it.  What she could not decide was how to be content. 

Her patients all arrived at it near the end, a quietness she had seen a hundred times, and she wanted to know where it came from, the way one wants to know where a spring rises.  She had followed the grotto water back through the limestone to a seep in the dark, and found only more water, coming from nowhere she could stand. 

For the dying she baked apricot pies, and she scalloped the edges with her grandmother's silver hairpin, the way her grandmother had, the way her daughter had liked to do, standing on a stool.  The pin was two hundred years old, worn thin at the tips, and it had held up the hair of every woman who had kept this grotto.  She baked one for Anton on a Thursday and did not tell him whose hands had first pressed that pattern into the crust.  Withholding it felt like the last stone in her pocket, and she was tired of stones. 

When she could not settle a thing, she climbed.  Since girlhood she had climbed the black pine above the grotto, hand over hand into the resin and the wind, because her thoughts came clearer off the ground.  That evening she went up with flour still on her wrists.  From the high fork she could see the whole cloud sea, and she watched it climb.  It rose out of the valley.  Out of the river her daughter had been christened in, out of the road, out of the fennel verge, out of Anton's fields; the sun lifted all of it, and the mountain caught it, and it fell as rain into the terraces, and ran through the limestone into her spring, and went down again. 

There was no source.  There was only the going around: the vines were one vine, cut and struck and cut again for three centuries, and the dead she had laid in the terraces were in the wine she poured for the dying, and the quietness they reached was not a place upstream but the wheel itself, turning them home.  Her daughter was in it too, in the rain and the fennel and the flour, and so was the hatred she had set down in March, and so, eventually, would she be. 

She came down and told Anton about Ilse.  She told him about the fennel, and the stool by the pastry board, and the hairpin, and he wept, and she let him, and then she fed him.  He died in August, in the hush, with the bees working the vines outside and no one shouting anywhere.  She buried him in the seventh terrace, under the oldest vine, and in the spring she took cuttings from it, as always. 

The girl who came to learn the work, Mira, was fifteen and clumsy and stayed.  On her first morning Vera washed the hairpin, dried it on her sleeve, and pinned up the girl's hair with it, then showed her the scalloped edge, pressed with a fork, because the pin had other work now. 

Vera still climbs the pine when a thing will not settle, but she does not go up looking for the source anymore.  She goes up to watch the valley breathe itself into the sky, and to let the hush ripen her, row by row.
