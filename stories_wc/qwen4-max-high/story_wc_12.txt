Snow had packed the chapel windows to the sills, and every sound arrived wrapped in wool.  Father Ambrose preferred it; muffled was not the same as unclear.  In the vestry stair hung the anchor, a lead plumb bob on brass wire, dropped nine feet down a shaft cut to bedrock.  The chapel had been built around that wire.  While the bob hung free of the walls, the room kept the same size in every direction, and whatever pressed in from the thin places had to knock.  Ilse had not knocked; nothing had needed to.  She sat in the front pew, still as poured glass, her painter's palette on the floor beside her.  She had been gilding the nine choirs on the ceiling, and for a week she had mixed every pigment into one gray.  One color, spread evenly, no edges anywhere.  That was how the thing announced itself, not with horns but with agreement. 

Ambrose set his loop pedal on the altar step and thumbed the switch.  The diocese called him the loop pedal priest and had twice asked him, politely, to stop.  Possession is a sentence that wants to finish.  Between its first borrowed word and its last, the person underneath is still legible, and Ambrose had learned to work only inside that gap.  Twenty minutes, perhaps.  He did not command; commands confirm the assumption that a soul is a room with one door.  He asked, and he shaped the asking, because nothing that lives in language can refuse a properly built question. 

Into the pedal he spoke anaphora, the same opening stacked four times: Who told you she was empty?  The loop caught it, returned it, and his own voice came back a half step lower, the way old tape does.  Ilse's mouth opened without her.  "Nobody tells us," she said, in a voice like a hand smoothing a sheet.  "Emptiness is simply available."  There was the first assumption, laid under everything like a joist. 

Ambrose layered a chiasmus over the anaphora: We do not fill what is silent; what is silent fills us.  Her head tilted a fraction, the way a lock tilts toward a wrong key.  He let the loops run and gave them what he had spent thirty years learning to give: rests.  Other priests decorated with candles, with bells, with gold; Ambrose decorated with silence, cutting a bar of it into every fourth measure so the phrase never closed.  The thing could not leave a gap alone.  It poured into the rests, explaining, insisting, naming its rights, and each time it spoke it spent itself on the empty air of a bar it had mistaken for an invitation.  It told him, gently, that he had chosen silence at nineteen because he could not bear to be interrupted.  It was not wrong.  "True," he said, and let the loop carry that too, because a man who hides his own assumptions cannot go looking for anyone else's. 

Ambrose knelt and took the palette.  He scraped the gray with his thumbnail and held it under the votive light, where the smear broke into its crowd: madder, bone black, ochre, a green like a pond in August.  "You assumed one color meant one thing," he said into the microphone, and the loop took it and kept it.  "You assumed her stillness was consent." 

Then he stopped feeding the pedal and let the last rest stand open, unfilled, four seconds wide.  Into that width he dropped his question, a plumb line down the well of her quiet: What is underneath you?  No answer came.  He waited, paying the wire out, and her silence took the whole length of it and asked for more.  Ilse's stillness came apart then, not shattered but unraveled, thread by thread: a shoulder dropped, a breath caught, one hand left the other and crept toward the palette.  "It's not gray," she said, in her own voice, thin and unmistakable.  "No," Ambrose said.  "It never was." 

He did not close the loop.  He left the bar of silence standing in it, and the pedal turned around that hole, and the thing would keep leaning toward the gap it could not resist, and leaning is not finishing.  In the vestry stair he checked the anchor: the bob swung a little, off true, brushing nothing, settling nowhere.  The sentence had not finished.  Snow pressed the windows, the wool held, and inside the muffle every word was clear.
