The reading room went sepia in the first yawn of evening, when the lamps came on and the photographs of four generations of Crays browned into the walls.  Nell Cray was the last of them, and she had come to end the line.  For ninety years the city had paid her family to remember for it: the Cray Trust, keepers of the inquiry cylinders, the voices of the dead filed in wax.  Her great-grandfather Amos had been the sworn transcriber at the Kestrel Mill hearings, where eleven women swore the owners had bolted the doors before the fire.  His transcript said otherwise, the owners kept their charter, and the Crays kept everything after that.  Golden shadows pooled in the corners of the room like something poured and left to settle.  She had come to uncover the truth Amos had hidden in plain sound.  Her only instrument was the gramophone, a hand-driven thing with no motor, and its crank handle, worn to the shape of her grandfather's thumb.  Cylinder forty-one was labeled KESTREL: SUSTAINED TONE, CALIBRATION, and the catalogue called it a test of the horn.  Four minutes of one note, nothing else, and every archivist before her had shelved it again.  But her grandfather, who had lost his voice to a stroke at fifty, used to come in here at dusk and crank that cylinder himself.  He never sat.  He stood in the far corner by the radiator, one hand on the wall, eloquently silent, and he would not let her stand there with him.  She had thought it was grief.  She had thought a great many things were grief.  She let herself muse over the ledger, turning the handle slowly, and the hum rose into the golden air.  Amos had built this room.  The dimensions were in the family ledger, in his hand: twenty-two feet by fourteen, ceiling eleven.  A man does not record a room unless the room is part of the record.  Here was the bridge of intuition she had been afraid to build: her grandfather's silent corner at one end, a column of numbers at the other, and nothing between them but a guess she could stand on.  A sustained tone in a closed room makes standing waves.  The note bounces off the walls and meets itself, and where the two versions cancel there are nodes, small pockets of quiet that hang in the air like beads on a wire.  Only that pitch dies in them.  Anything hidden underneath it, at any other pitch, would still be there, waiting in the corner.  She cranked at seventy-eight revolutions, the way his hand had taught her hand, and she walked the room with her ear low.  The pitch lived in her wrist; if she faltered, the quiet would slide off its hook.  The note swelled and thinned, swelled and thinned, a corridor of sound with rooms of silence off it.  By the radiator the tone went out like a blown candle.  And under it, thin as thread, a woman was saying names.  She held the crank steady while her arm burned.  The woman said the doors were bolted, said the key was gone from its hook, said Mr. Cray had asked her to say it slower for the horn.  Then Amos, close to the mouthpiece, patient as a clerk: again, and leave out the doors.  No key, no cipher: she had gotten in through standing waves.  He had not been able to burn it.  Instead he had cut a second thread into the wax, one loud note laid over her voice like a sheet over a face, and labeled it calibration.  And her grandfather, who could not say it, had found the one corner where the sheet lifted, and stood there evening after evening, letting his silence do the pointing.  That was the whole inheritance: a corner, a crank, a hush.  Nell let the crank come to rest.  The room went on being sepia, the photographs went on being golden and brown, and the eleven women went on being liars in every book the city owned.  Her aunts had already named her, the way families name coming weather: the dynasty ender.  In the morning she would carry cylinder forty-one to the newspaper and hand over the ledger with the room's dimensions in it, and the Cray Trust would be finished, and so would the Crays.  Somebody had to be the last one.  She put the crank handle in her coat pocket, where it would be warm, and stood a while in her grandfather's corner, eloquently silent, listening to nothing at all.
