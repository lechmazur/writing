They brought the substitute body to Marta because she was the only one in the valley who touched the dead without praying.  It had come out of the millrace at dawn, wrapped in weed, wearing Wren's face.  Marta laid it on her slab and worked the jaw, the elbow, the small hinge of the knee.  Bone setting was her trade, thirty years of it, and her hands knew what a child's arm should weigh.  This one weighed nothing, and the joints were withy, and where the marrow should have been there was dry pith.  Behind her, Wren's mother made a sound like a door.  "Burn the maze," said the miller. "Burn every hedge of it and the thing inside."  Marta did not believe in the fair folk, and she had never once called a grieving woman a fool for believing.  Doubt, in her experience, was a kindness you could hand people along with the bandage.  She had been keeping a tooth in her apron pocket for nine days.  Wren had pulled it loose in the garden and pressed it into Marta's palm, proud as a jeweler.  It had no root.  It was cold, and it stayed cold, and held to the lamp it showed a second tooth growing inside it, and a third inside that.  Marta was a skeptic, which is not a person with no gaps, but a person who refuses to stuff the gaps with straw.  She left the tooth where it was: a cleft in her certainty, open, aired, unfilled.  It was how she set bones.  You align the break and then you leave a space in the splint, because the limb will swell, and a binding with no give will kill what it means to hold.  On her ceiling she had painted every mistake she had ever made as a star: the collarbone she shortened, the fever she called teething, the boy she sent home walking.  Then she had drawn lines between them until they made figures, a Heron, a Lantern, a Girl Running, so that an error could be steered by instead of merely suffered.  That was her whole ambition: to turn mistakes into constellations.  She went into the maze at dusk with her satchel and a lantern, before the men could come with theirs.  Eleven turns, the loose paving slab, the gap in the yew that only a thin woman could argue with.  Inside, the secret garden the old physician had planted and the parish had forgotten: comfrey, willow, valerian, and a pear tree trained flat against the wall like a hand.  Marta had kept it weeded for thirty years and improved it without permission, grafting new stock onto old, boiling her linen, casting splints in plaster instead of bark.  She loved the old ways the way you love a grandmother you intend to correct.  Under the pear tree, something small was holding its wrist.  "Are you going to drown me?" it asked, in Wren's voice, with the mildness of a child who had already counted the exits.  Shrewdly innocent, Marta thought; the question was a splint, and she was the limb.  "I'm going to look at your arm," she said.  The radius was greenstick-broken, the way a child's breaks when it drops out of a tree or out of a life.  It let her work.  "I put the body in the water so they would look at the river and not at me," it said.  "Where is Wren?"  "I don't know," it said, and that was the first honest thing anyone had given her all day.  Marta bound the wrist and left the gap, two fingers of room for the swelling, for the part she could not predict.  The creature leaned against her knee, yearning so openly it was almost a craft: it wanted to be kept, to be given a chore and a name and a place at the ugly end of the table.  Marta had yearned for exactly that, once, at nine, in a house where nobody set anything.  She forgave it, which was not the same as absolving it, and not the same as believing.  At the maze mouth she told the men the truth she had: the body was withy and pith, the child was missing, and she was keeping this one.  That night she nailed the cold tooth to the ceiling, far from the others, in the empty quarter where nothing had ever gone wrong yet.  A first star, unfixed, waiting for lines.  She named it Aster, after the late flowers that come back whether or not you deserve them.  Below it the girl with two teeth inside her tooth slept, and Marta left the space.
