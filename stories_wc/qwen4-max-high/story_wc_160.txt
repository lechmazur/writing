The amphitheater breathed at dawn and again at dusk, and Vessa timed her work to its lungs.  Ash-light fell through the vents in the crater wall and lay in coins across the broken tiers, so that everything she did that morning was dappled, including her certainty.  She was the floodgate machinist, the third since the town climbed into the dormant volcano, and she had inherited a lake above three hundred houses and a wall of stone everyone agreed was crumbling and no one agreed about. 

The vine growers wanted the crater held full for the dry months.  The low town wanted it emptied, because water weight and old stone make a poor marriage.  The players wanted the amphitheater dry and swept for the summer plays, as their grandmothers had swept it.  Three honest goods, and Vessa had spent two years trying to make them share one gate. 

Her teacher Ilario had left her one sealed envelope and one antique surveyor's chain, sixty-six feet of iron links with brass tags at every ten.  The town advocate opened the envelope at the assembly and read out a table of numbers, dry as pumice, and both factions heard in it a verdict for the other side.  Ilario had been slyly heartfelt in life, praising a man's boots while mending his roof for nothing, and the table read like that joke with the roof left out. 

She carried the empty envelope up to the top tier and studied the flap.  Glue keeps records.  Under the last sealing lay six older ones, each a different recipe: fish glue from the drought year, apothecary gum, roofer's pitch, and every layer had lifted a mirror of the ink beneath it.  She read the ghosts backward through a wet lens, and the words Ilario had scraped off the page rose out of the adhesive: the widow at the low house, the school cistern, the hour the vines drink, do not let them watch me choose.  She felt her hands tremble, then felt the chain tremble in answer, link calling to link down the stone. 

The numbers were not gate heights.  They were links, counted on Ilario's chain, and hours.  She stretched the chain from the datum spike along the lowest tier and found the brass tag at forty lying in a worn notch, and another notch at seventy.  The amphitheater had been built as a ladder for water: a theatre in the dry season, a stepped spillway in the wet, the seats breaking the fall so the old wall never took the whole weight at once.  That was the compassion he had encrypted, because saying it plainly would have cost him the players' love and the growers' trust, and he wanted both to keep their pride. 

All morning the building inhaled, the vents drawing cool air down while the lake rose a finger's width with the night's rain.  At dusk it would exhale, warm air pushing out through the same vents, and that was when the stone loosened and a gate could be opened without a shudder.  She had one breath to set the pins. 

She set them at forty links and seventy, timed the release to the hour the vines drink, cut a small channel toward the school cistern, and gave the widow's low house the first hour, when the water runs cleanest.  When the amphitheater sighed, the lake came down the tiers in a slow white staircase, and the town stood in the dry seats above and watched the flood perform.  The players would hate the wet stone for a week and love the acoustics afterward.  The growers would grumble at the hour and drink at it.  The low town would sleep, which was the whole point, and never know it had been chosen for. 

Vessa coiled the chain, told the advocate she would charge admission next time, and did not trust her voice to say anything kinder.  Light came through the vents in coins, and the tiers lay bright and ruined and holding.  She was sure in patches, which was the only way she had ever been sure, and it was enough to open a gate.
