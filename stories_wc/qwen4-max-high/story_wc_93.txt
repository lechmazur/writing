Since friction left the world, nothing on the tidal bluff stays where you set it.  Nets slide off the rocks, knots loosen into rumor, and the sea climbs the cliff each night without ever wearing it down.  The village calls me elusive because I am never where they look.  I am the eel herder, and I have already had every conversation they intend to have with me.  That is the curse: foresight without surprise, a life read aloud before it is lived.  So I keep to the bluff, under the aurora, where the light is not ours.  It falls green and patient, like something lent by a neighbor who will eventually ask for it back.  We have lived in a borrowed dawn for eleven years.  Our sun collapsed; the dust of it still falls, heavy as grief, and the sky glows on credit.  I gather that collapsed star dust in a jar and mix it into clay.  Weight is the only argument left that the world will listen to.  With it I ballast the rocks I paint, and they hold their places in the tidal channel.  Then I lay them out in sequences: two, three, five, seven, eleven, gaps widening like breath.  The eels come up with the tide and read the numbers with their whole bodies.  Primes turn them left; the Fibonacci spacing walks them up the channel into the holding pool.  This is how I harness a run that no net can hold.  The village eats because arithmetic is the last thing with any grip.  But I do not paint rocks to feed them.  I want to understand the sacred patterns, why a number should mean anything to a creature with no hands and no gods.  My mother wanted the same, and she died in the middle of a sequence.  The tide took her while she was placing the ninth stone, and the eight behind her still glowed.  I promised her I would finish the count.  A promise is a strange thing after friction disappears: nothing wears it away.  It slides forward with you, untouched, forever.  Eleven years I have carried it up this bluff, and it has not thinned by a grain.  I have seen tonight already, the way I have seen all my nights.  I know the girl who will climb up to ask me for a fish, and the words she will use, and how I will not answer.  I know the tide will flood the channel to its highest mark in eleven years.  But at the moment my hand leaves the ninth stone, my foresight ends in a white silence.  Every version of tonight stops there, like a road that has not been paid for.  I saw the placing, never the stone; my sight stopped exactly there.  The girl comes anyway, hauling herself up the chain of weighted posts I set for her years ago.  She asks for a fish, exactly as I heard her ask.  In her hands is a stone, painted with nine bands, heavy with the dust of our dead sun.  She dove for it in the slack water, where nothing tumbles anymore, only drifts.  Eleven years the ninth stone has waited on the seabed, keeping its number.  I take it, and my hands know the weight my mother mixed.  I set it at the end of her spiral, nine bands facing the sea.  The tide turns, and the eels rise, and they read the whole figure at once.  They do not turn left into the pool.  They pour down the spiral instead, stone to stone, and the green light runs off their backs like water.  The sky empties into them and goes out.  The sacred pattern was never a way to take; it was the shape of giving back, a debt counted in primes.  My mother had been trying to pay it, and I had been eating the interest.  In the dark the girl asks what happens now, and for the first time in my life I do not know.  The curse was only the loan, showing me its own terms over and over.  I tell her I will teach her the count, and the words surprise me on the way out.  The village will be hungry tonight, and I will tell them why.  We sit on the bluff with the empty jar and the spent stones, and we wait.  Whatever light comes now will not be borrowed.
