The rain had beaded the skylight again, and under it the loft smelled of wet slate and lampblack.  Nadia was a cartomancer, and she ground her own onyx ink at the long table, three drops of water at a time.  Her grandmother had taught her the recipe, and the ink never faded, which was the point.  A reading that could not be kept was only entertainment. 

Since the cryptocurrency crashes, the city had sent her a new kind of client.  They did not ask about love.  They came with screenshots of dead coins and asked when the number would come back, as if she were an oracle with a customer service desk. 

Teo arrived on a Thursday, soaked, holding his laptop like a drowned animal.  "I want to know what happens next," he said. 

"Wrong direction," Nadia said.  She cleared the table and unrolled her grandmother's measuring tape, brass end worn smooth, the numbers softened by forty years of thumbs.  Her grandmother had been a seamstress who read cards on Sundays, and the tape had outlived every dress she ever made. 

"I don't predict," she said.  "I reverse engineer."  "You give me the ending, and I work backward to the choices that made it inevitable." 

Teo laughed, then stopped when she did not. 

She laid the first card at the tape's zero: the day he bought.  Then she began multiplying paths.  Each decision he described forked into the decision he had refused, and each of those forked again, and she wrote every branch on a blank card in onyx ink.  When he said he doubled down in March, she wrote the refusals beside it: sell, tell his sister, sleep.  Then she measured how far each would have carried him.  Multiplying paths was useless without scale, so the tape governed everything: one centimeter per week, one hand's width per doubling of conviction.  Otherwise the map became noise, and noise was what he had already paid for. 

By midnight the floor was a black delta of cards, and rain ticked the skylight above it like a metronome.  Teo crawled along the branches, asking not when but why at every fork.  Nadia was not excited by any of it; she was steadily curious, the way a surveyor is curious about a hill that keeps moving. 

She pried up a floorboard and brought out the older maps.  Nine of them, from nine clients since the crashes, each a delta of onyx branches measured with the same tape.  She laid them over Teo's, one by one, and the loft went quiet except for the rain. 

At the same distance from zero, on every map, sat the same card.  "Someone else will buy it later."  Not his words, exactly, but his fork, in nine hands and nine accents. 

"That's what I'm actually doing," she said.  "I'm not selling fortunes; I'm trying to map cultural patterns, the shapes a belief takes as it moves through people."  "A coin is only a rumor with a price tag."  If she could find where the shape closed, someone might learn to step out of it before the next one closed. 

Teo sat back on his heels, and something in his face unclenched.  "So I'm not an idiot." 

"You're a coordinate," she said, gently. 

Then she confided the thing she had never told a client.  Her grandmother's first map was not about money; it was about a village that agreed, all at once, that the bank was fine.  Three days before it wasn't.  She had measured that rumor with a dressmaker's tape, and she had died before the map was finished. 

"No one ever told me what the cards mean," Nadia said.  "I was handed a question and a tool."  "That's the whole inheritance." 

Teo looked at the skylight, at the rain sliding along the glass in paths that also multiplied.  "Can I add mine?" he asked. 

She gave him the brush, and the tape, and a blank card, and he wrote his branch in onyx ink that would outlast him.  Outside, the city kept buying and believing.  Inside, under the beaded glass, the map grew one fork wider, and stayed unfinished, and waited to be handed on.
