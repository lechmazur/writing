The husks had to steep nine days before they surrendered their color, and Odile loved that the ink could not be hurried.  She was the town's walnut-ink archivist: iron-gall ate through a page in eighty years, while walnut only held it.  Gently thorough, the harbormaster called her, and meant it as both compliment and complaint.  She checked every knot in every record twice, and she never once wrote that the sea had wanted anything.  The sea had no wants; it had weather, and men had debts.  Eleven years ago her brother Emil drowned inside the harbor mouth, and the old book said, taken by the sea.  Odile had struck that line and rebuilt the entry through proximate causes, the only method she trusted.  The lamp was dim; the oil had been sold to a mainland trader; the harbor account was empty; the labor day owed by every household had been commuted to a tax that nobody collected.  Four causes, each one a hand somebody could have held.  The covenant that built the jetty was four generations old, and its last clause was the one people forgot: "this bond shall be made again when context changes."  Nobody had asked what counted as change.  Context had changed eleven years ago too, when the labor day became a tax, and no one had called that change either; that was the failure she could not archive away.  Now the trader's ballast stones, dumped along the north wall, had seeded sea thrift, and the whole jetty bloomed pink at twilight above water that no longer matched the old soundings.  Odile took the flowering as evidence, which is what an archivist does with a miracle.  She posted the renewal for the evening the thrift opened widest.  In her coat she carried Emil's locket, and inside the locket a photograph the sea had steeped for eleven days before it gave him back.  Salt had lifted the emulsion until the faces were only weather.  She could still see it, though: Emil at nine, a plum in his fist, juice on his chin, laughing on this jetty ten minutes before nothing in particular happened.  That was the record she wanted, and no ledger in town had a column for it.  She had taken the post at nineteen to preserve the weight of small moments, and had learned that weight was the first thing a ledger dropped.  They came at dusk with pens, the forty households, grumbling about the labor day.  Odile read the four causes aloud, slowly, without accusation, letting each one sit in the salt air until somebody said a name.  The trader's agent said the oil had been cheap.  The old coxswain said he had trimmed the wick too short to save fuel, and had never told anyone, and had not slept well since.  She wrote both down in walnut ink, gently thorough, spelling the coxswain's name correctly because he deserved to be a cause and not a rumor.  Then she opened the locket and laid the ruined photograph on the new page.  Beside it she wrote what it had been: the plum, the chin, the laugh, the ten ordinary minutes.  "This is what the stones are for," she said.  The regenerated covenant kept the labor day and the lamp, and added one obligation: each household would bring the archive one small moment a year, named and dated, to be preserved with the same care as a debt.  They argued, and then they signed, forty names blooming down the sheet.  The harbormaster's daughter came first with her moment: her father whistling to the lamp as if it were a dog.  Odile entered it, and the ink dried the brown of Emil's hair.  The thrift closed for the night; the lamp came up behind her, lit on time, oil bought with money that existed.  She stood among the flowers with the empty locket in her fist, grieving so hard it turned into something like joy, and she laughed, and the sound went out over the harbor like a light being carried.
