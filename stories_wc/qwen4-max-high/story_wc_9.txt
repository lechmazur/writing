Three nights after the birth, Mara carried her sister's daughter up the goat path above the cloudline.  She had left the water flask behind to carry the child, and her mouth was already dry.  The priest called it cloudbound repentance: words of sorrow spoken above the clouds never fell back to earth, never landed in any living ear, and so the sky kept them.  Penitents climbed, spoke, and went down lighter; Mara did not want to be lighter, she wanted the hours back.  If sorrow accumulated up here, then perhaps the hours did too, the four she had spent reading cards for coins in a valley town while her sister labored and then stopped breathing.  Somewhere there had to be a silt of unspent time, and she meant to find it. 

The glade opened at midnight, black grass, white stones, and cloud lying in the hollows like poured milk.  It was so beautiful that her throat closed on the thin air, and she stood gagging on it, one hand pressed to the small warm weight inside her coat. 

She knelt and unrolled the birthday tablecloth.  Every year of her life the family had traced hands on it in onyx ink that no washing could lift: her mother's wide palms, her father's cracked thumb, her sister Teodora at six, at eleven, at nineteen.  Mara was a cartomancer, and she inked her own cards in that same black, so every fortune she sold was permanent.  The cloth was the only ledger of time her family had ever kept, and it had a gap in it now, a blank between last spring and this week. 

Mara set the saucer on a flat stone.  It came from Teodora's dolls' tea set, and it had a crack across its well no wider than a hair, the crack Mara had made at seven and blamed on the cat.  She poured ink into it and plugged the crack with her thumb, as she had for twenty years, because the ink was hers and the saucer was her sister's and neither could be replaced.  Then she laid her cards face down along the hem, all seventy-eight, and did not turn one.  Clients called this superstition; it was discipline.  A reader who knew her own fate stopped looking, and Mara had chosen, years ago, to stay willingly perplexed, because perplexity kept her climbing. 

The cloud came up through the cloth.  It filled the traced hands first, brimming in her mother's palms, in Teodora's small six-year-old fingers, until the linen looked printed with black water.  Then it went to the gap and pooled there, and kept pooling, thicker than anywhere else, a hand-shaped absence drinking the mist.  She had found it.  Lost time did not vanish; it settled in the shapes nobody had filled. 

She reached to scoop it, and saw the size of the hollow.  It was not her hand.  It was smaller than her palm, smaller than Teodora at six; it was the print of a hand three days old.  The hours she had lost were not hers.  They were the child's first hours, the ones nobody had witnessed, and they had come up here to wait in the shape of the only hand that could claim them. 

Mara sat back on her heels in the black grass.  She could speak her sorrow now and be free of it, and the sky would hold it forever, and Teodora would never hear a word.  Repentance up here was a letter with no address.  Inside her coat the baby stirred and made a sound like a hinge. 

So Mara unfastened the coat, all seven bone clasps, and let the cold in.  She lifted her niece out into the terrible beauty of the glade, dipped the tiny fingers in the leaking saucer, and pressed the hand into the gap.  The mist went black and stayed.  It did not give the four hours back.  It gave her a mark that would outlast every wash, a hand on the cloth in the year the family had almost stopped keeping. 

She said her sorry anyway, out loud, to cloud that would never carry it down, and felt nothing lift.  Then she wrapped the baby, fastened the clasps, kept the cards face down, and carried the cloth and the cracked saucer back toward the path, still not knowing, choosing not to know, whether she was forgiven.  There were hours below, and they were not lost yet.
