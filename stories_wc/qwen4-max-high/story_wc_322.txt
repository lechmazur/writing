My wife stopped knowing me on a Tuesday, and by Friday she knew a man who looked like me but had never been unkind.  The fever had begun editing her, and I am a fever dream architect, so I did what my trade permits: I built her a room.  I keep my workshop in the optical illusion gallery on Verge Street, where the staircase climbs by lying and the far corridor is four feet deep and painted to look like forty.  Illusions are cheap to maintain because they only have to hold from one angle, and a fevered mind supplies the angle.  Mine is a rational enchantment, which means every seam must be argued and every beam justified, or the structure sags into ordinary sleep.  You cannot charm a fever.  You can only prove something to it.  The charity hospital above rents me the closed gallery for nothing, because the illusions still keep their promises to an empty room and no one else wants them.  I work by weaving patterns, and my material is sinew thread, deer tendon dried and split, which shrinks as it dries and so pulls a joint tighter than any knot.  Bowyers use it.  So do surgeons, in the old books.  I wanted, plainly, to discover the texture of connection: whether the thing between two people was warmth or pressure or merely habit, and whether it could be held in the hand.  In the gallery I could measure it, because tension is measurable.  I strung the first pattern between the false corridor and the mirror room, and I carried Ilse down from the ward to walk it with me.  She was calm.  Fever makes people calm the way deep water makes them quiet.  She called me by my brother's name, and the thread across my palm went slack.  As memories alter, the geometry alters; that is the whole difficulty of my trade.  A room built for a woman who remembers a husband will not stand for a woman who remembers a brother-in-law, and I had argued every beam for the husband.  I rebuilt it realistically imaginary: the wallpaper she described from a house she never lived in, the window with the correct wrong tree outside it.  Nothing in it existed.  All of it was accurate.  The proof held.  The corridor lengthened to forty feet because she believed in forty feet, and belief, properly reasoned, is load-bearing.  For six nights I wove and she walked, and each night she revised me.  I learned the texture then: connection is not warmth.  It is draw.  It is the sinew drying, shrinking, pulling the far wall toward the near wall whether or not either wall consented.  On the seventh night she remembered something that had never happened: a cruelty, a door I had slammed, a year we had never spent apart.  The pattern could not carry it.  I had woven forgiveness into the seams and she no longer required any.  The gallery erupted.  Not loudly.  The staircase unfolded into three flat boards; the forty-foot corridor slapped back to four feet of lath and painted shadow; the mirror room let go of its extra woman.  The sinew thread, drying faster than I had calculated, contracted through my fingers and opened them to the bone.  I stood in a small dirty museum with my hands bleeding and Ilse sitting on the floor, entirely peaceful, waiting for the man who had hurt her to come back and be sorry.  That was the eruption.  Nothing burned.  The paint smelled like paint for the first time in years.  Here is what the trade does not tell you: rooms that erupt can be argued back.  I had the proofs.  I could have justified the husband beam by beam until she believed me again.  Instead I sat down on the four-foot floor and began weaving the pattern she asked for, the slammed door, the cold cities, the wrong tree at the window.  It cost me my name in her memory, and it held.  The thread shrank.  The walls drew together.  Somewhere in the pull, indistinguishable from the ache in my hands, was the thing I had wanted to touch.  She rested her head against my shoulder, forgiving a stranger.  I have never been so calmly afraid.
