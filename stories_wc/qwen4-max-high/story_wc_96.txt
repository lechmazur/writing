The tin says COFFEE in letters worn to ghosts, and it holds every penny anyone ever paid me for a poem.  That is my trade: vagrant, penny poet, four lines for a cent, and I sleep behind the ruin on Alder Street.  The ruin was the Carnegie library once, before the fires and the riots and the years nobody counted.  Its roof is gone, its shelves are rusted orange, and at night the stars sit in the reading room like patrons who never left.  Everything here is broken, and I have made a living arranging broken things into four lines that scan. 

I met her sorting books at the prison library, thirty days for sleeping in a doorway.  She was the trustee, twelve years in, and she ran that cart of donations like a surgeon running instruments.  A book shelved wrong is a book burned, she said, and she taught me the numbers until my hands knew them.  We sorted nine hundred books in a month, and somewhere in the poetry we became friends.  Then they let her out, and she came to find me under the open roof, and she was someone else. 

She molts personalities monthly.  In March she was Juniper, who spoke in questions and wore a man's coat; in April, Odile, who laughed like a hinge and would not touch anything green.  Inside, they had made her be one woman, numbered and predictable, and she had learned that a fixed self is a thing men can break.  So now she sheds them on purpose, the way a tree sheds to grow.  I keep the old names on catalog cards, filed under Biography, because somebody should be able to find her.  The cards live in the coffee tin, under the false bottom I hammered out of a can lid.  Pennies on top for the world to see; underneath, seeds, and the catalog of everything we saved. 

The city fenced the ruin in May and posted a demolition date, and she said, then we rebuild it first.  Not a protest, she said.  A protest is a fist, and fists are what they know how to hold.  We plant. 

So every night we cut the fence and carried in salvaged shelves, and the neighborhood came, because ruin is contagious but so is work.  We filled the catalog drawers with soil and pressed beans and nasturtium into the dirt.  We shelved the nine hundred books, spine out, in the order she taught me, and wrote the catalog out forty times by hand.  Kids wrote poems on the cracked plaster; I paid them a penny each, and the tin got lighter and heavier at once.  We worked by starlight all summer and saved the moon for the last night.  By then the vines had reached the second shelf, and the whole broken place was blooming. 

The crews were coming at dawn.  We had one hour, and we spent it waiting for light.  As the moon cleared the skyline it came through the missing roof and lay across the stacks like a hand laid on a fever.  That was the hour we chose, and everyone stood in it, forty of us, reading titles aloud.  She was new again that month, a quiet woman called Sol who did not flinch at noise.  I lifted the false bottom and gave out the seeds and the copies of the catalog.  Take the list, I said.  A library is not a building, it is an order somebody remembers.  They can knock down the shelves; they cannot knock down forty people who know where everything goes.  Sol folded her old names into her pocket, one card for every woman she had been, and said she would keep molting, keep making, because anarchy is only soil nobody has planted yet. 

At dawn the fence hung open and the crews found flowers coming up through the card catalog and a hundred people shelving in the rubble.  The foreman stood in the reading room a long time, and then he asked what the flowers needed.  Sun, I said, water, and to be left alone.  He wrote it down. 

They may still come with machines; we have the list, the seeds, and the tin with its hidden floor.  Let them break it again.  Every crack we make lets in more light, and we have learned to plant in the light.
