The smog turned the moon saffron, the color of threads steeping in hot water. 
Mira painted by that light because she had no other; eleven years of insomnia had made her a night worker, and she had stopped arguing with her eyes. 
Her studio was the rooftop greenhouse of a building the traffic below had been trying to erase for a century. 
Inside, crocus bulbs waited in trays, tomatoes gave off their green smell, and soot filmed the panes. 
Each night she wiped one clear circle, the way you clear a place at a table for someone who is late. 
The old woman who owned the roof, Teodora, lay in the flat below with no words left. 
The stroke in spring had taken her speech, and with it Vesht, the tongue of a valley now under the reservoir. 
She had been its last calligrapher. 
The doctors said the words were gone; Mira thought they might only be lying down. 
Teodora's brush stayed on Mira's table, its tip white as a moon sliver, and it refused every stroke Mira made on purpose. 
It worked only in daydreams: moonlight through the wiped circle, her gaze gone soft over the leaves, her hand wandering off without her. 
She would come back to herself an hour later and find Vesht on the board, letters like small hooks and rivers, and no way to read them. 
That was the work. 
She wanted proof that a kindness given to a stranger outlives the language it was given in. 
For nine months Mira had knocked on doors in the smog and asked strangers whether anyone had ever been kind to them for nothing. 
Thirty-one said yes. 
Eleven still had the letters Teodora had written for them when the valley was dry: petitions, apologies, one proposal, one note to a son at the front. 
A scribe who charged nothing, and whose name none of them had known until Mira told them. 
Mira painted each kindness on a small board, and the brush put it into Vesht, and every board ended with the same three marks she had never chosen. 
She assumed they were a signature. 
She was methodically hopeful: one door by day, one board by night, the moon waxing a fingernail wider each week toward full. 
She meant to rouse Teodora with them: thirty-one strangers who still remembered her hands. 
On the night the moon was three days from full, Mira and the night porter carried the old woman up in her chair. 
They set her among the crocus trays, facing the wiped circle, and left. 
Saffron light came through and lay on the old woman's face and hands, and nothing happened. 
Mira hung the boards on the wires between the tomato stakes, where the moon could read them. 
Then she talked, quietly, the way you talk to someone drifting off: the woman in the market whose petition kept her stall, the man whose mother heard from him once a month for four years, the girl who married because a stranger's scribe found the words she could not. 
She was not performing; she was doing what she did instead of sleeping. 
Moonlight on the leaves, the smell of green, thirty-one kindnesses swinging slightly in the warm air. 
Mira felt her own gaze go soft, felt the daydream take her, and let it. 
When she came back, Teodora had the moon-tipped brush. 
The old woman's eyes were still far away, somewhere in a valley with the water not yet over it, and her hand was moving. 
She wrote the three marks that ended every board. 
Then, under them, in the shaking block letters of the city, she wrote: IT REACHED ME. 
Mira sat down on the damp floor among the crocus. 
Not a signature, then: a receipt. 
Teodora had put it at the foot of every letter she gave away, and the brush had taught it to Mira's dreaming hands. 
Thirty-one strangers had kept the paper; the woman who wrote it had kept them. 
Teodora's hand stopped, and she looked at Mira, and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say it with. 
Then she put the brush back in Mira's hand and closed her fingers over it, which was an instruction. 
Mira carried her down at dawn, then set out a thirty-second board beside the brush. 
The moon would wane and come back; there were more doors. 
She would keep clearing a place at the table.
