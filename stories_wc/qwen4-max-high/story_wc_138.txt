In nine hundred years I have said goodbye ten thousand times and never once finished one.  The fragment sees to that.  Half an amulet, black as a stopped pupil, hung at my throat on a wire of lodestone.  Its curse is simple: whoever wears it cannot be gone.  Doors remember me.  Roads bend back.  Absence refuses to hold my shape.  So I stay, and the staying has worn me smooth as a stair.  Boredom, in an immortal, is not sadness; it is a house where every door has already been opened. 

We hide in the escondite, a refuge cut into a mountain of split light.  Every wall is a prism; every corridor forks the sun into seven corridors, and only one of them is real.  Forty-one families shelter here from the wars that walk the plain below.  I am the anchor that keeps their rooms where they put them.  I see the fold the rest of them cannot, the fourth direction where the mountain is tucked flat and stitched.  Rooms, to me, are shapes lying obediently in a hand.  Lately I have felt them flex. 

What I want is small and enormous: to learn what happens after goodbye.  Not the leaving.  The after.  Whether the room keeps your shape, whether the ache has a weight, whether anyone says your name into the prism and hears it come back changed. 

The almanac promised a solar storm on the eleventh night, a great bruised weather of the sun.  I began my intrigue in the coil room.  The escondite is pinned by magnetic fields: iron cores under the floors, humming wires that tell each stone which way is down.  The fragment rides those fields like a rider on a spine.  Reverse the coils while the storm floods them and the fields will buck, and in that bucking the fragment can be lifted from a throat. 

Tomas oils the coils, a boy of nineteen who counts the corridors aloud so they will not wander.  I told him the storm would come hungry, and that reversing the cores would shield us.  A lie shaped exactly like the truth, which is the only kind that works.  He believed me because I am old and he wanted to be the one who saved us.  There was a price, and I did not tell him.  The anchor must be worn; if I put it down, someone must pick it up, or the escondite comes unpinned and forty-one families fall out of the light. 

On the eleventh night the sky went green and began to breathe.  Thunder came, but hushed, as if the mountain had a hand over its mouth.  Under the floors the iron woke and screamed in a register only I could hear.  Tomas threw the levers.  Then the rooms mutinied.  They had been ordered into place for nine centuries and they were tired of it.  Stairs refused their landings; the granary opened into the chapel; the seventh corridor of the sun stepped out of the wall and stood there, real, humming, demanding a country.  I felt the fragment loosen at my throat like a tooth. 

I could have walked out then and let the light spill.  Instead I found Tomas in the coil room with his hands on the levers and his face full of green weather.  I told him the truth, which I had been saving like a coin.  "Someone has to hold it," I said.  "Or everyone goes out into the plain and the war finds them."  He was quiet for the length of three heartbeats, and in that quiet I heard the mountain swallow.  "You want to know what it's like," he said.  "On the other side of it."  He held out his hand.  Nineteen years old, and he understood the whole shape of me, folded flat, seams showing.  I put the wire in his palm and closed his fingers on it, and I did not let myself hurry.  The rooms came back to their orders, sullen, one by one, like soldiers. 

I said goodbye.  The word went out of me and did not come back.  I walked down the seventh corridor before it faded, and out through the mountain's fourth fold, into the storm.  Nothing bent me back.  The road stayed a road.  And this is what happens after goodbye: the ache is not yours.  You go on being a shape in a room you are not in.  Somewhere behind me a boy is holding a black stone and cannot leave, and I am the weight he carries, and the thunder goes on saying my name very quietly, forever.
