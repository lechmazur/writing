The truck took us out of the valley in the middle of my grandmother's funeral.  I was nine; for thirty-one years in Marseille I was a sentence stopped halfway.  In April I came back to finish the burial, and to find out whether God had stayed here.  I had left mine on the road.  I stopped praying at nineteen, the way you stop writing to an address that never answers.  My mother kept her rosary in a drawer, and I kept my grandmother's last letter in a book I never finished. 

The archaeologist, Vartan, was opening a tomb under the apricot rows.  Roots snaked between the bones down there, threading eye sockets, tying femur to rib like knots in a net.  He let me work if I learned to be careful.  I asked questions the way other people knock.  I asked the diggers about their lunches, the widow about her grafting knife, the boys about which trees fruited first.  Because I was warmly curious and not aggrieved, they told me things.  Vartan sent me walking the rows each morning to map where the frost lifted first.  Purposeful wandering, he called it; the ground breathes over a hollow.  Twenty-two rows, and I never walked them in the same order twice.  On the ninth morning, under a tree that fruited early, I found the breathing place. 

We spent May going down.  Apricot petals fell into the trench, and we sieved them out with the fill.  Roots had to be cut, and I apologized to each one, quietly, in my Marseille accent.  The tomb was my grandfather's family vault, and the last niche was hers.  They had slid her in and run, leaving the niche unsealed, waiting for a stone.  The stone was there.  A slab lay face down over her, and when we lifted it, sunlight fell on carved hour lines.  Vartan went very still.  It was the base of the chapel sundial, my grandfather's work, missing since the war.  My grandfather must have carried it here and laid it face down, so the hours would keep watch underground.  The socket for the gnomon was empty; the shadow's journey had been interrupted too.  Half the village had a journey stopped inside it, and mine was only the one that had gone farthest. 

I did not weep, and I did not pray; I asked Vartan for the pry bar.  I wanted to rebuild it.  Not the chapel, which was gone, and not my childhood, which was gone.  Just a dial above her, so that something in this valley would tell the time again.  June went into the setting.  We bedded the base on lime mortar above the sealed niche, level, facing south.  The widow's son forged a gnomon from the pry bar, and I paid him with the last of my Marseille money.  Vartan argued the angle with me for two evenings, and being argued with felt like being believed in.  I kept expecting the feeling to be false, the way a translation is false, but my hands stayed steady.  The roots went on threading the bones beneath us, patient, unhurried, feeding the fruit.  I began to suspect that nothing here had ended, only paused, and that I had been walking back for thirty-one years. 

On the last day of the dig we stood around it: the diggers, the widow, the boys.  The shadow crawled toward the noon line, thin as a hair, and nobody spoke.  I said her name, Anahid, and finished the prayer the truck had cut in half.  The shadow touched the line.  Nothing opened; no light came down the gallery; the dead kept their bones.  But the hour was correct, and it was correct because we had made it so.  The boys asked whether it would still tell time in winter, and I said we would find out.  I would not call it faith yet.  I called it noon, and I stayed to watch the afternoon come.
