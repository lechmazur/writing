The mist came off Pearl Bay like breath off a mirror, and Maren wiped it from the atrium's lowest pane with her sleeve.  Above her the prism atrium stacked the dark into thin colors, mist moving through its ribs.  She had one hour, the hour before everything shifts, to make the reckoners see. 

Pearl Bay audited itself by noctilucent reckonings.  All night the atrium gathered the glow of the high shining clouds, and whatever that faint light touched was written into the ledger as worth keeping.  Whatever stayed dark went onto the barges at the turn of the tide.  At dawn the ledger closed, the gates opened, and the old quarter would begin to come down. 

Maren was the harbor glazier, which meant she spent her life looking through things other people looked at.  In the black pane her own face floated over the bay, and she had long ago stopped deciding which of the two she was studying.  Her mother had held the trade before her, and had kept the reckoning book for thirty years. 

Maren read it now by lantern, and read what was not in it.  Between the crown prism and the great stair windows, her mother had left blank lines.  Forty of them, ruled and never filled.  Her mother had ruled those lines and left them empty, which was not carelessness but a question saved for someone. 

Maren knew what belonged there, because she had carried the glass herself: the ferry's cracked portlight, the bottle roundels in the netmender's roof, the blue cottage pane with the solder scar.  Her mother had mended them for free, and free had no column in the reckoning.  So she had spent the night reglazing the atrium's skirt with every one of them.  Forty panes of ordinary work, set low where the reckoners never looked. 

The trouble was aim.  The night glow was too faint to reach the low skirt at all; only the first sun would go down there.  Her mother had taught her to find the angles at the mill pond behind the atrium.  The pond was round as the atrium's skirt and ringed with forty stones, one for each pane, set there by her mother long ago.  You threw a stone and watched the rings run out and cross.  Where the rings met they doubled; where they passed through one another they left dark seams, and the seams told you where light would refuse to go.  It was not magic, her mother said; it was only water doing arithmetic in the open. 

Maren went out with a pocket of harbor gravel and knelt at the pond in the mist.  Glaziers came here to commune with the dead who had taught them, asking the water the questions the ledger would not hold.  She said her mother's name once, and the rings took it outward and brought nothing back but geometry, which was the only answer her mother had ever trusted.  She threw and read and shifted, threw and read and shifted, until the seams fell between the panes and the crossings fell on them. 

Maren did not want the atrium saved for its crown; she wanted the ferry portlight priced, so that the next glazier would be paid for mending it. 

The reckoners came at first light with the ledger on a stand, three men in oilskins who had never once looked down.  Dawn in Pearl Bay arrives gray; the mist holds the sun a half hour and lets it through as a low white bar.  That bar struck the crown prism, and the prism sent it down the skirt, and forty ordinary panes took the light and broke it into color across the open pages.  The blank lines filled with green and bottle brown and the scarred blue of the cottage. 

The rule was the rule: what the light touched was kept.  The eldest reckoner bent, read the colors lying in the ruled gaps, and understood that he was being asked to price a ferry window.  He asked whose work it was, and she said her mother's, and then her own.  He wrote forty entries while the tide stood still. 

Then the gates opened and everything shifted, but the ledger shifted first, and that is the order that matters.  Maren went back to the pond and threw one stone, for no reason she could have entered in a book, and watched the rings carry the morning outward until the water was flat again.
