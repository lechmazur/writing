In the mirror world, the sun set nine hundred years ago and never bothered to rise.  What light remained was borrowed, reflected, secondhand, and it was running out.  Sefa kept the alchemical orchard greenhouse at the edge of the village, where the glass walls held back the dark and the trees grew songs instead of fruit.  Each pear of cloudy glass held a tune somebody had stopped singing: a lullaby, a harvest round, a name called across a field.  Sefa was the memory keeper, and she was scattered.  Years ago she had traded her own memories into the trees to make room for everyone else's, so she knew the village's grandmothers by heart and could not recall her mother's face.  The villagers came to her when they lost something, and she gave it back, and this was how they stayed a people rather than a scatter of lit windows. 

But borrowed light thins.  That season the orchard's leaves went gray, the songs inside the pears grew faint as breath, and three houses on the hill forgot they were neighbors.  A realm dies from its edges inward, forgetting first that it is one thing.  So Sefa did what her craft required: she began to weave a dawn. 

Dawns are not made of light alone.  They are woven, warp of remembered morning, weft of forgotten songs, and the loom in her greenhouse had stood idle since the last weaver died unnamed.  For warp she needed the true morning, and there was one place in the mirror world where it could be found.  At the greenhouse's north end, set in a frame of blackened lead, hung the mica window.  Mica is stone that has learned to be glass; it does not silver, does not flatter, does not reverse.  Through its split amber sheets Sefa could see the waking world's orchard, the original of this copy, where the sun came up every day without being asked.  She combed threads of that sunrise through the mica, hand over hand, until her palms smelled of hot stone. 

For weft she went door to door asking for songs nobody sang anymore.  The baker hummed four notes his wife used to whistle; the widow found a rope-pulling chant; children offered a counting rhyme about a bird that no longer existed.  Sefa wound each onto spindles, and where two songs had once been sung together, she knotted them, so the cloth would hold the village the way the village had once held itself. 

Nine nights she wove, and the emerging dawn was a small warm thing, shivering, no bigger than a folded shawl.  New light must be swaddled or it dies of its own newness.  She had nothing left to wrap it in but herself.  So she went to the oldest tree and shook down the pears that held her own scattered life: her mother at a wash basin, a boy's laugh, a winter she had survived.  She left one pear unshaken, the one that held her name, because a weaver must leave herself a way back.  She spun them into a binding cloth, tasting each one last time, and swaddled the dawn tight, band over band, the way you bind an infant so it will not startle itself apart. 

Then she carried it to the mica window and understood the last rule of her craft.  A swaddled dawn cannot rise.  To rekindle the realm she had to unbind the very thing she had spent herself to hold.  Sefa stood a long while with her hands closed.  Then she opened them. 

The bands fell away, her mother's face went out like a candle, and the dawn unfolded through the mica and kept unfolding, over the orchard, over the hill, over the three houses that remembered, suddenly, that they were neighbors.  Everywhere the light touched, people found their forgotten songs in their mouths and sang them to each other, and the knots held. 

In the greenhouse a woman with empty hands sat down among the bright leaves, unsure who she was.  They came at midmorning, all of them, carrying the last pear from the oldest tree, and they sang her name until she could hold it.  Her name was Sefa, and now the village kept it for her.
