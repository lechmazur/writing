The greenhouse had been rusting longer than Sefa had been alive, which was why she chose it.  Nobody guards a ruin.  Below the hill, the city ate Standard: gray, complete, forgettable, issued by the Concordance to end the wars that remembering had started.  Sefa cooked in the ruin, and her food remembered. 

Her tongue read whatever hands left behind.  She could taste the grief in a widow's onions, the exact hot afternoon a boy stole a plum.  The Concordance called this a disorder; Sefa, a chef who had never finished school and had learned everything from dirt and fire, called it a palate. 

The seed arrived eleven years ago in a dead smuggler's tobacco tin.  Knowledge fruit, he had called it, the last pip of a tree the Concordance burned in every district but this one, where the fire took the roof instead.  It would ripen, he said, only when the two moons and the long comet stood in a line, which happens once in a hundred and twelve years, which meant tonight.  Her mother had cooked in an orchard kitchen that the Concordance paved over.  Sefa wanted one beautiful thing standing in the wreckage before she died, and she did not care whether the law called it a garden or a crime. 

She had kept the sapling alive eleven winters under glass gone orange with rust, feeding it ash, rainwater, and the strained water from her dishes.  Tonight it threw out ninety-one flowers, and it had roots in six inches of rubble. 

Odo climbed the stairs at dusk, as he did every night, to eat and to argue.  He had been a brain doctor before the Concordance ruled that memory was the disease.  "Memory is not a jar," he liked to say, tapping his skull.  "It is a path worn through the meat, and the right taste wears it back in."  "They took my license for saying so." 

The manuals he stole for her said to prune in dormancy, never in flower, and to trust the tables.  Sefa could not read the tables.  She put a leaf on her tongue instead and tasted eleven years of thirst, and a wanting like a hand held out.  "It is trying to make ninety-one children on one meal," she said.  "It will kill itself being generous." 

So she pruned.  She cut living wood, not dead, which was the whole art, and which she knew before Odo said it.  Branch after branch came off until three flowers stood alone, and the cuts wept a smell like her mother's coat. 

At the hour the moons took the comet between them, the light came through the rust and turned the whole ruin the color of apricots.  Three fruits swelled, went gold, and split their skins. 

She had invited twenty people, and twenty came, and a warden came with them to log the violation.  Everything she planned worked through synaptic plasticity, the slow wearing back of paths, so it could not be shot, arrested, or confiscated.  It could only be eaten.  At nineteen she had thought about poisoning the depot tanks, and had decided instead that the cruelest thing she could do to the Concordance was to give people back what they loved. 

She served the fruit sliced thin over hot rice, because a whole one would have flooded them; Odo had warned her that a mind rewired too fast tears.  She had pruned the tree, and now she pruned the portion. 

The warden ate because refusing food in front of twenty witnesses is its own kind of report.  He stopped with the spoon at his lips.  In the fruit was his grandmother's kitchen, the summer the orchards were taken, and the name of the dog he had been trained to forget.  He set the spoon down, wrote nothing, and asked for the recipe. 

That was the whole of her gentle rebellion: no barricade, no fire, only dinner, and twenty people carrying home paths worn back into them, wanting things they could finally name. 

Sefa wrapped the three pits in wet cloth and gave them out at the door, one to a hand, with instructions she could not write.  "Cut what is alive," she told them.  "That is how you make room."  Above the city the glass kept rusting, and under it, in the ruin, something beautiful was growing on purpose.
