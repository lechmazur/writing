The vines had been grey for three summers, and Odile Fenn had spent all three of them building things nobody asked for.  A kettle that whistled the weather.  An umbrella that counted raindrops and lost count on purpose, because she liked the number nine.  The village called her the tinkerer, fondly, the way you name a woman who has stopped being useful.  Under her bench, in a jam jar of rainwater, she kept the last living tendril of the mother vine, two inches of new wood with one bud at its heel.  Her mother had been the last cantor of the Vine Blessing, and had died with the fourth verse unsung.  The cooperative's agronomist had called the blessing superstition, and the village, embarrassed, had agreed.  Then the grey came, and the sprays did nothing, and nobody could remember what the songs had told them to do.  That was the thing Odile understood too late: the verses were never decoration.  They were instructions, folded into story so that children would carry them.  So she went collecting.  Three lines from the widow Sarrow.  A tune's shape from a deaf man who remembered his grandmother's mouth.  The rest from the mill ledger, where a clerk had tallied the singing in strokes.  She pinned the fragments into a brass barrel, the way a music box holds a waltz, and built a crank to turn it.  The rite could only be sung when everything aligned: the harvest moon sinking west as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, one dawn in nineteen years.  That dawn was Thursday.  She carried the organ up to the arbor before light, the jar tucked under one arm.  The arbor was still absurdly green, leaf over leaf above the dead rows, a verdant room with nothing left in it.  People came because they had heard she was doing something foolish, and stayed because there was nowhere better to be at that hour.  A hush gathered, the particular quiet of a crowd deciding whether to hope.  Odile turned the crank.  Her mother had said the harvest moon spins golden stories, and Odile had built a box to catch one.  The barrel spun out the tunes, thin and bright, and they went into people the way water goes into dry ground.  The miller sang the first verse, naming the rows.  Two old women sang the second, naming the women who had planted them.  The third was a joke about a goat, and the widow Sarrow laughed one cracked note and covered her mouth.  Odile did not have the fourth verse.  Bren, who had signed the paper calling the blessing superstition, sang it for her.  Cut the blackened canes below the third joint, burn them, seal the wounds with ash and whey, and graft living wood to the bitter wild root on the ridge, which the grey cannot climb.  Three summers of spray, and the remedy had come to them through forgotten songs, past a joke about a goat.  They had all told her they remembered nothing; they had only been ashamed.  Then the barrel reached the gap where she had never managed to pin the fifth tune, and the machine gave them silence.  Into that hush the widow Sarrow sang the fifth verse, all of it, in a voice like a hinge.  She had been waiting nineteen years for somebody to ask.  Others took the sixth.  Odile let go of the crank.  The barrel stopped; the singing did not.  That was the whole of her ambition, and she had never said it plainly: to give the village back its sacred practice, and to make it ordinary again.  Moonset and sunrise met in the leaves.  Light came through the arbor from both ends of the sky at once, and every dead row threw two shadows.  She knelt and bound the mother vine's tendril into a wild root the boys had dug from the ridge while the village sang.  Bren sent his boy for ash and whey, because the verse said so, and because his own father had once said so.  By full light the men were cutting canes.  By noon the smoke of the burning stood over the valley like something answered.  Odile took the brass barrel out of the organ and hung it on a nail.  A song kept in a box can be lost again; a song kept in children cannot.  The next aligning dawn would come in nineteen years, and she meant the children to be hoarse long before it.  In October the tendril took, and reached for the nearest thing to hold, which was her finger.  She let it.
