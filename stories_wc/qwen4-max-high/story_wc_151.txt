At dawn the Aurelian Arcade turned to poured gold, its glass roof throwing long bars of light across the brass shopfronts, and Nell walked into it the way other people walked into church.  The lattice had gone up into the sky sixty years ago and taken every message with it, instantaneous and logged forever; what remained on the surface was paper, and walkers to carry it, and Nell.  Surface mail, the clerks called it, with the tenderness reserved for obsolete things.  Her trade was pseudonyms.  A letter could not travel under a registry name without leaving a shadow in the sky, so people came to her, and she made them somebody else.  She was carefully inventive: three drafts minimum, checked against the shipping rolls, the saints' calendars, the obituaries, until the name was common enough to vanish and strange enough to be a door.  She drafted in chalk on an ebony clipboard, white letters on black wood, and wiped each failure away with her sleeve.  Nothing she considered ever survived to be scanned. 

Teodora Rusk was waiting at the mail house door with a letter in her two hands.  The walker's bell would ring at seven, and after that the letter would be gone six weeks toward the coast, so there was an hour, and no more, to find the woman a name.  "My son," she said.  "He walked out of the lattice twelve years ago, and I never wrote, because I could not think what to call myself that he would forgive." 

Nell clipped the envelope to the ebony and did what she always did first: she read the margins.  People put the truth outside the lines.  She had lived in one herself since she was nineteen, when she traded her registry name for a made-up one and found she could finally breathe.  In the left margin of the envelope, pressed faint as a moth, the woman had drawn a hopscotch grid: eight squares, and the ninth left empty.  "Where did you copy this?"  "He sent one, years ago, with nothing written inside."  Just the empty square; she had kept it twelve years and never understood it. 

Nell knelt at the arcade's threshold, where the children of the upper floors had chalked their game the evening before.  The walkers' routes lived there, in the sidewalk chalk, because children redraw a hopscotch grid faithfully every morning and rain deletes it every week, and no archive in the sky has ever bothered to remember a game.  Numbers for stages, a cross for a dead drop, a loop for a turn back.  She laid the clipboard on the flags and juxtaposed the two grids, the woman's faint pencil and the children's fat blue chalk, sliding one against the other until they agreed.  Eight squares, and a ninth blank, in both.  The blank had been there since Nell first took the shop; she had assumed it was a child's unfinished afternoon.  It was not.  A blank ninth square was a held berth: a walker at the far end of a route, waiting for a reply he had no way to request.  Twelve years of children copying an empty box, keeping a man's silence alive on the ground while the sky said nothing at all. 

"He isn't waiting for you to apologize," Nell said.  "He's waiting for the mail." 

She wiped the ebony clean and began.  Not Rusk: too near the registry, and too near the old hurt.  She tried the woman's mother's name, then a saint's day, then the street where the boy was born, and struck each out; a name had to be a door, not a disguise.  "What did he call you, before?"  "Dodo," she said, and looked ashamed of it.  Nell wrote DODO, softened it to DORA, and added the chalk word for the last square, the one you reach and turn around at: SKY.  "Dora Sky," she said.  "Common enough to pass any hand-check on the coast, and he will read it in one second, and know both who wrote and where to come back to."  Teodora's mouth moved without sound. 

Nell inked the sender line, Dora Sky, and the chalk route into the address block, and the envelope became a thing that could travel.  Then she took her chalk stub and wrote a nine in the empty square on the flags: berth filled, wait closed.  The children would copy it faithfully at breakfast, and the walkers would read it by noon.  The bell had not rung yet.  The letter rested on the ebony clipboard in the gold light, addressed, named, still unmailed, and Nell kept her hand on it a moment longer, the way you hold a door for someone who has been a long time coming down the road.
