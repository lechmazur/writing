Odo Pell repaired music boxes that were not broken.  Anyone could splice a snapped spring; his master had taught him the harder art, mending what isn't torn, listening for the note that would fail next winter and filing its tooth before it failed.  The town of Sallow used to laugh at him for it.  Then midnight started taking the light, and nobody laughed.  It began the year the bell tower's twelfth stroke went sour: after each midnight the days came back a little thinner, until noon looked like a lamp behind a curtain.  His daughter Nell asked what blue was, and Odo said it was the color of a warm trumpet, and then he said the sky was not getting darker, she was only tired.  That was the lie he would have to undo.  Beneath the tower lay the resonance chamber, a brick egg where every sound the town made went to settle and hum.  Odo went down with a lantern, a tuning fork, and a chip of the philosopher's stone the size of a barley grain, wrapped in wax paper.  His master's note said the stone perfects whatever note it hangs in, and that a lie will damp it faster than a wet thumb.  In the chamber's throat he found the covenant.  It was an iron plate, and it was flowering: ferns of rust curling off the signatures of the founders, who had traded one hour of light each night for a bell that kept the river from freezing and the year from slipping its gears.  A covenant of rust, the old repairmen called it: a promise that eats itself a little every year to stay alive.  The ferns stood crisp only near honest speech; they wilted near a lie.  Every fern on the plate hung black and limp.  Sallow had spent forty years calling midnight a thief instead of a creditor, and a wilted covenant cannot hold its terms, so the bell had taken the hour, and then the morning, and then the whole noon.  The hoard was here, all of it, folded into a hum Odo could feel in his teeth.  He could hear single things inside it: a cartwheel from last spring, a wedding, his mother winding a music box the year he was born.  He struck his tuning fork and the chamber answered, and by that answer he found the node, the one still point in all that ringing.  There he tethered the chip on a wire of brass, so it hung in the true note without touching anything that could damp it.  Above him the hammer fell eleven times, and he waited for the twelfth.  Between the twelfth stroke and its dying lies a pause the bell-keepers call the balance, the one moment the bell owes nobody anything.  Nell's whole childhood hung in the balance of it.  Odo put his hand on the plate to speak his claim, and the ferns curled tighter and the tethered chip went flat, because he had come down intending to say that Sallow had never agreed to any of this.  He heard the sourness, and stopped.  Nothing on the plate was torn, which was why no one in forty years had thought to mend it.  Then he said the true things, one at a time, the way you file a tooth: we signed, and we owe an hour, and I told my daughter the sky was fine when it was dying, and I want the light back anyway.  The rust lifted.  Fern by fern it stood up crisp and orange, and where the chip hung in its true note the hum began to perfect itself, unfolding into what it had been before midnight swallowed it.  Light came out of the brick.  Forty years of noons went up the flue and out over Sallow, and the chamber rang emptier and brighter than any church.  One hour stayed dark, honestly owed, and the bell took it and rang the river unfrozen.  He climbed up into a blue morning at midnight, and woke Nell to tell her the truth: that blue was nothing like a trumpet, that he had lied, and that from now on they would pay an hour and call it payment, not theft.  On the plate below, the rust stood crisp, and Odo went back to his bench to mend the boxes that were not yet broken, which is the only way anything stays whole.
