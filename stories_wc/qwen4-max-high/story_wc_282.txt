Humans had learned to hibernate sixty winters ago, and the coast had learned to keep them.  Maren walked the cave network with her palms open, reading grit, draft, and drip.  She was a sensory map maker; her charts recorded no distances, only what a body would meet in the dark.  Salt sting here.  Bat musk there.  A cold seam where the tide breathed through limestone.  Above her, three hundred people slept out the winter in the cliff's ribs. 

Her real instrument was the emotional heat map.  Grief ran cold and pooled in low galleries; comfort glowed amber near the sleeping vaults.  She calibrated it against the sleepers' monitors, and the caves bloomed into weather. 

Every sleeper crossed with a transitional object: a wool square, a wooden bead, a spoon worn thin by a thumb.  The Authority issued them by the crate, and by the crate they failed.  A borrowed thing could not carry a person over the threshold; only a loved thing could. 

Maren had no such object.  Hers had been a strip of her mother's coat, lost in her second winter, and no substitute would take.  So she stayed awake while the county slept, and made maps for the crossing, and wanted something greedier than maps.  She wanted to be the voice of the unknown, the one sound a dreamer could follow through a place no chart could name. 

In vault four, an old man named Teodor would not go under.  Three nights running, his monitor cooled toward the blue of open water.  The Authority's bead lay in his fist like a stone in a dead hand.  Maren laid her sensors along his cot and watched the cold pour off him, down the corridor, into a gallery no one had mapped. 

She followed it at low tide, wading, then swimming where the roof dropped.  The brine was dense enough to hold her up; she floated with her lamp between her teeth and let the cold lead her.  In the silt below, something pale turned.  An elm leaf, printed in stone. 

Fossil: thirty million years, mineral replacement, density two point six.  It should have sunk.  It rose to her hand and lay there, warm as a held coin.  Both facts were true, and Maren had stopped arguing with such things; the caves were factually mystical, measurable and impossible in the same breath. 

She knew the leaf.  Teodor had found it at eleven, in the summer before the vaults were cut, and had carried it into every sleep since.  The Authority had confiscated it in the standardization, logged it, lost it.  Half a lifetime the leaf had lain in the silt, and the cold had pooled above it. 

She swam back with the leaf against her throat.  She pried the bead from his fingers and put the stone leaf in its place.  His hand closed.  The monitor warmed from blue to green to the amber of a kitchen at dusk.  On her map the unmapped gallery flushed hot, and the cold seam closed like a mouth finishing a sentence. 

Maren sat with him until his breathing found the long tide of true sleep.  She had no object of her own, and would not cross this winter either.  But she understood at last what a transitional object was: not a thing, a permission.  Anything loved enough to be trusted could carry you into what you could not see. 

The Authority piped recordings into the vaults; the sleepers went under listening.  She set her recorder at the mouth of the gallery and spoke her map aloud: the drip at the third bend, the salt sting, the amber shelf where the sleepers lay.  She spoke the blank places too, naming them blank, promising to be there when the dreamers reached them.  Her voice went into the dark ahead of them, and the dark took it and held.  She would never sleep; she would be what the sleepers held. 

Somewhere under the hill an old man dreamed of an elm that died before there were people to stand beneath it, and did not wake.  Maren kept talking.
