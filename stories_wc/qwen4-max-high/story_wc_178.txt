The palace had forty days left to be nobody's.  The old king was ash in the marsh, the Persian governor still twelve roads away, and between them the seals hung broken on every door.  Tabni, junior astronomer, kept the water clock running because nobody had told her to stop.  In the vacancy the palace had been reimagined by necessity: barley in the queen's bath, the royal dais given to the water clock, gnomon stakes pegged down the processional way.  She kept the accounts too: nine jars of lamp oil, four reeds, a basket of clay scraped clean of other people's sentences.  No one had been paid since the king died, so she scraped tablets and reused them, and the archive thinned week by week.  Frugality was not a virtue in her but an instrument, more trusted than the gnomon, more trusted than the gods. 

One of the tablets she had scraped was Kidin's.  He went north with the surveyors in spring, the road swallowed him, and his last letter, which said ordinary things about bread, had seemed worth less than the clay beneath it.  She kept his numbers, because numbers cost nothing to keep.  Now, in the emptied throne room, where looters had pried the eyes out of the glazed lions, she recomputed his moon.  The observation could not belong to the day the army said he vanished.  It belonged to a night eleven days later.  It was an empirically ironic mercy: her arithmetic had eaten his handwriting and given him back eleven nights.  Eleven nights she could prove and never spend. 

She could not write to him, since the unknown has no address.  She could not write plainly either, because the next regime would read every plain word in the palace and burn whatever it disliked.  So she made a tile.  Nine cells, three by three: four, nine, two across the top, and fifteen every way you crossed them, row, column, diagonal, a small dusky harmony no censor could accuse of anything.  Whoever checked those sums would read the constant as a day, the fifteenth, the night they had promised each other on the roof.  The center cell held five, the still house, the pole around which every wandering thing turns.  Her devotion went in encrypted, and thrift was the reason: words would have needed a hundred tablets and a courier, while a square fit her palm and one firing.  She glazed it the blue of the hour when the lions turn the color of water. 

On a scraped scrap she cut schematic representations, since diagrams outlive the languages that label them.  The throne room became a rectangle, the water clock a stepped vessel, the gnomon a single stroke with its shadow's length written beside it, the nine cells nine houses of the sky.  In the rectangle she hatched one small square: the tile's place in the floor, at the hour the clerestory light would cross it.  She filed the scrap among the eclipse tables, where any astronomer of any regime would eventually look.  Then she cloaked the tile in a slip of plain mud and set it among the hundred floor tiles the looters had not bothered to lift. 

The covenant was not with Kidin, who might be ash too, but with the unknown: whoever kept the sky, in whatever tongue, under whatever king.  Her side of it was the numbers, and a promise to watch on every fifteenth until someone answered.  Their side, whoever they were, was to check the sums. 

On the fortieth dusk she knelt and uncloaked it, thumbnailing the dried mud away in flakes.  The light came through the clerestory exactly as her diagram had promised and lay down across the nine cells.  Fifteen, every way.  The blue held; the harmony held; nothing in the room said whether a man in the north was alive.  That was the term she had bargained for, and she signed it by leaving the tile where the sky could read it.  At dawn the governor's clerks would come with their inventories, so she cloaked it again, gently, the way you cover a sleeper.  Then she went up to the roof to wait for the fifteenth, and did not light a lamp.  Eight jars of oil left, and a sky that worked for nothing.
