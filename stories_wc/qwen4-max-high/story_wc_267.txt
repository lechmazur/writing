The fortress was cobalt under the snow, glazed brick fired blue three centuries ago, and from the valley it looked like something drowning slowly.  Bek had carried bags up its spiral staircase for eleven winters in felt slippers, without a word.  He was the last bellhop of the Silent Choir. 

The choir had sung once, on the feast of Saint Lucia, and the cornice above the village let go, and forty-one people were buried inside a hymn.  Afterward the fortress took in the frostbitten and kept quiet, and its servants vowed quiet with it.  The chapel bell was sold for scrap, the hinges greased weekly, the doors left ajar so no latch would click.  Bek kept a ledger of it: forty-one names on the left page, what sound had cost, and on the right what silence cost, because he wanted, more than pardon, to understand the cost of safety.  The right page had stayed empty eleven years. 

The girl in room nine was Mira, and her hands had lain in the snow six hours before the dogs found her.  Now the nerves were knitting, and during nerve regeneration the pain arrives before the feeling, a bell rung in a room nobody has entered yet.  The physician gave her nine weeks: fingers that learn nothing while the nerves knit learn nothing ever.  He said the hall piano was the best therapy in the fortress, and then he said that of course the piano was not permitted.  Bek had stood at the foot of her bed and said nothing, and that was the unpardonable silence, the kind no vow covers, the kind a man chooses while a child's hands go quiet forever. 

He had also, the winter after the avalanche, taken the bolt out of the piano bench, one hexagonal inch of iron, so the bench would fold and no one would ever sit.  He carried it in his breast pocket against his heart, and called it maintenance. 

He found her out the way the Silent Choir found everything, via the slightest discoloration on a dusty spiral staircase: a pale smear on the seventh tread, another on the ninth, dust wiped where a sleeve had dragged.  He followed the marks up past the shuttered floors to the music room.  Mira was on her knees at the piano, trying to lever herself onto the folded bench, and her hands would not hold her.  In the dust of the keyboard lid she had written one word: REMAIN.  Not help, not play; remain.  She did not want to be lifted; she wanted a witness who would not leave the room while she was useless in it.  So he stayed in the doorway and watched her fail, which was the first honest thing he had done in eleven years. 

Then he knelt and threaded the bolt home, and the bench stood.  She sat.  What she played was seraphically mundane: five notes up, five notes down, an angel at its exercises, slow, wrong, weeping.  Above the room the cornice held its breath, and a sheet of snow slid off the roof with the sound of a page turning.  Bek opened his mouth and hummed the sixth note, the one her thumb could not reach.  His voice came out rust, and the vow broke like ice in a bucket.  She found the note.  Then she lost it, and found it, and lost it, and the snow did not come down. 

The cornice might still let go tomorrow, or in the thaw, and they would pay whatever the mountain asked.  Safety was not free; he had simply been paying for it in other people's hands, and calling the receipt peace.  That night he opened the ledger and wrote on the right page, under eleven years of nothing: one piano, eleven winters, a girl's two hands, almost.  It did not balance.  Forty-one names never balance.  But the column was no longer empty, and in the morning he would carry her bag up to the music room, and she would climb the stairs herself, and he would remain.  Eight weeks left, and the snow still on the blue walls, and the light coming through it the color of a bruise healing.
