The lighthouse kept turning after the last keeper left, and nobody watched it anymore.  Rana watched the trees instead.  The arboretum stood on a cliff above cold water, forty acres of wind-bent pines and trial beds, and the keeper's cottage was its seed house now.  It existed to find out what would live on this coast, which was a question Rana could stand behind.  She had come here in October, eleven months after the wedding.  Not her wedding.  Her cousin's, in a courtyard under a lemon tree strung with bulbs, where a drone had heard forty people celebrating and decided they were something else.  She had lived under a folding table.  Eleven people had not.  Her brother had signed her into the cliff program because the extractor fan in her flat sounded like something hovering.  Bea, who ran the program, walked the gravel paths with her twice a week and asked what her body was doing.  Not what she remembered, but what her jaw did, her gut, the small muscles behind her knees.  Somatic markers, Bea called them: the body's weather report, older than language and usually right.  Ivo, the head gardener, called her quietly talented and then went back to his pruning, which was how she preferred praise.  Her grafts took.  Nine in ten, when the nursery average was six.  In her chest pocket she carried a crumpled photograph of the courtyard, the only one that survived, creased so long that the folds had gone soft as cloth.  Her aunt had mailed it, along with three cuttings from the lemon tree, which had lost its lights and none of its branches.  The tree lived.  The people did not.  Rana could not make those two facts argue, so she stopped trying.  In November the coast survey sent a drone along the cliff to measure erosion.  Rana heard it from the trial beds and froze.  Not fear first, just lock: knees gone to wood, breath parked at the top of her lungs, the trowel held out like an offering.  Bea crouched beside her and did not touch her.  "Name three things," she said.  Rana's thumb was on the photograph through the fabric of her jacket, and she felt the crease, and the salt grit on the trowel handle, and her heel against gravel.  Three markers, all of them here, all of them now.  Inside the lock she found a small hard point that was not moving, and she stopped fighting it.  A pearl of stillness, Bea had called it once, and Rana had laughed, because a pearl is only an oyster's answer to grit, a wound wrapped in smooth layers until it can be borne.  Not healing.  Coating.  She moved one finger, then the trowel, then her heel.  The drone went north, and the sea kept doing what it does to cliffs.  That afternoon she took the lemon cuttings out of the seed house fridge, where they had waited in damp paper, and carried them to the bench.  Lemon on its own roots would die here in the first salt gale; everyone at the arboretum knew it.  So she cut into a trifoliate rootstock, sour and thorny and hardy to twenty below, and set her cousin's wood into the wound and bound it tight.  Ivo looked at the binding and said nothing, which was his applause.  "One in three," she told Bea.  "The graft may take and the salt may take it after, and I am not going to pretend the odds are better."  Bea asked why plant it at all.  "Because one in three is a real number," Rana said, "and I would rather hold a real number than a beautiful lie."  She pinned the photograph above the bench, crumpled, unflattened, the creases running through the lemon tree and through the place where the bride had been standing.  Smoothing it would have been a lie too.  In February she carried the pot to the walled bed below the light, where the pines broke the wind, and set it in the only shelter the cliff allowed.  The beam swung over her every twelve seconds, automatic, unwatched.  Somebody had to keep something here, so she kept the graft, and the count, and the small hard stillness she could find now in the middle of any lock.  In April, one bud pushed.  She wrote the date on the label, and did not write anything else.
