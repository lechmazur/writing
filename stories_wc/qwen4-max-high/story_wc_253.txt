The giant turbines came to the ridge the spring after the Happiness Mandate, and their blades pressed a new hush down over the farmhouse.  The old quiet had a shape; this one had a schedule.  The performer climbed out of his van into the thrum, carrying a knife, a sack of pegs, and his license.  The license named him a Domestic Epicist, Class Two, permitted to make grief light enough to carry legally.  The mandate had not abolished grief; it had abolished weight, and he was licensed to lift what remained.  His face did not move when he smiled; it had not moved in eleven years, which was why widowers requested him by name. 

Bram met him at the door with an apron on.  "They audit sound," Bram said.  "They audit sound," the performer agreed, "so we will give them a hero boiling water." 

In the kitchen the kettle sat cold, and the table held two plates, one of them set out of habit.  Their shadows crossed the kitchen every four seconds, and Bram had stopped noticing.  On the sill stood a bouquet gone to paper, funeral flowers dried to the color of weak tea.  Keeping them was a violation; the mandate required the bereaved to compost their mourning by the fortieth day.  Bram saw him look, and said nothing, and the turbines said nothing loudly. 

The performer took a peg from his sack and began to whittle it, shaving the wood thin so his hands would keep a rhythm.  Then he opened his voice like a door.  "Behold the kettle," he said, "which has held nothing since March, and which is now asked to hold the whole Atlantic."  Bram's mouth twitched.  "He fills it to the third rivet, as she taught him, because the fourth rivet sings, and the singing is unbearable."  The tap ran, and he narrated the tap.  He narrated the match, the blue crown of the burner, the bread going under the knife like a small surrender.  Bram laughed once, a cracked sound, and then his shoulders began to go.  Laughter is permitted; the shoulders were the risky part, so the performer raised his voice to cover them, and whittled, and did not look at the man's face.  That was the service: to make the ordinary enormous until the enormous became ordinary, and grief could ride on top like a child on a hay cart. 

Bram had paid for two hours; at the ninetieth minute he nodded at the sill.  "Do those," he said.  "They are hers."  "The inspectors come Thursday," Bram said, "and I cannot put them in the fire myself." 

The performer weighed the bouquet, which weighed nothing, and named a price for the extra verse.  The price was high, and he watched Bram pay it, and felt the small clean click of a transaction, which was the nearest thing he had to feeling.  "Behold the roses," he said, "which drank a whole summer and refused to die on schedule."  He fed them stem by stem into the stove.  "Behold the wire that held them, which outlasted the wedding."  The paper petals went up orange and were gone in a breath, and Bram cried without sound, which the turbines swallowed. 

When the stove door closed, one sprig was in his fist, unburned, held like a coin.  He did not tell Bram.  In the van there were forty-one others, each from a different sill, each labeled with a date in his neat hand.  A collection is a way of holding a thing without being held by it.  He told himself the sprigs were inventory, tools of the trade, though he had never once used them.  Any inspector who found the drawer could ruin forty-two households, and he kept it locked, and called that prudence. 

Under the turbines he drove out, and the thrum filled the cab: the old hush remade, the same silence with a machine inside it.  He narrated his own hands on the wheel, the gravel, the gate, the mile of dark corn, in a voice like a cathedral.  Nobody laughed, and nobody was lightened.  He kept narrating anyway, all the way home, to see whether anything in him would rise to meet it.
