Between the fire-eater's last swallow of flame and my own set, I had twenty minutes, and I spent them the way I always did: counting.  Sixteen people at the rail, four at the dead claw machine, one man at the eighth pillar where he had stood for eleven nights.  The arcade had lost its glass roof to a February storm, so the stars sat in the iron ribs above us and the sea slid under the boards.  Everything smelled of salt and hot pennies.  Between acts the crowd comes unshaped, and that is when the real work is done. 

I am a street performer, and people tell me I am too analytical for it.  I keep a ledger of footfall, of coughs, of which songs make a woman touch her own throat.  My mother left me a chess set with every piece missing but the bishop, ivory, its mitre worn smooth.  A bishop never leaves its color, she said.  That is not a cage; that is a road. 

This town has one street, one chapel, one song the drunk men ask for, and a tide that comes in and goes out and calls that a life.  So I stand on the diagonal tiles and let three dice in a cracked cup decide: the first chooses the key, the second the tempo, the third which pillar I face.  What the crowd hears is wilderness, a saw bent under a bow, wailing like something lost in the dunes.  Every wail is arithmetic.  I serenade that monotony with something that sounds like accident, and I make it by ordering randomness into a shape that feels like fate. 

I am lightly attentive, and that is the real trick.  I never stare; I rest my gaze near people the way you rest a hand near a moth.  Unguarded, they tell me everything: which knee aches, which song their mother sang. 

The man at the eighth pillar hums four notes and has never once spoken.  He requests nothing, which is why I wanted him.  Tonight the dice gave me a slow waltz in a minor key, facing his pillar.  I had loaded nothing; I had simply rolled the cup until that sequence came up, and then stopped, which is its own kind of loading.  The girl who refills the claw machine sings while she works, and I had written down in April that she sings those four notes.  Three days ago I let a rumor drift to her that the machine pays best at slack water. 

At nine fifty-one the tide turned.  She came to the eighth pillar with her bag of tokens, because rumor goes where you point it.  I lifted the bow.  The saw found the waltz, the waltz found her humming, and the man's four notes rose to meet it. 

He said, "That's my wife's."  He said it aloud, to a stranger, under stars, in a flooded arcade, and the girl answered, "Mine too," which was a lie she offered kindly, and he laughed for the first time in eleven nights. 

Serendipity, orchestrated.  Nothing here happens; things are arranged until they resemble happening, and the resemblance is the gift. 

Then he turned to me and said, "You did this."  I said yes, because I have never lied in my ledger.  He had run these rides for thirty years, and a man who watches queues all night learns to see the hand that shapes them. 

"Eleven nights," he said.  "You've been standing on your diagonals, moving us like pieces, and never once coming straight at anybody."  He nodded at the cracked cup on my case.  "Play something you didn't plan." 

The tide was coming through the grates, and I could feel the whole board of the evening, every square accounted for.  I had built it so that nothing could ever happen to me. 

I set the bishop down on the wet tile, on its own color, and left it there.  For the first time in eleven nights I did not know what the next bar would be.  Then I put the bow to the saw and played the first thing my hands wanted, which was ugly, and too fast, and mine. 

The man sang, badly.  The girl laughed.  Somebody's tokens went into the water and nobody moved to save them.  I did not write it down.  Let it happen.
