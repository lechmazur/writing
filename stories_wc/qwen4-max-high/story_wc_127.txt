The grotto sweated at dusk, and Ilse Varga laid out her petals on the warm stone.  They were fragrance chemist's blotters, cut into five-petaled stars, and each one could hold a single drop of somebody's feeling long enough to be judged.  Light came through forty feet of old resin and arrived the color of tea, and everything in the walls had once been alive. 

Ilse sold counterfeit emotion, and she was professionally thorough about it.  Forty-one assays per batch, logged in a ledger gone soft with resin dust.  Once she had sold an untested batch, and a girl had laughed at her mother's grave for a week.  The thoroughness was not vanity; a pure lie does nothing at all.  Every dose she sold was a half truth: one part genuine feeling, nine parts filler, the true part doing the work while the false part kept the buyer safe.  She was fond of her buyers, who wanted to feel correctly rather than truly, and she never once corrected them.  Amber was the same arrangement, which was why she worked here.  A real wasp, a false forever. 

She rubbed a bead of the wall against her wool sleeve and held a petal near it.  Truth carries charge; filler does not.  The petal leaned toward the amber and touched it, and she logged the ratio with the fondness a jeweler keeps for a flawed stone. 

The man came down the scree path while the light was still orange.  Teodor, a ferryman, widower of eleven days, wanting grief.  He had buried his wife in front of her sisters and felt nothing, and the nothing was ruining him.  Ilse liked him immediately, in her way, which was the way of a woman who likes weather. 

She held a clean petal to his mouth, told him to breathe his wife's name onto it, and rubbed the amber.  The petal jumped hard enough to sting her thumb.  He was full of grief; it had simply frozen too deep to thaw on its own.  So she sold him a counterfeit, a cheap one, seeded with a stranger's true sorrow at the ratio of one to nine.  The false grief would go in first and find the real grief and shake it awake, and he would weep for his own wife while believing he had bought it.  A half truth, honestly priced. 

He paid, and drank, and his face came apart.  Ilse watched with great tenderness and no feeling whatsoever.  That was her condition, and had been since childhood: she could assay any heart in the world and her own stayed inert.  Insight without empathy, a scale with nothing on it.  She had spent her working life trying to fuse the two, and the grotto had been her best attempt.  No buyer of counterfeits ever wept an undiluted tear, and she had waited a long time for a grief that was real and only asleep. 

At dusk the resin ran, and the resin subsumed whatever stayed.  It had taken her boots in spring, her left hand in summer, and now it stood at her collarbone, warm as a mouth.  She had told no one, and had kept the ledger current, because someone would have to price the stock after her. 

Now she asked Teodor for one thing more, and he gave it, being grateful and wrecked.  She held a fresh petal under his eye and caught one tear, whole and undiluted.  Then she pressed it into the resin over her heart.  The amber closed over her chin while he climbed back toward the ferry, and she was glad for him, in the fond, dry way she had. 

As the transformation completed, the charge came up through the paper and into her.  She had known grief's every ratio; now she also had it, a ferryman's whole sorrow arriving in a woman who could finally read what she was holding.  Insight and empathy, fused at last, one second wide.  It was not counterfeit, and it was not hers, and the amber took it as it took the wasp: the true thing kept, the living thing stopped.  A half truth, forever, honestly priced.
