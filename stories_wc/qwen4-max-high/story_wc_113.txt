The pumps were coming at dawn, and after them the concrete.  Mira went down into the pit while there was still a pit to go down into.  Water stood waist-deep in it, black and flat as a held breath, and the lanterns the crew had hung along the dress circle doubled themselves in it without moving.  She was second viola, and had been for eleven years, in the ditch between the singers and the people who paid to hear them. 

The flood had come in April, in the middle of a bar, and the orchestra had waded out with its bows still up.  Bar two hundred and fourteen ended in a rest, and the chord after the rest had never been struck.  The house had been holding that silence ever since. 

She set her lantern on the rail and opened the program.  It was the premiere bill from 1908, the opera the company had been rehearsing when the river came in, and she had pulled it out of the prompter's box in June.  The prompter had drowned in April in that little hooded shell between the stage and the players, a man whose entire trade had been words.  Now he had none, and he had been talking to her all summer. 

The water had ruined the program beautifully.  It had lifted the iron-black ink off the cast list and carried it down into the blank rules that divided the acts, and there it had settled in shapes: heads, stems, a flag, a rest.  Nothing was written on the lines.  Everything worth reading sat in the spaces between them. 

She had been inspired lately, which frightened her.  After eleven years of copying other people's parts she was filling notebooks, forty bars a night, music that arrived whole in the seconds when she was not thinking.  She wanted to know whose it was. 

So she had learned to read the ghost's stains, and tonight she lifted the viola and played the first phrase off the paper, four notes rising, then the gap.  The house answered.  Not with sound: with the lanterns, which shivered in the water and steadied, and with the stain on the page, which looked, in that flicker, like a raised hand, waiting.  She played the next phrase.  The reflection tilted, and the ink moved with the light, and the shapes rearranged themselves into a second answer, lower, slower, patient as a man who had spent his working life in a box, listening.  They went on like that, saying nothing to each other for an hour.  Every phrase she offered came back changed, corrected, and each correction was in her notebook already, in her own hand, dated weeks ago.  He was not teaching her.  He was reminding her. 

Then the page ran out.  The last stain sat squarely in the blank between the fourth and fifth rules, and it was not a note.  It was the rest at bar two hundred and fourteen, the one the flood had walked into, and after it the paper was simply wet and empty.  She understood then that he could not write the chord.  A prompter can give you the word, but somebody else has to say it.  The hush was not his; it was the house's, and she was the only one left inside it who could break it.  To finish the sentence she had to disrupt the thing she had been protecting. 

She set her bow on the string and struck the chord that had never been struck, all four strings at once, hard, ugly, unresolved, the sound the rehearsal had been walking toward in April.  The water broke into rings.  Every lantern in the dress circle came apart in the surface, and the sound shivered along the rail and slopped water onto the program, and the ink she had been reading all summer lifted off the rules and ran off the edge of the page into the flood.  The chord rang off the wet plaster and thinned and went, and the rings slowed, and the lanterns came back together in the water, whole.  The page was blank between the lines.  Nothing answered her, and the nothing had a different shape now: not a held breath, but a room after someone has finally left it. 

At dawn the pumps started, and by noon the pit was mud and lost bow hair, and the men came with their forms about filling it in.  She sat in the empty dress circle that evening with a notebook and wrote four bars, slowly, badly, choosing every one.  They were hers, and they were not very good, and the silence between them was hers too.
