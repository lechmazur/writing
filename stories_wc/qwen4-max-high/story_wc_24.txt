After blowing the whistle, Marit stood in the doorway of the horizon measurement station and let the sound go on without her.  The fjord took it the way it took everything, without rippling.  Eleven seconds later the far cliff handed it back, thinner. 

She was the fjord's hydraulic clock shepherd, and her forty clocks were moored in the shallows below the station like patient animals, and not one of them agreed about when the echo came home.  Their floats rose and fell on the tide, their siphons dripped, their needles wrote forty different answers on forty paper drums.  The spread was two seconds, which was normal, and which she had never once been able to forgive.  The survey office wanted the mean of the flock, a number no single clock had ever said. 

Halvard, who had kept the station before her, had written in the margin of his last log: the clocks do not measure time.  She had taken it for the wandering of an old man's pen.  She had also taken his key.  It was a mantle clock key, small brass, the bit worn round as a river stone, and it fit nothing in the station but the clock on the shelf above the barometer, listed in Halvard's inventory as eminently peculiar.  Among forty clocks that ran on falling water, that one ran on a wound spring, and it was the only one Halvard had ever refused to correct. 

Marit went out along the causeway with her crook and nudged kelp from the sluices, and the water made the small sound water makes when it is not being asked anything.  She came in and dried her hands, still hearing the count she had used at noon, the way he had taught her, through counting backwards.  Forward counting always arrives late; you chase the instant and reach only the place where it has been.  Backwards, from ten down, you meet it; zero arrives with you at the exact edge of the thing. 

The count was for the sun's lower limb touching the sea line at noon, the one observation the station existed to make.  In December the sun here never climbed; it came up to the sea line and stopped there, like a visitor who will not enter.  The telescope carried a razor edge at its focus, a blade of blackened steel that sliced the horizon into a clean before and after.  You had to squint or the line frayed; the eye, wide open, is generous, and generosity ruins a measurement.  Ten, nine, eight, she had counted, squinting, and the sea line came up under the blade like something rising to be judged.  Zero, and the whistle, and the sun's edge kissing the water and going no further. 

Now she turned to the shelf, because the question had been standing behind her all morning.  The little clock had stopped.  Not at dusk, as it should have, but now, with the short day still half unspent.  She fitted the key to the arbor and felt the slack come up through the brass: two turns that morning, not three.  She had skipped the third on purpose, impatient with a dead man's riddle.  Around her the station held its fjord quiet, a silence with weather in it. 

Halvard had wound it three turns every morning for sixty years, no more, so that it would run one day and then end.  Not because he could not wind it further, but because he wanted a clock that could be used up.  The water clocks measured the fjord leaving, drop by drop through the sluices to the sea; they would run as long as the moon pulled, and they never had to be paid.  The spring clock measured what was put into it.  It measured her hand, her morning, her attention, and it stopped when she stopped giving.  That was what razor edges were for, she thought: not to divide the day, but to show that an instant has no width and cannot be stored, only spent. 

She sat with the stopped clock while the light on the water went from grey to no colour at all.  In the log she wrote the dip, the refraction, the eleven seconds, the two-second spread of the flock.  Under Halvard's note she wrote: they measure what leaves.  Then she gave the little clock its third turn, late, and heard the spring take it.  Outside, forty clocks went on subtracting the fjord.  She stood in the doorway and counted backwards from ten, and met the evening exactly at its edge.
