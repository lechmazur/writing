The prep room hummed all night, and the dead never once looked dead.  Nadia worked the night shift at Kolar Funeral Home because the living never asked her for favors.  Tonight her father lay on the porcelain table under a sheet, and she had already set his features.  The arterial fluid had put a false bloom in his cheeks, a pink he had never earned.  He had sold hope in this city for thirty years and never once delivered it.  Political asylum, mostly: affidavits, arrest warrants, the beautiful stamps of ministries that no longer existed.  Nadia, the honest one, had kept his ledger since she was eleven, in a school notebook.  She wanted the mathematics of a fair trade: what a stolen thousand owed a stolen year.  The columns never closed.  At midnight she telephoned the last name in the ledger, and at two Sabina came down the service stairs with a removal notice folded in her fist.  Nine years ago she had paid Nadia's father four thousand dollars to make her persecution legible to a clerk.  He had taken her real documents, her brother's death certificate, the union summons, and improved them.  The consulate found his invented watermark, called her a fraud, and closed the door.  Her flight was Thursday.  Nadia had found the note in his coat that afternoon, in his hand: the well holds the warranty.  The well was his ink pot, a vintage glass thing from the nineteen twenties, heavy as a heart, its brass lid sealed with wax.  He had pressed that wax with a stolen consular seal, so that even his hoarding looked official.  Under a cake of dried ink, she believed, he kept the originals, his leverage against refunds.  As a girl she had shaken it and heard paper whisper, not liquid.  A scalpel would breach the wax in one second and shred the paper beneath it.  Her father would have done exactly that, then sold the pieces.  Instead she filled the machine's basin with warm water and set the pot in it, lid up, like a tooth in a glass.  Then she waited actively, which is the only honest kind.  She stirred the water every ten minutes to keep it warm.  She sponged her father's hands and clipped his nails and combed the hair he had pomaded for courtrooms.  Beside her, Sabina wrote the truth in pencil: dates, cells, the smell of the corridor, nothing improved.  Twice Nadia wanted to fix a sentence that sounded weak, and twice she put the pencil down.  Weak was what truth sounded like.  By four the wax had gone soft as a lip.  Nadia breached it with her thumbnail and lifted out three folded papers, dry as leaves, entirely real.  The ink cake below them was still hard, so she added water and kept waiting.  The heat liquefied it slowly, a black bloom climbing the glass, and something in Nadia liquefied with it.  Grief is a solvent, she decided; it liquefies rigid thinking.  Her columns, her interest, her dream of a debt closed by payment went to slurry beside a man whose face was pinker than hers.  Nothing he owed could be paid back, because what he took was time, and time does not accept cash.  A fair trade was not an equation.  It was an inequality you agreed to carry for the rest of your life.  He had spent Sabina's fee in a single summer, so Nadia wrote a check against her own savings, the money for a car she had wanted since she was seventeen.  Then she dipped his pen into his ink and wrote her own affidavit, naming herself, naming the years she had typed his letters and known.  That page could take her license and her freedom; she signed it anyway.  Sabina read it twice and said, in her own language, a word Nadia did not need translated.  The forger's ink had never been used for anything true.  It dried black and permanent while the man who had never told the truth cooled on the porcelain behind her.  At eight she dressed him for the ground; at nine she stood with Sabina at the legal aid door, first in line, the originals flat in a clean folder.  There would be months of this, years maybe, and no guarantee at the end.  Nadia had learned the method from the ink: soften, stir, keep the water warm, and do the work while you wait.  Behind her the city smelled of rain and exhaust, unbearably alive.  In her bag the empty ink pot caught the light, clean glass at last, holding nothing back.
