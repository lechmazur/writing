The tower city hung over the gorge on cables the width of a wrist, nine hundred rusted platforms strung between two cliffs like beads on a dropped necklace.  Nobody had broadcast from it in forty years, which was why Naima Quist liked working there.  She was a celestial signal interpreter, licensed, bonded, and behind on her rent.  Her tools rode in a canvas roll: grease pencil, tuning wrench, two clamps, and a chunk of Hollywood sidewalk.  The fragment was terrazzo with a brass letter still in it, half an M, salvaged from a star on a boulevard nobody remembered.  Brass of that old alloy rang at a known pitch, so interpreters used it as a reference, a fixed note to measure the sky against.  It was the cheapest honest standard left: a dead industry's souvenir, still true. 

Tonight the moon was at perigee, close enough that the gorge exhaled and the cables hummed, and the sky's faint traffic pressed down within reach.  She had one hour. 

At the city's heart, in the master throat where every antenna's cable once converged, somebody had plugged the feed with a shard of hush.  It looked like grey glass and it weighed nothing, and around it the wind stopped mid-noise.  The last crew had jammed it in during the Panic, when the towers began screaming, and the city had gone silent so thoroughly that even the gulls left.  Naima had been nine, listening on a rooftop set, and she had heard the scream cut off mid-word.  Everyone else heard an ending; she had spent thirty years insisting it was an interrupted beginning, and the guild had stopped returning her calls. 

She clipped her safety line, set the lamp, and opened her ledger to a fresh page.  Then she did the only thing her trade actually required: she started throwing away what she knew. 

Assumption one, that the hush was a plug.  Plugs block, but components carry, and this one sat in the circuit like a valve. 

Assumption two, that silence was empty.  She laid two fingers on the shard and felt it: serenely electric, a calm current with no heat and no hurry, like a battery that had been waiting patiently for forty years.  Empty things do not hold a charge. 

Assumption three, that the sky spoke in words.  She crossed out her phonetic charts, the alphabet grids, thirty years of decoding scaffolding, and left only the raw question: what is moving, and how fast. 

Outside, the moon pulled the gorge's air upward and the dead dishes tilted a degree, groaning.  Naima set the Hollywood fragment against the shard's flat face, brass to glass, and struck the terrazzo with her wrench.  The old star rang its one reliable note.  She had come to tune the shard, not the towers.  That was assumption four, gone: she had spent her career aiming dishes at the sky when the only instrument in the city that could hear was the thing everyone had used to stop hearing. 

She slid the brass along the shard's face, shortening the contact the way a finger shortens a string, until the calm current climbed into the star's note.  The hush went taut, and the silence in the throat became a listening silence instead of a dead one.  Through it, at perigee, with the moon leaning close, she heard what the shard had been holding all along. 

Not the scream.  Under the scream, faint and enormous, an intake of breath: something vast drawing air before its first word, slowed to a crawl by distance, still arriving.  The towers had not been dying; they had been the first to hear it, and the noise had been their panic, and the hush had been a mercy, a muffler pulled over a sound too big for forty-year-old equipment.  The ending she had mourned was a beginning nobody had built room for. 

Naima let the brass rest, wrote the time, the pitch, and the moon's distance, and did not cry, because the ledger needed steady handwriting.  Whatever was speaking had been about to speak for four decades, and it would keep arriving; perigee was a door, not a deadline.  She climbed down to the cable house and filed a requisition by lamplight: two miles of copper, six clamps, a new throat housing, one apprentice who could be taught to discard assumptions.  Under Reason for Work, she wrote the only thing she had ever believed: something up there is starting, and it deserves a place to be heard.
