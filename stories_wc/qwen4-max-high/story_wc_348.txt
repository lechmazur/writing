The ravine below Kelso Fork braided itself into nine channels that October, and Mira heard them the way she heard everything, as sections entering one at a time.  Strings first, then the woodwinds of the alders, then the gravel bars holding their long rests.  She was a polymer chemist, and her lab spent its days trying to make two plastics that hated each other hold hands.  Polyethylene and nylon, oil and water in solid form, would rather crack than blend.  Her mother had conducted a youth orchestra for thirty years, so Mira thought in movements before she thought in equations. 

She had come back to the ravine with a failed sample in her jacket and her brother's ashes in a film canister.  Jonas had been eleven and she nineteen the last time they stood here.  The water forked, she chose the left braid because it looked faster, and she called back over her shoulder for him to follow.  The undercut bank gave way under his sneakers.  She pulled him out in ninety seconds, and ninety seconds was long enough to take his speech and his cello.  For seventeen years he lived in the back room of their mother's house, and Mira sent money from three time zones and visited at Christmas.  Money was the wrong solvent; it dissolved nothing.  He died in March, and her mother said, in a voice with no vibrato left, you were the one who chose the path.  Her mother would not come to the scattering.  So Mira stood alone at the confluence and shook him out into the foam, and the foam carried him downstream in white lace. 

Then, because she was who she was, she sat on wet gravel and looked at her failure.  The sample was a gray bar that had snapped clean at the weld, the two plastics refusing each other even in ruin.  For two years she had tried to force them into one substance, milling them finer, hotter, faster, homogenizing until the mixture looked like agreement.  It always cracked at the boundary, because there was still a boundary.  She had been trying to erase the seam, the way she had spent seventeen years trying to erase one sentence shouted over her shoulder. 

The foam would not stay where she had poured it.  She took a stick and traced the foam, channel by channel, then copied its lines into her notebook.  The pattern was not random: the bubbles never gathered in the fast middle of any braid, but always along the seams where two channels leaned against each other and slowed.  Nine channels, one river; the separations were not damage, they were how the water carried its gravel.  And the foam, the fragile stuff, the residue, collected exactly at the interfaces, stitching the braids together with nothing but surface.  Stop milling them into sameness; build one chain with two faces, one end in love with each side, and let it live only in the seam.  Not a solvent, not an eraser: a translator, something that carries meaning across a boundary without dissolving either side. 

That was when she saw the chrysalis, hung under an alder leaf at the exact height of a child's eye.  Empty, jade-colored, split along a neat line at the shoulder, still holding the shape of what had left it.  Jonas used to find them for her, pockets full, and she would call the case a coffin, and he would shake his head, already sure she had it backwards.  Silk is a block copolymer too, she remembered: hard segments, soft segments, all its strength kept at the boundaries between them.  The split was not a wound; it was built in, a line of weakness designed so that something could leave and be met by air.  As the pieces fall into place, she thought, the score stops being separate staves and becomes one sound, and no instrument has to stop being itself.  Something in her chest went slack, the way a rope goes slack when the boat is finally tied. 

She put the chrysalis in her pocket beside the broken bar and drove to her mother's house.  She did not bring money.  She said the sentence she had been milling finer for seventeen years: I chose the left channel, and I told him to follow me, and I have never said it out loud to you.  Her mother listened the way she used to listen to a bad rehearsal, without stopping it.  Then she set the empty case on the table between them, and they laid their hands on either side of it, not touching.  She had come to atone, and atonement was not erasure; it was the seam, held.
