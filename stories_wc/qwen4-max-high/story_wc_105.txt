The greenhouse lay below the tideline, a glass bowl sunk in the old drydock, and the shipwrecks ringed it like a fence of ribs.  Inside, forty apple trees had outlived the fleet that was meant to carry them south.  Sanna knelt at the last tree with a knife and a strip of grafting tape.  The scion came from the tree her father planted the year she was born.  A graft knits in one warm night, while the cells divide and the cut faces forget they were ever separate.  She had until dawn. 

Sanna designed hearing aids, small shells of plastic and patience, and she was good at it because she heard colors faintly.  Cobalt hummed, rust muttered, and her father's voice had turned grey when she was nine.  Nine was the winter the ice took her brother.  Their father had tested the crossing, he said, and it was sound.  She had heard the grey in the sentence, and she had spent thirty years trying to build a device that would catch that frequency: the sound of trust breaking, so that no one else would have to take a promise on faith. 

Thirty years of failure had taught her that the break was never a noise.  So she had stopped collecting sounds and begun collecting absences.  She took her recorder into rooms and kept only what was missing: the half beat before a man said yes, the hole where a laugh should have been, the space a name leaves when someone decides not to say it.  Four hundred of them, catalogued by color.  Number one she had never recorded, because it lived only in memory, and memory keeps no waveform.  Tonight she had rebuilt it out of the sentence itself: his words, and the hole in the middle of them. 

The forge stood on the shingle above the dock, ashen and cold, its floor a grey drift of scale and coal dust.  Above it the aurora unrolled green and violet, and Sanna heard it the way she heard all color, a thin ringing at the edge of nothing.  Her father sat inside on the anvil block, where he had sat for sixty years, hands open on his knees.  He had followed her down as he did most nights, to be near the shape of work. 

She clamped the transducer to the anvil and set his palm flat on the iron, so the metal would carry the signal into his bones, and she played him absence number one.  The old man's face came apart.  "I couldn't hear it," he said. 

He had not heard anything in thirty years; the hammer had taken his ears the way the sea takes a shoreline, a little at a time, then all at once.  Ice sings before it fails, a high thin note, and he had lost that register before her brother was born.  He had said the crossing was sound because he could not hear it saying otherwise, and because his daughter was watching him, and he had wanted to be a man who knew. 

Sanna sat down in the ash.  Grey was not the color of a lie.  It was the color of a voice with nothing coming back to it, a man shouting into a room that had stopped answering, and she had built a whole life on misreading it.  The absence she had chased was not betrayal.  It was deafness, and shame, and it had been sitting on this anvil every night of her adulthood, waiting for her to ask. 

She could still imprint it, still make the warning she had promised herself, four hundred absences pressed into a chip that would tell the wearer when a promise went hollow.  Instead she opened her kit and imprinted his ear, the otoblock first, then the slow blue putty, the small cold intimacy of a man holding still for the daughter he had failed.  He did not ask what she was doing, and she did not explain, and that was the first trust either of them had offered in thirty years. 

The mold would need a week to become a shell.  Tonight she gave him the prototype, and turned it up, and said his name.  He wept without covering his face, which she had never seen him do.  When he answered, his voice was not grey at all but rust and cobalt, the forge and the water, and she stood in the ash and let the colors ring.  Below them, under glass, inside the ring of wrecks, the graft was knitting in the dark, cells dividing, two woods learning to be one.
