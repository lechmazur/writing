The Palace had been dark for eleven years, and it still hummed.  Vera heard it from the lobby: a low, patient thunder under the floor, the organ blower the crew had started that morning to test what could be salvaged. 

She was a crystal gazer by trade, which meant people paid her to look into a quartz sphere and tell them who was coming.  In truth she read objects, not futures; the sphere was only a lens, and a place to put her eyes when the touching got too loud.  She had come for the reason she never told clients: to find where unused love is stored.  Her husband had been dead four years, and the love had not gone with him; it sat in her chest like a held note, looking for somewhere to land. 

The demolition crew gave her one hour.  She spent it climbing to the projection booth.  Vera was spatially imaginative; she could hold a building in her head and walk it backward, so by the third landing she already knew the booth's shape: the rewind bench, the lamphouse, the little window aimed at the seats like a confession slot.  The smell met her first: hot dust, acetate, machine oil.  On the bench lay a film strip, spliced and respliced, its sprocket holes worn fuzzy. 

She laid two fingers on it.  A psychometry reading is not a vision; it is a room you are suddenly standing in, wearing someone else's hands.  This was the room she had already built in her head, and the hands were Ansel's, the last projectionist, and they were shaking.  He was photographing letters.  Goodbye letters, dozens of them, written to a woman who played the organ here and then married someone in another city.  He never mailed one.  Instead he shot each page frame by frame and spliced the frames into the tail of every reel, so that for thirty years the letters flashed past the audience at a twenty-fourth of a second apiece, too fast to read, too slow to be nothing.  Under the lamphouse heat they had incubated for thirty years, warm as eggs, never hatching. 

Vera set her sphere on the bench and drew the strip beneath it, and the quartz swelled the tiny photographed handwriting until she could read.  Dear Ruth.  I wrote the words you asked for.  I cannot write the tune.  The letters were lyrics, all of them, verse after verse of a song she had asked him for and he had never finished, because the melody lived in her hands and her hands had gone to another city.  So the words had waited in the dark, forgotten lyrics searching for a tune. 

Vera understood the storage then.  Unused love is not kept in the heart; it is kept in repetition, in a man running the same few frames through a machine ten thousand nights so that something in him could keep saying it.  She thought of the second cup she still set out each morning, the coat she still turned toward the door. 

Below, the trucks idled; the thunder came up through the seats.  The crew had run a line up for her hour, and the projector was still threaded.  She struck the lamp and the shutter began its hushed thunder, twenty-four soft detonations a second.  The letters flew out the little window and broke across a thousand empty seats.  Vera read them aloud from the strip, slowly, giving each word the time a person would need.  That was all the letters had ever wanted: not an answer, a receiver. 

Somewhere in the third verse her voice found the shutter's rhythm, and the rhythm found the blower's low pipe, and the words slid into the noise of the room and fit it.  The tune had been in the building all along, waiting for someone to sing the words at it.  She sang until the last verse. 

Then she took her own letter from her coat, four years folded, and read it to the empty house in the same voice.  It took nine minutes.  Nothing in her chest was stored anymore; it had all been spent on air, which is where love goes when it finally moves. 

She wound the film onto its reel, put it under her arm, and went down to tell the crew they could start.  Behind her the blower kept breathing its one low note, and she carried the tune out with the letters.
