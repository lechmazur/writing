The wind bells struck once, and the mist leaned in to swallow the sound.  He had cast them himself from the carousel's brass poles, and they rang a long time; a man could fit a whole small life inside one chime.  Emil stood in the carousel car with a curl of pine falling from his plane.  Around him the roundabout stood dismantled, its poles bundled like reeds, its mirrors furred with dust.  Beyond the doorway the other cars sat in the grass, their painted lions bleached to the color of bone.  He had not spoken in eleven years and did not consider himself robbed.  Silence was the block before the horse, everything still possible inside it.  Fog rose off the spring below the ridge each dawn and filled the train like slow milk.  It ate the scrape of the plane, the knock of his heel, the tick of the cooling rails.  The company had called it the misting hush, back when there was a company to call it anything.  The train had come here by mistake.  A switch thrown wrong at Cutter's Junction sent twelve cars down a spur the maps had forgotten, and the desert closed behind them.  Walking the wash for water, they found a hidden oasis instead: a spring under a rock shelf, cottonwoods, grass no boot had ever flattened.  The wrong turn saved them, then slowly emptied them; one by one the families walked out to the road.  Emil stayed, because the carousel was half carved, and he was quietly persistent the way water is persistent.  In his pocket he carried a halo fragment, a gilded crescent snapped from the angel above the canopy the night the cars tilted.  He had tried it against every head he carved, and it fit none of them.  A broken ring is not a smaller ring; it is a question.  The girl came up through the mist with her shoes in her hand, and the bells were still ringing.  She had found the place in April by taking the wrong fork of the wash, the same wrongness that had brought the train.  Since then she had come every Sunday, and every Sunday he answered with silence, and she kept coming.  Today her father's truck was packed for the coast, and she had come to say so.  She did not say it; she sang.  A counting song, thin and clear, the one the band used to play while the horses rose.  The hush took the edges of her voice and left the middle, so she sounded far away and very near at once.  Emil set down the plane.  He had kept his voice the way he kept the gold: not lost, only unspent.  He put the halo fragment into her hand and closed her fingers over it.  "It's broken," she said.  He shook his head and turned the crescent in her palm until its two ends pointed at her, like arms.  Mist light ran along the gilt and the whole car glimmered, and she stopped holding it like something hurt.  Then Emil did the thing he had not done since the cars came off the rail.  He sang.  One note, low, cracked at the start, riding under hers the way ground rides under a river.  The mist took it and gave some of it back.  She laughed without losing the tune, and sang louder, and he found the next note waiting where the first had been.  This was what he had meant all along by saying nothing: that the empty bar is not the end of the music.  The bells were thinning now, the brass letting go of the last of its ring.  She would go to the coast and take the open gold with her, and he would stay and carve.  Inside the chime there was still room for one more verse, and they took it.  Then the sound went out like a coal in water, and the hush closed over the train.  Emil lifted the plane, and in the silence he could hear the horse he had not carved yet.
