The workshop floated because it was being eaten.  Acid gnawed the zinc plates beneath the hull, and as each plate thinned the pontoons rose a finger's width, and the ratchets took up the slack, and the great cogs turned.  Oblivion was the engine; Ozias had built it that way on purpose.  Wren had never asked him why, and now the sea had him, and asking was impossible.  She was an alchemist the way a cracked bell is a bell.  Every transmutation she attempted she tested twice, then a third time, certain only of her doubt.  Ozias had mended intentions, not metals.  People came to the floating workshop with broken vows and half-dead purposes, and he seamed them with gold and sent them home carrying their fractures visibly.  Gold does not hide the break, he said; it admits it, and stays.  He would make them say the broken vow aloud and lay the leaf along the place where the voice failed.  Wren's own intention had snapped the morning the fume took him: she had meant to succeed him, and now she meant to let the workshop sink.  To keep from forgetting him, she quilted memories together.  Scraps of his coat, her mother's sleeve, the sail from the day they first cast off; each patch held a scene, and the fumes ate any scene left unspoken.  So she narrated the quilt aloud, patch by patch, while the cogs counted the plates dissolving below.  It was the only spell she trusted: saying a thing kept it.  On the ninth night the ratchets began to slip.  The zinc was nearly gone, no barge had come with more, and the hull rode so low that acid licked the floorboards and left them glassy.  In the crucible lay Ozias's last gold, enough to sheathe the hull against the acid forever.  A gilded workshop would never sink and never turn: nothing eaten, nothing said, nothing meant.  Wren could not choose between that flawless death and the plain one.  In Ozias's drawer she found his key ring, its key long dissolved, only the charm left: a bronze disc the size of a thumbnail, punched with pits.  He had told her once, laughing, that when she could not decide she should go and match the pockmarks.  Below the workshop, in the drowned city, stood the founder: a statue four centuries old, her stone face eaten into a constellation.  The sea ran thin there, Ozias used to say, thin enough to survive one breath's worth.  She greased her skin with tallow, took the charm in her fist, and went down into the green.  Everything after that happened between breaths held underwater.  Cold clamped her chest; the fume-lit water showed her the founder's cheek, pocked like a moon.  Wren pressed the charm to the stone and slid it, pit against pit, the way Ozias had taught her to read a seam.  It caught.  Every hole in the bronze sat over a wound in the stone except three, and those three sat over gold.  Someone had filled three of the founder's pockmarks with gold, long ago, in the seam-script Ozias used for mended intentions.  She could not speak underwater, so she narrated it inside her held breath, the way she narrated the quilt.  The seams said: I did not intend to last.  They said: I intended to mean something while I was eaten.  Her lungs turned to fists.  The charm was no map to treasure; it was a template for telling damage from repair.  She kicked up through the green with the founder's sentence lodged in her chest.  The second breath came tearing in, and above her the gears were still counting.  She warmed the gold and narrated her broken intention aloud, all of it: I meant to succeed him, then I meant to sink.  Where her voice cracked between the two meanings she drew the gold in, and the crack held, and shone.  I intend to mean something while I am eaten.  Then she stitched the charm onto the quilt, a small bronze patch among the coats and sails, and narrated the founder too, so the fumes would not have her.  The zinc had four days left; she turned the cogs toward the barge lanes, where people still broke their vows.  She would still test every transmutation three times.  Doubt was a seam, and she had learned what to put in it.
