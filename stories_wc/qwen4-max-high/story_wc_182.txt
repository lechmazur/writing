The time traveler arrived on a Tuesday, and by Friday the mountain had begun to hum.  Vessa felt it in her teeth: ten thousand shelves of unread books holding a chord no ear was built to catch.  She was an arcane linguist, which at the Ossuary Bookstore meant she was paid to listen to silence and call it grammar.  The store occupied all of Mount Ossuary, nine hundred corridors of stock no catalog had ever finished.  People went in reading and did not come out.  The Concord licensed rescuers, and Vessa was the best of them, carefully resourceful in the way of the chronically underpaid: chalk stubs in a tin, twine measured in arm-lengths, lamp oil rationed by breath.  Forty-one people out; one not.  Her brother Teo had died behind a door whose warning was carved in Old Ilm, and she had opened it at nineteen, in a hurry, unable to read.  The lesson had existed, shelved and unread, which was the whole tragedy of the mountain.  Somewhere in the rock, the rumors said, stood the library of unlearned lessons: every warning ever printed and never taken.  She had spent eleven years trying to find it. 

The traveler came to her workroom with frost on his coat from a winter that had not happened yet and laid a botanical diary on the table.  Pressed flowers, seed sketches, dates of bloom, and her own handwriting, sixty years older.  The captions were in a tongue that did not exist yet, and she read them anyway, the way you read a face.  "These grow only where a lesson was refused," he said.  He had come back for one book, the report that would have stopped the burning of the seed vaults.  No one who went looking had ever returned, so he had brought her the map instead: her diary, from a future in which she found the door. 

The diary listed three hundred plants; only two hundred twelve were pressed into it.  Vessa did not study the flowers.  She studied the gaps.  Eighty-eight empty mounts, each labeled with a species and no specimen, each species named for a corridor.  Her future self had clearly stood among them and, forbidden cargo, had written names into empty mounts and carried nothing out.  She spent nine nights in the catalog room cross-reading absences: shelf numbers never assigned, invoices for stock never received, patrons who vanished in clusters.  The three sets of holes overlapped in one wedge of rock the Concord had listed as solid.  The mountain grew new corridors wherever lessons went unlearned; that was why readers were lost; that was why the catalog could never close. 

She took that finding upstairs and asked to renegotiate her charter.  The old terms permitted one living body out per mission, no cargo.  They listened to her arithmetic: every unlearned lesson lengthened their labyrinth, swallowed their customers, and stranded their stock in corridors no one could walk twice.  The new terms allowed one book out for every reader who came home alive, and the Concord would fund the lamps. 

They went in on the eleventh night, the traveler and the linguist, past the last chalk mark anyone had ever recovered.  She rationed the oil, marked every junction twice, and carried the diary open at its emptiest page.  Where the corridors should have ended, they found a door with no shelf number, and behind it a garden.  Books stood in rows with roots through their spines, pale stems rising out of unread pages, eighty-eight species waiting for someone to learn them.  The silence there was not empty; it was a full score, every part written, no instrument lifted.  On a low shelf she found the primer of Old Ilm, the twelve pages that would have saved her brother.  Two readers had gone in; she had written the clause herself, and she had counted.  She took the primer; the traveler took the report on the seed vaults. 

On the way out they found a lost apprentice, three days gone, and Vessa spent her last oil getting him to the door.  Three came out, and the clerk, honoring arithmetic, said nothing about the books.  In the diary, on a mount that had been empty, a pressed stem appeared, dry and gray, named for a corridor that no longer existed.  The mountain was one corridor shorter, and its chord had lost one unresolved note.  Vessa read Old Ilm aloud until dawn, badly, to no one, while under her feet a thousand unread pages waited for their turn to be heard.
