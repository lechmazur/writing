The prisms in the cafe windows broke the afternoon into pieces and dragged them slowly across the terrace tiles.  Everyone at the airfield called it the drifting prism terrace; Wren called it the only clock she trusted.  When the red band reached the third tile, her father would come up from the maze shed.  Everything after was the butterfly effect, and her father meant it literally.  His corn maze that autumn was cut in the shape of a monarch, and six-year-old Wren had chased a real one through a gap in the stalks and vanished for nine hours.  One small wing, and everything rearranged: her mother's flying, the house, the way her father measured the world.  Her mother had been four hundred feet up that evening, dusting the neighboring beans.  She landed to find search lines and lanterns, and by Christmas she was flying somewhere else, leaving a crate of logbooks under the stairs.  Wren was eleven now, and she had spent the fifth summer reading them.  The columns were fuel and hours and tail numbers, but in the remarks her mother wrote weather codes, the way other people wrote prayers.  Wren learned them off a library printout: CAVOK, ceiling and visibility fine; BKN008, broken cloud at eight hundred feet.  She wanted the entry for the fourteenth of October, the day she disappeared.  She asked nobody for help; she was featherly determined, soft and relentless, settling on the same question every morning, too light to blow away.  She had a question underneath the question, and it was why she came to the terrace every day: why do some thresholds feel sacred?  The maze gate, where her father let go of her hand to tear tickets.  The hangar door, where her mother stopped being a mother and became a crew.  The shadow line on these tiles, where the drifting light stopped.  Today she found the page.  Fourteenth of October, three hours of spraying, and in the remarks, in her mother's small square hand: MIFG.  Shallow fog.  Wren said it aloud twice, and the prisms went on dragging their colors over the tiles.  Shallow fog lies flat in the low ground, chest-high to a child, invisible from above; a pilot looks down through clean air at a field that is swallowing her daughter.  So her mother had not lied, and had not been careless, and had still not seen her.  The distance between four hundred feet and three feet was the whole story.  Wren put her palm on the table.  It had been her father's drafting table until he went digital, and the cafe bought it at the yard sale, and Wren had claimed it as her seat.  She had learned to read its grain the way her mother read the sky.  Under the varnish the pine ran in brown currents, and where a knot sat, the grain bowed and went around, and came back together on the far side.  Analyzing the grain was how she found his old pencil lines, pressed through paper into the wood: years of mazes, ghosted in grooves.  Every one of them bent around the same blank spot, the way the pine bent around its knot.  She could intuit the shape of it without measuring: the architect was not drawing traps.  He was drawing the nine hours, over and over, the exact length of walk a father needs to reach the place where he lost his child.  The blank spot was the low corner where the fog had held her.  He could not put her there, so he built a distance he could walk.  The red band touched the third tile.  Her father came up from the shed with a roll of paper under his arm and stopped, as he always stopped, where the awning's shadow cut the tiles.  He had stopped there every day for five years, waiting to be let across.  Wren understood then why thresholds are sacred.  They are the only places where a distance is a choice.  These were necessary distances, not walls: the room a person needs in order to be found instead of caught.  She stood and crossed the shadow and took the blueprint out of his hands.  It was this year's monarch, and the low corner was still blank.  Wren borrowed his pencil and wrote MIFG in the empty place.  Shallow fog, she said.  She was right above me, and it was clear where she was.  Her father read the four letters a long time, the way you read a field.  Then he knelt on the warm tile until his eyes were level with hers, down in the low ground where the fog had been.  The prisms dragged their colors across the tile, around the two of them, and closed on the far side.
