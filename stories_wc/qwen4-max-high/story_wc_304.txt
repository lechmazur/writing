The grotto hung in the sky like a held breath, its mouth open to the aurora.  The rock floated on nothing anyone could explain, high enough that the aurora brushed it, and the villages sent their dying up in wicker baskets on the cable.  Ilse had laid four hundred faces on its stone shelf, and the light had taken every one.  Tonight the face was Marit's, who had taught her the trade.  Below, in the villages, they called Ilse a beautician; up here she was a mortician, and the two words named one work.  She combed the hair, she warmed pigment on her thumb, she drew the mouth back into the shape it had worn while laughing.  Beauty was not vanity here; a calm face quieted the mind, and a quiet mind left readable grain. 

She set the sounder beside the pillow and let its needle drift through Marit's fading static.  Other morticians heard only hiss; Marit had taught her to find patterns in static the way a beautician reads a face, by looking at nothing long enough that something arrives.  Grain became weather, weather became a stairwell, a dog's ear, a kitchen in rain.  Marit's whole life was going up in pieces, and none of it could be kept.  One thing could. 

In Ilse's tin lay a single emerald chip, no wider than a fingernail, its corner broken.  Marit had spent the rest of the tin on strangers, and had kept this last one for her own ending.  A chip could hold one spark, and only one. 

There was a threshold of stillness in every dying: the moment the grain smoothed out and the mind stopped making shapes.  Before it, you could still take something; after it, the aurora took everything and left a beautiful, empty face.  Marit's rule had been simple: wait for the hush, but do not wait past it. 

Marit could not speak, but her eyes followed Ilse's hands, lucidly watchful, correcting nothing.  She had watched those hands learn the brush, and the wire, and the slow art of closing an eye.  Ilse understood, then, what she was being asked for: not a monument, only one last spark carried out of the dark before oblivion closed the ledger. 

The needle swung.  She could take the kitchen in rain, or the stairwell, or the long green afternoon Marit had never described to anyone.  Each choice would burn the others away; that was the cruelty of the craft, and she had performed it four hundred times without once flinching.  Now her hands shook, and pigment smeared on the old woman's cheek, and she wiped it clean with her thumb. 

The static thinned.  Grain went to glass.  Ilse leaned into the sounder and looked at nothing, the way she had been taught.  And there, at the edge of the stillness, one shape held.  Not the kitchen.  Not the stairwell.  Two young hands, awkward on a brush, and the patience that had watched them until they were good.  Marit's last legible thought was Ilse, learning. 

Ilse made a sound she had never made in this grotto.  Then she did her work.  She set the emerald chip in the sounder's cradle and let the needle infuse it, thread by thread, with the pattern of her own hands being loved.  Green light crawled into the fracture at the corner and stayed there, small and steady as a lamp in a window. 

The needle flattened.  The threshold of stillness came and went, quiet as a door closing in another room.  Marit's eyes stopped following.  Above the shelf the aurora leaned down, green and rose, and lifted the rest of her out through the grotto's mouth in a slow, radiant hush. 

Ilse finished the face anyway, because the mourners would need it beautiful.  She left the mouth in its laughing shape.  She dropped the chip into her empty tin, and the tin was no longer empty.  There would be a girl with clumsy hands and no patience, and Ilse would sit beside her and watch.  She would watch until the hands were good, lucidly, without correcting, the way she had been watched.  And when her own static came, thinning toward that quiet line, she hoped somebody would lean into the grain and find her there, looking.
