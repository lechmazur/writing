The bequest arrived before its donor was born, which in the Orchard Archive was ordinary.  Curator Ilse Vann had catalogued nine such gifts, and bewilderment had become her professional posture.  This one was a pistachio shell cameo blank, no wider than a thumbnail, uncarved.  She held it to the dome light and found no face in it, only the pale grain of a nut that had not yet been grown. 

Beyond the glass the astral orchard blossomed on its lonely asteroid, twelve hectares of pistachio trees flowering into vacuum.  Petals never fell here; they drifted out and froze into a slow bright ring, so the rock wore its own weather like jewelry.  Everything here was lovely, and nothing here was safe. 

Inheritance in the orchard was nonlinear: the roots distributed estates in whatever order they liked, backward, forward, sideways across generations.  A woman could receive her granddaughter's wedding ring at nineteen and wear it into the marriage that produced the granddaughter.  The ledger held all of it, first page to last, planting to cold death, and an heir's whole entitlement existed only between those pages.  Ilse opened the ledger at the first page, as the rules required, and began reading toward the last. 

Her entry sat in the middle, in ink still wet: one cameo blank, donor unnamed, date unfixed.  Beneath it stood the Archive's single prohibition: an heir shall not inquire after the donor.  Forbidden knowledge was the only kind the orchard grew reliably, and Ilse had wanted it since she was a girl reading seed catalogues like scripture.  So she inquired. 

She asked the root-clerk, a patient woman with bark-colored hands, who consulted the grafting maps and then said, cheerfully, that the donor was Ilse.  Not an ancestor named Ilse, not a namesake: Ilse Vann, curator, in a year the clerk was forbidden to speak.  Ilse thanked her and walked the bright rows, a bewildered curator whose disquiet had begun, strangely, to glow. 

She was to inherit a blank from herself, and the blank was blank because she had not yet carved it.  That was the loop, and it was simply complicated: one small nut, one small law, no way out.  Her grandmother had used that phrase about grief, and about love, and now it fit a shell. 

Ilse did her thinking in bathrooms, which she had never confessed to anyone.  Something about tile, running water, and a mirror that showed only her, made philosophy possible where offices could not.  She had understood mortality in a lavatory on Ceres, and forgiveness in a station restroom, and now she locked the Archive's small bathroom and ran the tap. 

In the mirror she watched her own profile, and the breakthrough came with the steam.  A blank was not empty; it was every face refusing to be chosen.  Carving a portrait would close the loop: she would learn her own last year, and the orchard would give her nothing else, ever.  Leaving it blank kept the gift moving, and kept her ignorant, and kept her an heir.  She had come to the asteroid to pursue forbidden knowledge, and here it was, offered as a trade: one certainty for all further possibility.  Ilse laughed once, unsteadily, at the tile, and the laugh sounded like her grandmother's. 

She dried her hands, unlocked the door, and went back to the ledger, still open between its first page and its last.  Under donor she wrote her own name, and under date she wrote: not yet.  She wrapped the uncarved shell in tissue and addressed it forward, to herself, care of the roots.  The wet ink of her entry dried as she watched, which meant the bequest had already happened. 

Outside, the orchard blossomed harder, petals rising into their bright frozen ring, and Ilse stood in the light with her hands shaking and her mouth smiling.  She would never know whose face the shell was keeping.  She turned to the last page, closed the ledger, and began, cheerfully, to be afraid.
