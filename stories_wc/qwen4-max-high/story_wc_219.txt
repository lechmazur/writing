The rain had washed the tower to the color of old bronze, and by dusk the pilgrims began to converge.  They came via dusty footprints left on the overgrown sculpture path, prints the width of a hand, pressed into the pale grit between drowned statues of forgotten saints.  Ilse Varga watched them from the tower's third window, glowing faintly as she had glowed in the four springs since first contact, when a visitor had touched her sleeve and left without a word.  The pilgrims called her the Lamp; she called herself a chemist with a skin condition.  Doubt was the only faith she had kept. 

The university had taken her chair for publishing a paper arguing that homecoming was a chemical event, a lock of scent and a key of memory, and not the sacred thing the Contact had made it.  She had wanted, then and now, only to understand the chemistry of homecoming: why salmon turn toward one river out of all the salt water in the world, why her mother's kitchen could be rebuilt in her nose from three molecules.  The ministry had answered her by building a pilgrimage.  Route Nine, they called it, an artificial pilgrimage to the tower where the visitors had stood, complete with a printed guidebook and a schedule of weeping.  They had asked her to bless it, because a lamp is useful to a road. 

In her pocket she carried the only home she had left: a thumb of Baltic amber with a fly inside it, her mother's, from a coast now closed to everyone.  Forty million years ago the fly had smelled a wounded tree and flown toward it, and the resin had taken it, and now it was forever arriving.  She had shaved a grain of it that morning and run it through the spectrometer: pinene, camphene, the ghost of a forest that no longer existed.  Home, it turned out, was a smell that outlived its country.  The fly had gone home and been held there, which is one definition of home. 

That night she went down the sculpture path with a lantern to prove the road a fraud.  Rain had erased the prints; the grit lay smooth and innocent between the moss-shouldered saints.  At four in the morning a small crawler came out of the tower's base, unrolled a stamp of the visitor's three-toed foot, and pressed the dust back into meaning.  Sixty prints, then it withdrew and let the spring do the rest.  It had been doing this for four years, in the rain, for no one.  Ilse sat down on a drowned plinth and laughed until her light shook.  Here was her proof: the sacred track was maintenance, the miracle a municipal service.  She could publish by noon and be right, and being right had never once smelled like anything. 

At dawn the pilgrims came, following the false prints through the wet sculpture path, and she watched a man in a cheap coat kneel where the fortieth print fell.  He was not weeping for the visitors.  He was weeping the way her mother had wept at the smell of caraway: something in him had come home, and the road had only given it permission.  The salmon does not ask who put the river there. 

So the skeptic did the only honest thing left to her: she made the fraud true.  She ground the amber to powder, all of it, the fly and its forty million years, and stirred it into the grit where the crawler would stamp at four.  Pinene in the dust, camphene on the soles of strangers, a forest nobody had ever walked in carried up a road nobody's god had made.  Let them converge; let them kneel; let the molecule do its quiet work. 

Her paper, when she wrote it, was titled The Chemistry of Homecoming, and it began: the return is real even when the road is built.  The university refused it, of course; she posted it on the tower's bronze door where the rain could read it.  By evening the pilgrims had carried the smell of her mother's coast into nine countries, and her light, which had never been the point, went on glowing in the bronze quiet of the spring.
