The anesthesia let go of me in order, the way it always does: hands first, then jaw, then the deep hinge of the hip.  They had cut the port out of my collarbone at noon, and by dusk I could feel every millimeter of the hole.  That precision was theirs, not mine.  The program called it enhanced interrogation, and it wired me to be proprioceptively enhanced, so that no limb could hide, so that a flinch became a confession with coordinates.  Eleven years later the wiring still works, and I still cannot lie with my body. 

I walked out to the ultraviolet forest because it is the only ground that has ever agreed to hold me.  The Republic that made me is a filing cabinet now; the grove is what grew over the facility.  Birches seeded in the drainage of the exposure yard, and somebody strung the lamps back through them, purple, humming, honest about what this place is.  They call it ultraviolet penance: the officers who ran the rooms come here to stand under the light for the number of hours they gave us.  An hour for an hour, as if pain were arithmetic.  I came for the same arithmetic, and for the opposite reason.  I wanted the mathematics of mercy.  I wanted to know what a kindness costs, in units, so that I could stop owing the world an amount I cannot name. 

In my pocket I had the strap of the mask, pewter, seven holes, cold as a coin under the tongue.  Nine millimeters between holes; I have measured that spacing with my thumbnail a thousand times.  It is my ruler, my rosary, my only document. 

I cannot remember the room by looking for it.  What I have instead is the body's testimony: my hands go behind me at the smell of warm plastic, my breath divides into fours, my jaw sets to a width that once held a bite block.  I read those responses the way a surveyor reads stakes in the ground, and slowly the room comes back, wrong way round, built out of my own flinches.  Under the third lamp my wrists crossed behind my back without being asked, and there he was.  Not his face.  His hand, and the sound of pewter giving one notch, and the quarter inch of air that came into my lungs afterward.  Nine millimeters of slack, given by a man who was not ordered to give it, on a night when nothing in me had earned it.  That is the whole of my data.  One notch, unrepaid, against eleven years. 

The surgical pain arrived then, on schedule, climbing out of the collarbone in a slow bright wedge, and I did what I have always done.  I braced.  Feet apart, knees soft, shoulders drawn up around the ears, spine made into a beam that could take the weight of the next thing.  Bracing is the last thing they left me that works.  I stood braced under the lamp with the strap in my fist and waited for the blow that light has always promised.  Nothing came.  The grove hummed, a moth went past, blue as a vein, and the pain in my shoulder was only my own, ordinary, mine. 

I had come to divide the notch by the years and get a number.  But the notch was not payment; it was not proportional to anything; it did not reduce.  Mercy is what will not go into the fraction.  It is the remainder that never resolves, irrational, non-repeating, which is why the man could give it and never say what he had given.  And it is why I cannot pay it back, only pass it on, at an angle, to someone who has not earned it either. 

I let my shoulders down, one side then the other, and the body did not know what to do with the surplus.  I threaded the strap through its fourth hole and wore it loose around my wrist, where I could feel it and not be held by it.  There is no country to send me home to, so I sat down in the drainage of the exposure yard and let the roots take the weight.  Tomorrow the officers will come to serve their hours under the lamps, and I will be here, unmeasured, keeping the one number that does not divide.
