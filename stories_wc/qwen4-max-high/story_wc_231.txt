The opal dock at Vardo went pale in the mist, its planks slick as the inside of a shell.  Margit came down to it every evening at nautical twilight, when the stars show but the horizon has not yet gone.  Mariners call it the only honest hour: you can still see both the light and the edge.  Margit needed both.  She kept the lighthouse and, by an old arrangement with the harbor, she kept borrowed memories.  People lent them out when a memory grew too heavy to carry alone, and the light had room.  The town called her eccentric because she wrote down absences instead of events: which buoy bell failed to ring, which window stayed dark, which name a man skipped.  Gaps, she said, are the only witnesses that never lie. 

Three evenings ago a stranger had walked out along the opal dock and handed her his memory, as the custom allowed.  He was old and polite and did not shiver, though the mist soaked him.  Then he took a dried Edelweiss boutonniere from his lapel and pinned it on her coat.  He tapped her sternum twice, pointed at the lamp on the point, and stepped backward off the end of the dock.  The mist closed over the place like water over a stone, and she was the only one who saw it.  No boat came out, no one on the shore looked up, and by morning the harbor book said calm sea, no arrivals.  To see a thing nobody else will admit is its own kind of exile. 

What stayed with her was the gesture.  A man about to drown does not usually give away his flower and knock twice on a stranger's chest.  She meant to decode it, and she was softly persistent, the way the lamp is persistent: no shout, no hurry, only turning.  So she investigated the only way she knew, by observing gaps. 

At nautical twilight she sat in the lamp room and let the borrowed memory open in her.  A mountain hut, thin air, a slope of scree, none of it anywhere near Vardo.  Hands she did not recognize, scarred across the knuckles, breaking the stem of an Edelweiss, which is forbidden, and laughing while they did it.  Then a wedding, a wool coat, the flower going into a buttonhole, and a young man's face tipped up, delighted, being looked at.  Margit began her ledger of what the memory left out.  No reflection in the hut window.  No shadow beside the young man's shadow.  No name for the hands, though the young man said something twice, and both times the sound was missing. 

That was the shape of the hole, and Margit knew what fits it.  A borrowed memory comes from the lender's eyes, so the lender is never in it: no face, no reflection, no name in his own mouth.  The scarred hands were not the stranger's hands; she had seen his hands, smooth as soap.  The young man being looked at was the stranger, forty years lighter.  So the memory had never been his to lend.  He had borrowed it from the man who picked the flower, who went into this water long ago, and never gave it back.  The lending register confirmed it: one loan open forty years, drawn by a young man the week after Oskar Vale was lost off the point.  For forty years the stranger had carried the one thing nobody can make for himself: the sight of his own face being loved. 

Now the gesture read plainly.  The flower pinned to her coat was not a gift but a repayment, handed to the keeper because the lender was past reaching.  The two taps were the count of them, two men and one flower.  And he had pointed at the lamp, not the sea, because a debt returned still needs somewhere to be kept.  He had not asked her to remember him.  He had asked her to hold them both until the light went out. 

She climbed and lit the lamp, and the beam went round over the mist, over the dock, over the place where the water had closed.  In the ledger she wrote no drowning.  She wrote: one Edelweiss, dried, received; one memory, held for two; the gap where a man's face should be, left open so the looking has somewhere to go.  She wore the flower down to the dock at that hour every evening after, and let the scarred hands pick it, and watched.
