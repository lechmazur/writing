The council gave Marta Vess one night to record the grain elevator's last words, because at dawn the demolition crew would come.  She was Brucktal's choral librarian, keeper of two hundred years of harvest hymns, and she filed every voice by the note it needed underneath it.  Discord, in her archive, was only a voice nobody had answered yet.  The elevator had begun talking in October, and the town called it madness.  Marta heard something else: a stammer, a lost beat, a thing groping for its own measure.  It had learned speech the way grain learns dust: eighty harvest seasons of crews shouting up the legs, Polish and Ukrainian and German and Spanish, all of it silted into the bins.  It had spoken on the clock tower's quarter hours, and when the tower fell silent the elevator lost the beat that held its languages together.  So she climbed the astronomical clock tower first, with her grandmother's cuckoo clock weight in her coat pocket, three pounds of iron shaped like a pinecone.  The tower's own driving weight had been sold for scrap in the hungry years, and the star drum had not turned since.  The pinecone was too light for the striking train, but the star drum had its own small chain and its own pinned comb.  She hung it there.  Gilt stars swung out over the square, catching her lantern and throwing it down as a sky the town could borrow.  Under the hush of borrowed starlight she walked out to the silos on the near bank, and the elevator tried to say her name.  "Marta, ich kann nicht, no puedo, hold, hold, hold," it said, and the bins shuddered.  She did not hush it and she did not translate it; she answered inside its broken grammar, interspersing languages the way the crews always had.  "Ich bin here, estoy aqui, ya, ya, spokijno, we have time," she sang, low, under its rattle.  The comb plucked four notes, the pinecone sank a notch, and the elevator caught the downbeat like a tenor finding his feet.  Then it said one clean thing: it was tired of holding.  Eighty years of holding grain, holding grief, holding the names of men who went up the legs and did not come down.  It wanted to carry across.  Marta looked over the gorge at the far bank, where the harvest families lived now, eleven kilometers from school, from church, from her archive, because the spring flood had taken the bridge.  She had come to record a death and she decided, in one breath, to rehabilitate instead.  That was her whole nature: she felt the thing's ache exactly, and then she acted before pity could soften into delay.  The elevator's conveyor gallery was a steel corridor forty meters long, hinged at the headhouse, built to reach over railcars.  The gorge was thirty-eight.  It could lower itself, it told her, but not alone, not in time; a body that big needs a conductor or it tears its own joints.  So Marta conducted.  She stood on the catwalk in the borrowed starlight and beat time with her arm, and she sang the harvest hymn the way the crews sang it, one line German, the next Ukrainian, then Spanish, then Polish, never letting one tongue get proud.  Every twenty minutes she ran up the tower and hauled the cuckoo weight back to its hook, and the stars kept turning, and the beat never dropped.  The gallery came down like a long breath, rivets singing, dust pouring off in gold sheets, and its far end found the stone footing of the drowned bridge.  Marta sang the last line in the old tongue nobody living spoke, and the elevator set itself down, and held.  At dawn the demolition crew found a bridge, and a librarian asleep on it with an iron pinecone in her fist.  The first across was a girl with a schoolbag, and the elevator counted her aloud in four languages, gently, like a man counting grandchildren.  They let it stand.  Marta rewinds the little weight every hour now, and the town keeps its stars on loan, and the silo says one word at each crossing: weiter, dalje, adelante, dalej.  Onward, in every tongue it has.
