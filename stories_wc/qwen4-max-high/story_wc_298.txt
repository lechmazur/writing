The lamplighter had passed, and the corridors held their half-glow, which was the only hour the orchard would listen.  Wren went down with her net and her brother's burned card.  Below the city the apple trees grew in the ossuary, roots snaking between femurs and skulls, drinking what the dead had left.  The apples tasted of other people's summers.  Every root bore a graft, and every graft bore a name, because down here destinies were bartered like cuttings.  A year of your life for a child's lungs.  A marriage for a harvest.  Teo had traded everything he was going to be so that Wren, nine years old and blue-lipped in the canal, would be anything at all.  The Graftwright had burned the card to seal it, the way the law required.  Wren had snatched it from the brazier: the Star, scorched to black lace, a single hand still pouring.  Now Teo sat aboveground by a window, empty as a swept room, calling her "you" because he had no word for sister.  The hole in him was the shape of a whole life.  Wren meant to patch it.  She had spent eleven years collecting him: the whistling lesson on the bridge, the stolen pear, his laugh arriving before his jokes.  The Graftwright was waiting among the roots, lamp shuttered to a coal.  She was a big, slow woman who could snap a thighbone and who had once, Wren remembered, splinted a sparrow's wing with a fishbone and thread.  Fiercely gentle, both hands, always.  "Burned means finished," she said.  "Burned means loose," Wren said.  She had read enough to know: a destiny whose card is destroyed does not vanish, it slips its leash and wanders the half-lit corridors until the lamps die, looking for a body to fall into.  Tonight she would trammel it before it settled into some stranger.  She spread the net across the corridor floor, three meshes wide, and into every knot she had inlaid meaning: hold, brother, here, home.  That was her craft, the one the Graftwright had taught her before they quarreled: inlaying meaning everywhere, in bark, in bone, in wax, until a fate had enough story to hold its shape.  The Graftwright used it to make barters stick.  Wren wanted to use it to give something back for nothing, which was the recklessness the old woman kept naming.  Then the corridor's half-light thickened, and something came along it like heat off a road.  It smelled of pears and canal water.  It was Teo's, and it was ruined: edges eaten, whole years gone to ash, a life with holes burned through it.  Wren threw the net.  The meshes caught and held; the thing thrashed, and every knot she had loaded with a word tightened like a hand.  "Help me graft it," she said.  "It won't take," said the Graftwright, "and half of him is ash; you'll hand him a sieve and call it a soul."  "Then I'll patch the holes," Wren said.  She knelt at the root that had drunk her brother's future and cut a graft into it, and the old woman, swearing softly, knelt too and held the wound open with fingers that never trembled and never hurried.  Wren crumbled the burned card into the cut, char and sap, and inlaid it with everything she had kept: his name, his twelfth summer, the sound of him laughing at his own joke before he told it.  Each thing she inlaid left her as it entered the wood: she gave up the whistling lesson, the pear, the laugh, and could no longer remember whether he had taught her to whistle at all.  Not a bargain.  A patch.  The lamps were dying by inches; she worked in the last of the half-light, and the netted thing went quiet under her hands the way a frightened animal does when it finally believes the hands.  "It will hurt him," the Graftwright said.  "It will be his," Wren said.  Dark came.  The graft closed over the ash.  Above, in the room by the window, Teo turned toward the stairs and said her name, wrongly, half of it, the way you say a word in a language you are only beginning to learn.  Wren sat down in the black among the bones and the roots and wept, and then she took out her thread.  There would be more holes tomorrow, and she had a whole life left to inlay.
