Rain fell on the fortress at Bir Hadid for eleven minutes, and the whole garrison smelled wet stone for a day.  Nasira cooked with cotton gloves, because bare hands told her too much.  A cook who tastes memories learns to keep her fingers out of the pot.  That morning the bread came back from the ovens tasting of somebody else's drowning.  Salt, rope burn, a boy's name swallowed halfway.  She knew then that the rain had found the pavilion. 

Under the dunes lay an old reef, coral grown white and locked in ice that no season accounted for.  The fortress had been a harbor once, and the fortress kept what its people would not say.  Any dream left unspoken at dawn went down the cistern stair and froze there, and freezing turned it to remorse.  Stored regret keeps well, but it does not keep still.  Rainwater had cracked the seal, and the melt was climbing the walls, and whoever touched the damp caught it.  By noon three sentries were weeping at their posts and passing it along with every hand on a shoulder. 

The commander sent for the chef, because a chef was what they had.  Nasira took her lamp and the square of grit cloth she used on scorched pans.  Her trade was sanding: she could wear the rough edge off a memory until a person could hold it without cutting themselves.  She had done it once too well, on her mother, and afterwards the woman remembered a daughter with no face.  Since then she left grain in everything. 

The pavilion breathed cold at her ankles: coral columns, a frozen floor, meltwater standing in stepped pools.  She read them the way she read a tidal flat, because the reef remembered its own tides.  The rim pools were shallow and pale, ordinary regrets, missed funerals, unkind words.  The middle terraces were darker and fed downward, and the deepest hole was still freezing at its lip, which meant something down there was still being told.  She followed the channels pool to pool, tasting the runoff at each lip, until the water went so bitter her jaw locked. 

The deep pool held the commander's dream, and she had come at the close of it.  In the last seconds a dream lets go of its dreamer, and that is when it can be lifted out.  If it ended without being said, it would set like salt in a pan, and the leak would never stop.  She put her bare hand in. 

He had opened the sea gate to spare the lower town, and the water had taken his brother instead, and he had never said the boy's name aloud since.  Nine years of it, frozen in rings, and every sentry upstairs was drowning in a grief that was not theirs. 

She could subsume the whole pavilion, take every stored regret into her one body and carry it out.  But the coral was load-bearing; the fortress stood on what it had refused to say.  Sand it all smooth and there would be nothing left under the walls but a hole.  So she took only the deep pool, mouth and hands, and it went into her like swallowed ice.  She sanded it, one pass, enough to take the barb off the rope and leave the brother whole.  Grain left in. 

Upstairs the commander was gripping the well rope with both hands and could not let go.  Nasira took his wrists, bare, and gave him back his own grief in one clean weight, and then she did the only thing the pavilion could not do.  She said the name out loud, in the courtyard, where the men could hear it: Rashid, who was fifteen, who could not swim.  The commander made a sound like a beam giving, and then he said it after her.  Spoken, it stopped being storage.  All along the walls the damp went out of the stone, and the sentries dried their faces and were embarrassed, which is a good sign.  Below, the deep hole closed its lip and the reef held. 

A residue stayed in her back teeth for a week, the toll for carrying another man's water.  She cooked with gloves again, and the bread tasted only of bread, and the fortress stood.  Nothing under it was smooth, and that was why it held.
