During the migration of clouds, when the sky's slow herds crossed the island and the first rain smelled of iron, Sena went down to the obsidian lagoon beneath the dunes to fill a bucket with black sand.  The water lay still as poured glass, kelp drifting in the shallows without hurry, and the volcanic beach along its lip gave up the sand easily, warm from the heat buried under the island.  She climbed back through the burned orchard gracefully alert, weight shifting before her foot committed, reading the wind the way other people read faces.  Eleven years a firefighter had taught her that; her father had taught her the rest.  He was the one who had lit these trees.  From his cell on the mainland he sent letters, and once a foreign coin from his deckhand years, drilled and strung on red thread.  It was his old method: hang the coin in a doorway, watch which way the thread leans, and you know how the air will carry a fire.  She used it now in stairwells, holding the thread up before she sent anyone in, and it had saved four lives that she knew of.  The town knew whose daughter she was and told her, politely, to let the orchard die.  She answered every letter, and she kept climbing the terraces.  Her captain called it unyielding compassion and meant it as a diagnosis.  The graft was her argument.  The last of her mother's apples had lived: one green limb above a trunk the fire had chewed to charcoal.  She cut a scion from it, no thicker than a pencil, and opened the charred rootstock below the burn line, where the wood was still wet and pale.  She hung the coin beside the trunk so the red thread gave her true vertical, set the graft to that line, tongue into tongue, and bound it with waxed tape.  Then she packed black sand around the roots, because volcanic sand drains and holds buried heat, and a union knits faster in warmth.  She wore a graft herself, pale skin islanded in scar on her forearm, and she knew that joining was not the same as healing.  The boy who lit the dune grass that night was fourteen and had her father's name in his mouth like a song he half knew.  Sena smelled the smoke, hung the coin in the wind, and came at the fire from the side the thread told her to.  She pulled him out of the marram by his collar, put him face down in the wet sand at the lagoon's edge, and beat the flame off his jeans with her coat.  Afterward he waited for her to hate him, arranging his face for it the way you brace for a wave.  She gave him the bucket instead.  They carried black sand up to the terrace in the dark, and she showed him the bound graft and the plumb of the red thread, and told him what his hero had done to these trees and to her arm.  She did not soften it, and she did not send him away.  Come Saturdays, she said, and bring your hands.  The rain came properly then, the whole herd of clouds settling over the island for a month, and the lagoon rose black and patient beneath the dunes, and the kelp went on drifting.  In the sixth week the scion pushed two leaves, and the tape came off, and the union held: a ridge of new wood, thick and ugly and alive.  She wrote to her father that the scar had taken.  When the wind turned and dried the crest, the dune above the lagoon let its black sand slide and began to hum, the low note the grains make when they let go together.  Sena sang with it, badly, her throat still rough from years of smoke, and the boy listened and did not laugh.  This was what she had wanted to prove, to the town and to the man in the cell and to herself: that scars can sing, that a burned thing can be joined to a living one and made to carry a note.  Below them the obsidian lagoon lay quiet under the dunes, and the clouds went on migrating south, and she stood in the singing sand with the coin hanging plumb from her finger, teaching the boy to hear which way the air was going.
