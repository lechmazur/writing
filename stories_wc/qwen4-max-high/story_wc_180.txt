The orchard grew along the rafters of the abandoned cathedral, ninety feet above the nave, where the roof had fallen open to the weather and the pigeons had planted the first pears.  Moss furred the beams, and the branches spanned the whole nave, so thick with fruit that the light came up green.  Halden the mender had grafted every branch of it, and his quartz eye, set in after the kiln burst, could find the seam in any living thing.  It could not see blue, which mattered later. 

In the cathedral town, vows were deciduous.  You spoke them at leaf-fall, they held through the year, then they dropped, and you had to stand up in the cold and say them again.  Nobody thought this cruel; a promise that could not shed would strangle the thing it wrapped.  His daughter Junia had let hers fall three autumns running.  The guild called it breach of contract, struck her name from the ladder rolls, and the east span she had been bound to began dropping green leaves in July. 

Halden kept climbing anyway.  He persisted the way water persists, without argument, finding the crack: her hook oiled, her name chalked on the beam where the guild had scraped it.  Junia had gone down to the river town to sell glass, leaving a daughter called Pip, seven years old and endearingly feisty, who had bitten the guild assessor on the thumb. 

Pip came up the ladder that morning with her chin out.  "You're not allowed to fix my mother," she said.  "I'm fixing a tree," Halden said.  "You're fixing her." 

He had brought his old satchel, the one from before the kiln, and had not opened it in eleven years.  Now he shook it out onto the rafter planking: grafting wax gone to amber, a bone button, a paper of seeds nobody had named, and a hair ribbon, blue once, that Junia had worn until she was nine.  To his quartz eye the ribbon was the gray of dishwater.  "What color is this?"  "Blue," Pip said.  "Obviously." 

She tied it into her hair and looked at him with the flat suspicion of a child who has been given things and then had them explained away.  A fallen vow cannot be bound with new string; the binding must be older than the break.  He held out his hand and let her decide, which was the hardest thing he had done all morning.  Pip pulled the ribbon out, yanking a knot loose, and slapped it into his palm.  "I get it back at leaf-fall," she said.  "Every leaf-fall," he agreed. 

He warmed the wax in his fist, cut the deadwood back to the pale living layer, and set a scion from the west span into the east.  Then he bound the splice with his daughter's hair ribbon, and his hands shook, and he pulled the knot too tight.  "That's wrong," Pip said.  "It is."  He loosened it, muttering what his own master had beaten into him: leave slack, a graft swells, you tie for the tree it is going to be, not the one it is. 

He meant wood.  Pip heard something else, and sat back on her heels with her mouth open.  "That's why she stopped," she said.  "You made her say the same words every year, and she got bigger, and they didn't." 

Halden's quartz eye found the seam in that, and it was not in the tree.  Forty years he had taught that a vow must be resworn word for word, and he had never asked whether the words still fit the mouth.  "Say it your way," he told her.  "I'll hold the wax."  She put her palm on the splice and promised to come up every autumn and tell him what the trees needed, and he could tell her what he knew, in whatever words fit. 

By August the scion had leafed out, three leaves, then seven.  At leaf-fall the ladder creaked twice, and Junia came up out of the trapdoor with glass dust on her sleeves, daring him to say the old words first.  He said nothing, which surprised them both.  Pip untied the ribbon from the healed graft and put it in her mother's hand, and Junia, who had come to argue, knelt and bound a second scion instead, saying something he had never taught her.  Halden left the knot loose. 

"What color?" he asked, because he still could not tell.  "Blue," said Junia.  "Obviously," said Pip, and pushed the unnamed seeds into her mother's pocket.  Below them the nave lay deep in dropped leaves and dropped promises, and the orchard held, and would need saying again next year.
