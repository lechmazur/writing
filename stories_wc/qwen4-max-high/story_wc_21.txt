The letters were found first, forty-one of them stitched into a confiscated suitcase lining by a lawyer who never got to use them, and everything after was consequence.  Archivist Elias Rooke read them in the floating library, moored low above the coast, where the zeppelin Concord held the last unburned records of the coastal families.  Below, the dunes moved like slow water over the obsidian lagoon, that black glass basin where the Register patrols had sunk the people they took from the salt flats.  Rooke was solemn by trade and by wound; he had spent nineteen years cataloguing what the Ministry called disturbances and what the families called graves. 

His particular affliction was that objects pulled him backward.  Hold a letter long enough and he stood in the room of its writing, breathing the lamp smoke, hearing the pen.  Retrocognitively trapped, the clinicians called it: a man who could visit any past and keep no present.  It made him the finest archivist alive and the worst witness at a hearing, because he answered questions from rooms that no longer existed.  Letters carried him only into the rooms where they were written, never into the mornings they described. 

The forty-first letter named a diary, and the diary came up from the lagoon in a lead tube, its pages dense with a cipher of dots and shells and tide marks.  Hester Vail had written it, the schoolteacher the patrols took in the second summer.  She survived the water and not the aftermath; the past closed over her, and she lived the night of the arrests every hour until she died.  What she wrote, she wrote from inside that night, in a code no one had broken in thirty years. 

At dawn the Ministry would dredge the lagoon for hull glass, and Commissioner Sarn had come aboard to parley.  She wanted the diary surrendered as unverified material; Rooke wanted the dredging stayed until the names inside it were read.  Neither could compel the other, which is the only condition under which parley means anything.  He asked for the night.  She gave him until the engines warmed. 

He went down the rope stair to the shore.  The letters described what Hester did every morning after the patrols let her go: she walked the tide line and arranged what the sea gave her, cockle shells in threes, black glass in sevens, kelp knotted at the ends.  The families had recorded it as pity, the leavings of a broken woman.  Rooke knelt in the cold surf and laid the debris out exactly as the letters described, shell for shell, shard for shard, because a cipher made by hands can be opened by hands.  Then he closed his fist around a shard of the lagoon's glass and let the past take him. 

He was in her morning.  He felt the arthritis of her fingers, the salt cracking lips that were her lips, the bright loop of the arrests running behind her eyes like a second tide.  She could not write the names plainly, because the patrols read everything she wrote.  So she set them on the beach where the families could read them, and copied the pattern into the book: three shells for the third house, seven shards for seven taken, a knot for the child never found.  The debris was the alphabet and the diary was the sentence, and Rooke, standing in her body, learned to read it in the time a wave takes to fail. 

He climbed back with his hands bleeding, his pockets full of shells, and the names in his mouth.  Sarn waited at the rail, the engines ticking warm beneath them, and he did not argue policy.  He read ninety-three names into the dawn, and where each name fell he set a shell on the chart table, until the debris spelled a village across her map of the dredging lease.  Sarn was quiet a long moment.  "That is not evidence," she said, but she said it the way people say a thing they have decided to stop believing.  "It is a record," Rooke said.  "You may dredge it, but you will dredge it named." 

The stay came through at noon, temporary, revocable, a small dry victory.  Rooke sat with the open diary while the zeppelin turned above the dunes, and the black lagoon below held its glass and its dead.  He could not stop seeing her hands in the surf, laying shells in rows, finishing the only sentence the world had let her keep.  He had no present left to keep; he had spent it all on hers, and he would spend it again.
