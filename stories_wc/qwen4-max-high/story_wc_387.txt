The rain stopped an hour before moonrise, and the parapet went on talking.  Water left in its drain channels whispered as it worked its way down to the lake, a sound like women counting under their breath.  Ren, whom the town called its net-mender and its philosopher, sat on the wet stone with a net across her knees.  She thought with her hands.  It was firefly season, and the paddies below the terrace blinked in long, uneven waves. 

The tax clerk climbed the stairs with his ledger held above his head, out of the drip.  "Nine boats," he said.  "The catch you report is half of last year's, and I am told the lake is as generous as ever."  "Someone is lying to the province." 

Ren did not look up.  "Sit, sir."  "The stone is wet, but the tea is hot."  He sat, because it was easier than arguing with a woman who had not yet disagreed with him.  From her basket she took a bowl, a tin, and a bamboo tea whisk, and she beat the tea until it stood up in a green foam. 

"How many tines?" she asked, polite as a girl at a gate.  He counted.  "Eighty."  "Eighty-eight, split by hand from one piece of bamboo by a man in the hills who is paid in rice and whose name is in no ledger anywhere."  "Your foam is standing on his work, sir." 

The clerk set the bowl down carefully, as if it had become evidence.  "I count boats," he said.  "I cannot count what I cannot see."  "Then come and see it," Ren said.  "We will go by following moon paths." 

She led him along the parapet to the water steps, out onto the causeway where the lake had risen over the low fields.  The moon laid its roads across that water, long glittering lanes that shifted when you shifted, scintillatingly somber, bright as coins and cold as a wake.  "Walk in the light," she said.  "The moon finds our floats for us." 

He walked, and the lane of light ran ahead of him, and in it he saw the cork floats of nets strung out in lines he had never once written down.  Nine boats.  Two miles of net.  And along the lines, at intervals, small pale bulges where the mesh had been torn and closed again. 

"Every tear is a rock, a storm, a bad night," Ren said.  "Every bulge is a knot I tied by lamplight."  "Storms came early this year, and we work twice as hard for half the fish."  "Count them and you will know how many nights the lake was held together."  "A net is a pattern that hides itself by working." 

Then she put two fingers to her mouth and whistled, low, two notes, the second one falling.  Down the shore, whistles answered, one after another, and lamps came out of doorways, and women climbed to the parapet with nets over their knees and sat down in the wet stone as if it were a bench made for them.  The drain channels went on whispering under their voices. 

The clerk opened his ledger on his knee and found that his columns had no room for any of this.  "If I write it, they will tax it," he said.  Ren bent her head, the picture of a modest woman, and drove her needle through the mesh.  "Then write it as repair, sir, and let them try to tax a hole."  He laughed, once, and it frightened him how loud it was. 

He drew a new column and headed it Nights, and he began to ask names, and to spell them wrong, and to be corrected.  Above the paddies the fireflies pulsed, and he saw for the first time that they were keeping time with one another, a whole hillside agreeing on a beat nobody had taught them.  "There," Ren said.  "Another pattern."  "It costs nothing to look." 

Before he left, he tried the whistle, and got it wrong, and she showed him again, patiently, until the shore answered him too.  He would be back in firefly season, he said, to count the mends.  "Bring the man who split the bamboo," she said, softly, as if asking a favor.  "His name belongs in a book."  Behind him the parapet whispered, spending the last of the rain, and out in the dark the mended nets held the lake together.
