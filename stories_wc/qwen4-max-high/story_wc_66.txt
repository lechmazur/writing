After the story famine, the city swept its ash into the river and called the water clean.  Mireth swept hers into an inkwell.  She was the orchestra's copyist, forty years of it, and diligence had worn her hands smooth as riverstone.  She had never added a note of her own, and she did not intend to start.  She intended only to teach dust to tell stories.  Everything the Bureau had burned had become dust, and dust was the one thing it could not confiscate; it rode home on boots and bread and breath.  She worked as thistledown travels, without weight or haste, letting the air carry what she could not push. 

The ash came from the archive fires, and it remembered being paper.  Ground fine, mixed with gum and rainwater, it made an ink that looked like any ink.  It was secretly luminous.  By day her parts were black staves, correct to the hairpin.  In darkness the notes lifted off the page as a faint green script, words running beneath the music like a river under ice.  But she wrote the words with gaps, whole clauses left hollow, because a complete story could be seized, and a seized story could be burned again.  The gaps were for the lyric. 

Every night she walked up to the ember alcove in the mist, a kiln niche above the river where the old coals had never quite gone out.  The warmth kept her transmitter's valves dry; the mist cloaked the antenna wire, and she cloaked the signal under the hiss between stations, where the Bureau's listeners heard only weather.  There, with the coals breathing at her elbow, she sang the pirate broadcast that filled her gaps.  She never sang the same lyric twice.  That was the craft of it: elusive song lyrics, a line that slipped its own wording as you reached for it, so no inspector could write it down and no fire could finish it.  A story that lived only in the joining of a glowing page and a remembered tune could not be confiscated.  It could only be attended.  For a season she had sung one instruction between the verses: bring a small light, and come in the dark. 

In autumn the Bureau sent an inspector to the Thursday rehearsal, because the ration of one story per quarter had made people hungry in ways the quotas could not measure.  He read her parts under the lamps and found them flawless: bowings, dynamics, page turns laid out for tired eyes.  Diligence is the best cloak there is.  He found nothing, because there was nothing to find until the light went.  At bar sixty the score asked for darkness and a rest; she had copied it faithfully, ninety times, in her steadiest hand.  Ninety rests, ninety pages waiting. 

On the night of the concert the hall went dark at bar sixty, and the inspector's pen stopped.  Under ninety stands, and in the cupped hands of the audience, small amber ovals opened: the shortwave radio dial glow, each no brighter than a thumbnail, each tuned to the hiss where weather lived.  By that little light the green script rose, and her voice came thin and warm out of the mist, singing a line about a woman who swept ash into an inkwell.  The players read the gaps and filled them with what they heard, and what they heard was already changing.  Then the dust began.  The archive's ash had never truly left the city; it hung in the curtains, in the felt of the hammers, in the lungs of everyone present, and now it glowed.  Motes drifted above the stands, green and slow, arranging themselves along the lyric like notes finding a stave.  The dust was telling it. 

The inspector wrote three words, looked down, and found they were not the words he had heard.  He put the pen away, gently, the way you set down something sleeping.  Mireth was not in the hall; she was in the alcove with the coals, singing, her hands still smooth, still exact.  She had added no note of her own.  She had only left the rests where the dust could speak, and taught it, patiently, one elusive line at a time.  In the morning the river ran a little greener, and the city swept, and she ground her ink.  There was next week's music to copy, and gaps to leave in it.
