The lamp turns at dusk and lays its copper light across the reef, and everything it touches settles into repose.  Dust hangs above the stair without falling.  Nothing hurries here; even the gulls take the long way around the tower.  This lighthouse prison keeps its impossibilities the way other houses keep mice.  I trim the wicks, and I do not mention them. 

My father was the boy who cried wolf.  When the water finally came for our street, he told the truth, and the doors stayed shut.  So I learned early that a name can be a gag.  Here they call me Wolf, and they hear me the way you hear weather. 

Nine men went down the cellar stair in October and did not come up.  The warden wrote transferred, and the ledger closed like a mouth.  At the spring low tide the reef will show them, and the boat comes Thursday.  Whatever is said after that will be said by the warden.  I have until the water drops to make my own account the way in. 

Below the light, the prison is a labyrinth, corridors folded by a mason who wanted no man to find the sea twice.  He confessed its plan, they say, into the chapel screen, carving as he whispered, so the lattice is the maze.  I lifted it out of the confessional in June, and no one asked the lamp boy why.  Father Anselm had heard me through it since June, and never asked whose son I was.  Copper light comes through its holes and prints a small maze on my palms. 

In that light a hush is a thing with a body.  It gathers where nothing breathes, and it moves when you come near, the way a cat moves.  I have spent four months chasing hush down those folded halls.  You cannot carry a lamp and a thread and a memory at once, so I tell it.  Each turn gets a sentence, aloud, through the lattice held to my mouth, where my voice has no face and no father.  Left at the cistern because the cistern drips.  Right at the sweeper's closet because the hush slid there and thinned.  A story is the only thread I own that does not run out. 

At the maze's foot stands a narrow door, left ajar for decades, its edge scuffed pale by every passage.  The place sustains that too: a door that never swings, neither open nor shut.  I read the scuffs the way the priest reads a penitent.  Two pairs of boot heels going down deep, then coming up light.  Nine times.  Between them, the long smeared drag of weight taken sideways through the gap.  And one pair thin-soled, going down, never up: the priest who believed me.  The door remembers what the ledger forgot. 

Beyond it the hush lies thick as wool, and in the copper light the dust does not fall but hovers, and my heels lift a half inch off the stone.  I hover there and say the last sentence of my story, and the hush takes it and holds it.  Nine men, stacked where the tide will uncover them, and the warden's boots in the scuffs. 

Tonight I set the lattice into the narrow door, wedged in the gap that has waited decades for something to fill it.  Now the door is a confessional.  When the inspectors come down on Thursday, they will have to pass it, and a voice will speak through the holes and give them the turns.  They will not see the wolf's son; they will hear a man telling them where to step.  Left at the cistern, right at the closet, down through the door.  By the time the water drops and shows what is stacked below, my account will already be the way they came.  That is the order I need: story first, bodies after.  My father had it backwards, and the street drowned. 

Above me the lamp goes round, patient, laying copper over the jagged reef, over the boat's small sail, over the water that is quietly leaving.  I sit with my back against the screen and rest.  The hush settles around me like a dog that has finally been followed home.  This place sustains impossibilities.  Let it sustain one more: a liar's son, believed.
