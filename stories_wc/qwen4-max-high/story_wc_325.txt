The wave crashed into the cliff, and then came the hush, the four seconds when the whole coast seemed to listen.  Nell counted them the way other people counted money.  Slack water lasted forty minutes, the hinge between one tide and the next, and she meant to spend all of it underground.  She was a caver who could not bear small spaces.  She had taken up caving after the funeral, so that no space could ever again be one she could not enter.  Her helmet lamp shook in the squeeze called the Throat, and the rock took her shoulders like a coat it meant to keep.  Empty your lungs, her mother used to say; you are smaller than you think.  She breathed out until the panic thinned, and pushed through.  In her hand she carried the tone set, an old fault finder that tracked invisible things: current, breath, the ghost field of a dead cable.  Her mother had used one for twenty years, listening along the coast for the hurts in undersea cables.  Three miles down, those cables carried every voice anybody had, and nobody thanked the women who found the breaks.  The set hummed and led her left, along a buried line dead since 1974.  It ended at a steel door frame frosted white.  The Hydroacoustic Research Annex, forgotten on every chart, had been built into the cave to test repeaters under real pressure.  Its lower gallery opened to the sea through a fissure, so the engineers had sealed that throat with a freeze plug, a collar of ice made by boiling liquid nitrogen through coils in the rock.  Liquid to gas, gas to cold, water to stone.  Her mother had come back for the archive three years ago with two dewars and a borrowed pump, and the tide turned before she finished.  The sea kept her.  Nell had brought four.  A return, not a rescue.  She coupled the hoses, opened the valves, and the coils drank.  Nitrogen went to vapor with a sound like frying, fog rolled out white and heavy, and the seep in the fissure stiffened, clouded, and rang under her knuckle.  Stone again.  She gave herself twenty minutes before the sea remembered its weight.  Inside, the drained gallery was a museum of the obsolete: repeater housings like artillery shells, racks of glass relays, kelp lying across the floor, a chair still turned toward the trench where the cable came up.  Above, the surf kept crashing, and between crashes the hush came down through the rock, and in the hush she could hear the ice ticking.  The codex lay on the chart table in its oilcloth, set down by a woman who needed both hands free.  It was the station log, bound by hand in boards and calf, because the first director loved old books more than paperwork.  The official histories named four men.  But in the margin of every page, beside every fault and every fix, someone had laid tiny flecks of gold leaf, and beside each fleck a name in pencil.  Illumination, her mother had called it, the way monks marked what mattered.  Nell turned the pages, and the gold was illuminant, each fleck taking her lamp before the pencil did.  One, then ten, then a hundred, a seam of light running through the record like the cable through the dark.  Agnes.  Perpetua.  M. Halloran.  Her mother's name was there too, gilded by the last woman who kept the log, who thought the fault finders deserved a margin.  Nobody had ever been paid to remember these women; that was the whole of Nell's errand, to stand by the unremembered, to be the one who stayed in the room.  The ice began to weep.  Solid to liquid, the oldest betrayal.  She took the codex against her chest and went back through the collar while it was still a doorway and not a mouth.  Behind her the gallery filled with the sound of the sea taking back its room.  The Throat was worse going up, because the book made her wider, and she had to breathe out and out and trust the arithmetic of her ribs.  The tone set hummed against her hip, following the dead cable home.  She came out onto the shelf at the turn of the tide, into the crash and then the hush, and sat with the book open on her knees.  In the silence between waves she read the names aloud, all of them, slowly, so the coast could hear.  Then she carried them up into the world.
