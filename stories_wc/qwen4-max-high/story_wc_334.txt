The morning Maren stopped humming, she knew something had been taken.  Sixty-two years of the same tune over the same boot brush, and then a Tuesday of silence.  She could still feel the shape of the melody in her mouth, the way a tongue keeps a missing tooth.  Habits break last, after the names and the faces have gone.  She packed her string, her plumb bob, her wax paper for spore prints, and drove down to the orchard the sea took when she was nine.  The shipbreakers had beached their dead hulls in a ring around the cove to break the waves, and the sea had come in under them anyway.  Now the orchard stood in salt water, and the greenhouse stood in the orchard, its glass roof fallen in like a dropped sky.  Her grandmother Ada had painted that sky.  Constellations in lead and lampblack on every pane, so the trees below could ripen under stars in winter.  Maren was seventy-one now, a folklorist who had never trusted a story she had not dug up with her own hands.  Decades later she was still chronically curious, still wading in at the lowest tide of the year with her notebook held above her head.  Inside, on the rotted sill where the apple roots had turned to peat, a fairy ring had fruited overnight.  Small brown caps, gills the color of weak tea, cold as the water.  She knew the rule from a hundred kitchen interviews: the ring keeps what you set down inside it.  She had set something down here the night the sea wall failed, and had carried the hole in her ever since.  The ring was not a circle.  She strung it, peg by peg, and saw the mushrooms spaced like stars, with gaps where stars should have been.  Above her the surviving panes still held their painted stars.  She matched them, pane to cap, and the ring resolved into Ada's sky.  Where a pane had shattered the constellation was broken, and beneath the break the ring held an empty socket of black peat.  That was how she detected the taking, by studying broken constellations.  Each hole in the sky was a hole in her.  The Plough had lost its handle, and with it her father's voice.  Orion had lost his belt, and with it three winters, a wedding, the smell of her own children's hair.  One pane still hung whole in the frame, and it was cracked across.  Under it stood seven caps: the little constellation Ada had invented for her, the Apple Tree, that no almanac ever printed.  When that glass went, the last of Ada would go with it.  The coast women had told her the terms when she was a girl, and she had written them down without believing them.  Bare hands, black peat, and the weight taken back all at once.  She had spent her life writing other people's faith down and never once kneeling.  She pushed her sleeves up and put both arms into the ring to the elbow.  The peat was warm, which was the only impossible thing.  It came up through her wrists like water finding its level: the flood night, the lantern swinging, Ada's hands black with lampblack, lifting her onto the potting bench while the sea walked in between the trees.  Ada had painted the Apple Tree on the last pane that afternoon and taught her the tune to sing it by.  Nine years old, Maren had asked the ring to take the night, because she could not carry it, and the ring had taken the night and the afternoon both.  The other gaps she left; she had given those freely, one heavy thing a decade, and only Ada had been stolen.  Above her the cracked pane let go and fell, and the Apple Tree went to pieces in the water.  It did not matter; she was already holding it.  She took her spore print anyway, because she was who she was, and wrote the tide, the hour, and the temperature in a hand that shook.  Then she waded out past the wrecks with her arms black to the elbow, humming.  She still cannot find her father's voice, but the tune is hers, and the boots, and the brush, and a woman's dark hands lifting her above the water.
