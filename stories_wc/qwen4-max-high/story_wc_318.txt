The crew called it a symphony because Emil Voss, who had once conducted the municipal orchestra, would not let them call it anything else.  Six leaf blowers, tuned a quarter tone flat, ranged along the curb like sections of an orchestra.  He raised his hands and the wind came, and the leaves went where he told them.  Only one house on Alder Street did he work for free. 

The Bell house had not laughed in two years, not since Toby went into the culvert after a paper boat.  Marta Bell still set three plates and had not said the boy's name aloud since the funeral.  The silence had gotten into the walls like damp.  Emil's trade was leaves; his work was tending to the unspoken.  Everything a household cannot say falls eventually, the way leaves do, and rolls downhill. 

On Alder Street, downhill was the storm tunnel that ran beneath the houses, a mile of poured concrete that returned every sound three times.  The engineers had labeled it an echo chamber and then stopped going down there.  Emil went nightly, through the grate, when the water ran low.  The cold down there smelled of pennies and rot, and his breath came back to him from three directions. 

Dry leaves keep their words shut.  He would macerate them, steeping the drifts in standing water until the fibers softened and let go of what they had swallowed.  Then the pulp sighed, and the tunnel said, in Marta's voice, the things she never said upstairs.  Toby.  Toby.  I should have been watching.  He never answered her, because answering is not tending. 

Upstairs on the sink stood Toby's toothbrushing hourglass, three minutes of blue sand.  Marta turned it every night at nine, because a thing still running is not finished.  Emil waited in the dark below until the final grain slipped through, until the house went quiet all at once, and only then did he lift his hands. 

He had lowered the blowers on a rope and aimed them up the Bell cellar's disused coal chute, which opened into the tunnel wall.  The flatness of the tuning was the whole trick.  In true pitch the wind carried only noise; a quarter tone under, it carried the lullaby.  He knew four lines and never the fifth, so he half sang them, and the off-key wind carried the halves up through her floorboards.  The unspoken rode along in it, softened, released.  Marta would wake at two smelling wet leaves, humming a tune she could not place. 

The chute worked both ways.  Twice she had laughed in her sleep, a short, surprised sound, and both times Emil had stood below with his hands shaking. 

The sixth blower was his own, older than the crew, and he never started it.  Its pull cord handle was worn smooth on one side, the shape of a small grip.  Starting the engine had been Toby's job.  The boy would brace his sneakers, haul the cord, and fall over laughing when it caught.  Again, again, all autumn, until the street complained.  It was Emil who had shown him where the paper boats came out.  That was his own unspoken, and he tended everyone's but his. 

On the last night of the leaves he set the old machine on the ledge with its cord hanging free.  Above him the sand ran out.  He half sang his four lines, and the flat wind rose, and the pulp let go of everything the house had buried.  Then the handle lifted.  It hung a moment at the height of a child's chest, and something hauled.  The engine caught.  The laugh came with it, whole and unbroken, and the tunnel gave it back three times, and Emil did not conduct.  He kept his hands down; that was the tending.  The chute took it up into the house. 

Marta Bell laughed aloud in her sleep, and then, waking, said her son's name into the dark, and the walls let the damp go.  By spring she was laughing at the radio, badly, the way people do when they are learning again.  She never knew who had brought it back, and Emil never told her, because telling is not tending.  He is down there still, mournfully eager, waiting for the sand to run out, holding a laugh he is not allowed to answer.
