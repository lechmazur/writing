They found Amelia Earhart's plane on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the world had decided it was finished with her.  Ren watched the announcement on the tender's laptop and felt nothing that resembled closure.  A position, she thought, is not an absolution.  Ninety fathoms below the hull lay the library of the Perpetua, a liner torpedoed in 1942 with four thousand books in her forward hold.  The manifest had called it sundries, and for eighty years nobody had gone looking.  Ren was its conservator, and she had ten days left.  The foundation had wanted Earhart; it had settled for a ship, and now the plane was found and the money was leaving for the atoll.  She was quietly analytic, the kind of woman who measured grief the way she measured salinity, in numbers small enough to carry.  She had been on first-name terms with death since she was twenty-three, when a cave in Yucatan silted over and took her brother and, for four minutes, her.  The resident who signed her certificate was named Ivo Marchetti.  Hers was voided; her brother's was not, and she had called death Ivo ever since, the way you name a creditor whose account remains open.  What she wanted from the Perpetua was not treasure but a number: the physics of letting go.  She wanted to know what force was required, and how long it took, and whether anything survived the release.  On the first dive she found the globe on the librarian's desk.  It was a souvenir sold in the ship's own shop: the Perpetua in miniature, four funnels of painted tin, sealed in glass with water and a drift of mica.  Eighty years underwater, and the seal had held; the little ship still sailed in its own weather, going nowhere, never arriving.  In the lab she shook it and timed the settling: forty-one seconds, every trial, the flakes descending at their terminal velocity, drag against gravity, a rate rather than an event.  She noted the figure in the log and felt the first clean grief of the whole expedition.  The books could not be pulled apart.  Waterlogged paper has no strength left; the pages adhere by hydrogen bonds, and force tears what you are trying to save.  You save it by untangling threads: the linen sewing of each signature, strand by strand, in a bath, until the gatherings drift free of their own accord.  Ren had untangled eleven volumes in eight weeks, and every one had gone into the freezer whole.  On the second-to-last dive she lifted a book of poems from the shelf beside the desk, its cover furred with rust and worm.  The flyleaf carried pencil, and pencil, unlike ink, does not migrate.  "We must be on you but cannot see you," it read, "gas running low."  Below, in the same pencil: A. Vale, radio officer, Itasca, 2 July 1937, 0843, and beneath that, for the ship's library, so that someone always knows what she said.  Vale had copied the last transmission and then, five years later, sailed as a passenger into a torpedo.  The world had found the plane and called the story closed, and here was the sentence that closure had been built on, still soft, still legible.  The volume would not survive drying; the text block had gone to pulp at the gutter, and only the flyleaf held.  The freezer space, the tender, the money: all of it ended Friday.  So Ren filled a bath with cold fresh water, laid the book in it, and began untangling the threads.  Kettle stitch, then the cords, then the linen passing through the fold, each strand eased out with a needle until the binding forgot it had ever held anything.  The poems clouded away from her hands and settled at the bottom of the tray like silt.  The leaf came free without tearing.  She noted the hour, 04:12, and the words released and the words lost: one page out of two hundred.  Letting go, it turned out, had no force in it at all, only patience and a rate.  On the last morning she carried the globe back down, shook it once, and set it on the librarian's desk.  The mica took forty-one seconds to come to rest, and she stayed for all of them, watching the little ship finish its weather.  Then she let go of the glass and left it there.  The sealed thing keeps its weather forever; the rest of us have to come apart to be carried up.  She surfaced with the leaf sealed in a wet box, and told Ivo, politely, that he would have to wait.  Somebody, at last, would always know what she said.
