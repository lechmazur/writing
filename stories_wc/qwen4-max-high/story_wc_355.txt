I bow to my audience: one stuffed heron, a harmonium, forty tuning forks, and the stars through the open roof.  A firemaker should rehearse her flourishes, even in a shadowed workshop under stars, even with nine minutes left.  Below the hill, Vessen waits on Armistice Night for my lantern to rise; then the rockets go, and everyone gasps together.  I make the town's fireworks because powder that only makes people look up is the one use for it I can stomach.  My pacifism is an eclectic thing, assembled like my shelves: a Quaker tract, a dentist's mirror, a ship's chronometer, my brother's diary.  The diary has a brass clasp, locked, no key, and Emel is eleven years dead.  He was a sound-ranger at the front, paid to find enemy guns by listening, and he wrote me one letter before the shell found him.  The clasp opens for the right quiet, he wrote, and I believed for years he was being poetic at me.  I would not pry it; forcing a lock is a small violence, and I have a policy.  So I did what I do: I catalogued silences.  Three hundred and six entries, each described the way you describe a face.  Snow arriving: a held breath inside a wool sleeve.  A kitchen after a quarrel: tin, and a dog's ears.  People call this a gift; it is only that I cannot help hearing the shape of a nothing.  If I can say a silence exactly, I can build it; that is the whole of my method.  Entry two hundred and nine is his: the ceasefire, eleven November, described in that letter as a room with the furniture taken out.  Eleven Armistice Nights I read that letter as poetry; tonight, with the town waiting, I read it as instructions.  Nine steel reeds sit inside the clasp; each stands rigid while its own note sounds, and slackens only in that note's absence.  He had listed the nine in his letter, disguised as scenery: lorry idle, wire buzz, a wet rope, a gun's cooling tick.  The ceasefire was not a sound; it was those nine sounds subtracted, and my brother had built a lock that only listening could pick.  So I doused the lamps, because lamplight hisses, and set my forks and cancellation jars along the bench.  Then I recovered his silence by tuning frequencies out of the room, one at a time, the way you take furniture out of a parlour.  The chronometer's tick went first, then the river's low shoulder, then the crickets, then the whine my own ears make when they are frightened.  Below, the mayor's speech ended; I heard the crowd's applause die into that particular hush, wool-sleeved, held.  Four minutes; my fuse-man would be watching the hill for my lantern.  The eighth reed fell, and the ninth would not.  The ninth was the sound of a man breathing through his teeth, waiting to be shot.  It was mine; I had been making it for eleven years.  I let the breath go, and the clasp opened like a mouth deciding to speak.  Emel's last pages were not about us.  He had catalogued the silences across the wire: their sentry's held cough, their soup, their boy singing wrong words to a right tune.  Same shapes, he wrote; I have checked; ours and theirs come out of the same mould.  That was the road he had left me, and it went straight through the enemy and out the other side into a person.  I had ninety seconds, a lit lantern in my hand, and a town holding its breath for me.  So I gave them my curtain speech, from the workshop door, loudly, meaning every syllable.  Vessen, before the light, give me one minute of the quiet my brother heard at the ceasefire, which is the quiet the men opposite him heard, and is yours!  I raised the lantern and did not drop it, which was the signal for wait.  And the town, God love them, held.  Two thousand people not saying anything: entry three hundred and seven, a room with the furniture taken out, big enough for everybody.  Then I dropped the lantern, and the sky broke open, and under the noise I could still hear it.
