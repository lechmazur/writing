For nine winters Sabra Quill lived alone in the stormlit observatory above the clouds, with a one-eyed cat named Ampersand and a kettle she called Bishop.  Below the cloud deck the world had gone gray and hungry; above it, lightning walked on its knees all night, and the cobalt glass portico turned every flash the blue of a bruised plum.  She loved that portico the way other people love a face.  Her work was listening: a hand cranked receiver, a wire strung to the lightning rod, and the long hiss between stations where the future leaked in.  It came out of her in meter, whether she wanted it or not.  "Fourteen at the kiln when the third bell tolls; rain will come to wash the coals."  She hated the rhyme; rhyme is a hook, and people swallow it whole.  But when the kiln town counted fourteen dead exactly as she had sung, they began to climb.  They left offerings on the ice: canned peaches, a wedding ring, questions she could not stop answering.  Three peoples lived under the Veil, and each wanted a claim on her.  The Kiln burned coal and wanted her to bless their furnaces; the Salt Choir wanted her to bless their tides; the Ledger, who rationed lamp oil by the spoon, wanted her to bless their arithmetic.  Each sent a runner up the ice with beans, batteries, and a contract.  She ate the beans and used the contracts to light Bishop's fire.  The Veil was not weather.  Her predecessors had built it, a shade of ten thousand panels hung above the sky to cool a burning world, and then they had died and left it closed.  In the archive she found the folding diagrams, brittle as wasp nests, and understood that dawn could be manufactured.  Ancient shadow, hinged and patient, waiting to unfold in a sequence that would let the sun down in ribbons instead of a killing flood.  Three ground stations cranked the sequence, one per people, and no two of them would turn together.  The Kiln feared the Choir's floods; the Choir feared the Ledger's ledger; the Ledger feared everyone who could not be counted.  So Sabra became three prophets.  The bands were broken anyway, all skip and static, each people hearing only its own splintered frequency, so she sang three futures into three cracks.  "Turn, and the furnaces shall have a morning."  "Turn, and the tide will keep its promise."  "Turn, and every debt shall be legible at last."  She swore allegiance to each in the only language they trusted, and every oath was true the way a splinter of glass is true to the window.  The diagrams asked one more thing: a cobalt lens, ground to the old tolerance, to set the panels' timing against the storm's own blue.  There was exactly one such glass in the world, and she kissed it before she broke it.  Ampersand left her for three days over that.  He came back on the fourth and sat on her only coat, which was his way of signing a treaty.  She ground the portico's panes on the telescope's wheel, cutting her palms, sleeping in the wind, and the observatory stopped being a home and became a tool.  On the night she had promised everyone, the storm stood beneath her like a lit floor, and she called the count into three microphones at once.  Three cranks turned in three valleys, each certain it belonged to the chosen people.  Above the clouds the ancient shadow began to unfold, panel after panel, ten thousand black petals opening in the order she had rhymed, and the lens held the timing true.  Light came down in ribbons, cool enough to bear, and the cloud deck lit from above for the first time in nine winters.  By noon the frequencies stitched themselves together, the way static sometimes does, and the three peoples heard all three oaths and did the arithmetic on her love.  The Kiln called her a liar, the Choir called her a counterfeit, the Ledger struck her name from the ration rolls.  No more runners came with beans.  She had never belonged to any of them; she belonged to the morning, and mornings do not write thank you notes.  She sat in the empty frame where the portico had been, palms scarred, watching three valleys learn to squint.  "Rain by Thursday, and a girl with my chin will climb the ice," she sang, because the hiss still leaked and someone down there would still swallow the hook whole.
