After the hedgehog uncurls, the grove allows her to move.  She has been waiting an hour in the leaf litter, knees numb, palms up.  The siskins in the alders open their beaks and nothing comes out.  That is the world now.  Air stopped carrying sound eleven winters ago, and the silence has not lifted since.  She is a tree-song sculptor, which used to mean she carved alder into instruments and let the wood say what it had held.  Her grandmother taught her that a tree keeps every wind it has stood in, and that carving is only letting them out at once.  Now it means only that she still wants music, and cannot stop wanting it.  She remembers her mother humming over a basin, and the memory has no sound in it now, only the movement of the shoulders.  She has always been symphonically minded: a single note is nothing to her; she thinks in stacked voices, in the way one line leans on another.  Wood still trembles, and bone still receives it; she learned this by pressing her teeth to a rail.  The sculpture stands at the center of the siskin alder grove, and no one alive remembers who set it there.  Alder, a woman leaning forward with her ear turned to the ground, moss in the hollow of her throat.  She has spent the autumn reading its stress cracks.  Cracks are not damage; they are a record of everything a shape was asked to bear.  The fan of hairlines at the shoulder means a hand pressed there, daily, for years.  The long split down the spine means it was carried, face down, across something uneven.  The crushed lip at the base means it was set down hard, once, by someone in a hurry to do something else.  To read it she has to move as they moved: press her palm to the shoulder, tilt her back to the carrying angle, drop her weight the way the drop fell.  Understanding is not a feeling she has; it is a motion she makes.  By the third week the cracks had become a score.  Where the strain gathered, tension; where the wood let go, release; the whole grief of a stranger laid out in loads and rests.  Above the grove the dead line still runs pole to pole, and its porcelain insulators are still seated in their brackets.  She climbed for six of them, and her arms shook on the descent.  Porcelain was made to keep a charge from leaking into the earth, and it keeps a tremble the same way.  Bare wire tied to a branch loses everything into the ground in a heartbeat.  Wire seated in porcelain holds it.  So she has strung the alders, trunk to trunk, each span isolated at both ends, each span tuned to a line of the crack score.  Six voices, stacked the way the sculpture bore its weight.  The hedgehog had curled tight when she first strung the wire, a fist of spines in the leaves.  She stopped then and sat still until it opened again, because a music that only frightens is not music.  Now it has uncurled, and its nose is working the damp, and this is the hour she has been waiting for.  She sets her palm flat on the nearest trunk and strums.  Nothing in the air.  The wire moves under her fingers, and the porcelain holds the movement, and the alder takes it down through its grain to the roots.  The ground carries it out in a slow ring.  She strums again, the second voice, the carrying angle, the long split down the spine.  Then the third, the daily hand.  She is not making sound; she is making the shape of what a stranger could not put down.  The siskins stop feeding and set their feet wide on the branch, reading it.  The hedgehog does not curl.  It comes forward across the leaves with its spines lying flat, and it presses its small warm weight against the heel of her hand.  Through the bone of her wrist she feels it answer, a tremble too small to name.  This is what she wanted: not to be heard, but to be moved along with.  She strums the release, the place where the wood let go, and waits for the hedgehog to walk away unafraid.  It goes.  She stays until dusk, playing the cracks to a grove that cannot hear her, and is listening anyway.
