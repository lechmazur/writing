The garden grew sound the way other gardens grow beans.  Bram kept eleven beds of hum, three of thunder, and one long row of hush that never came up.  He was a fisher, not a farmer: he cast into the waves and hauled out whatever swam in them.  Cool-headed, the village called him, and it was true, and it was also the wound.  Nine winters ago the goddess who sang these rows into being had stopped singing, and only her echo still moved through the beds, thin as a fish under ice.  Bram meant to catch it.  Not for glory.  For Nell.  His daughter had slept since the night the constellations began sleepwalking, sliding out of their grooves and wandering the sky like children loose in a dark house.  Her body walked with them.  Every night she rose and zigzagged the paths, never along a straight bed, her bare feet finding the same crooked road the stars took.  The almanac named that season the Unstitching, when patterns break themselves and nothing holds its own shape.  Only then, it said, does an echo rise to feed.  Only the first lullaby, the one the goddess crooned over new grass, could call a sleepwalker home.  So Bram baited.  He kept a ledger, and on its cover he had inked the rule: this book transliterates emotions, one grief to one glyph.  Plant the glyph, and the bed grew the feeling back as sound.  For nine winters he had buried his longing shallow and cast above it, and the echo had never once turned.  Longing is loud.  An echo is a shy, wild thing, and it flees a hook that sings.  His hook was a rail spike, drawn from the drowned track at the garden's edge and filed to a point.  Iron that had held one straight line for a hundred years was the only thing that could pin a wandering sound still.  The almanac's last line was the one he had refused for nine winters: the echo is taken by voicing silence.  Not by keeping quiet; any fool can keep quiet.  You must give the silence a throat, shape it, push it out with teeth in it.  Bram's calm had always been a lid.  Under it sat everything he had never said: that he was out on the water the night Nell stopped waking, that he had found her walking and told her hush, go back to bed, hush.  Nine winters of hush, packed down like snow.  On the last night of the Unstitching he went out without the ledger.  Above him the constellations stumbled off their grooves, the Bear licking south, the Sisters straying like heifers through a broken gate.  Nell walked ahead of him, zigzag, zigzag, and he followed her crooked road because straight lines were finished.  She led him to the row of hush that never came up.  The row was barren because he had only ever kept quiet over it.  He knelt in the cold dirt and set the rail spike on his line.  Then he opened his mouth and gave his silence a voice.  It came out as a lullaby with the sound taken out of it, the shape of a croon, all breath and jaw, a wolf mouthing her cub in the dark.  Nine winters of unsaid went into that hush, and the hush had teeth.  The beds leaned in.  Something old and hungry rose under the hush row, because silence voiced is the only bait that tastes like the beginning of the world.  The line went tight.  Bram, cool-headed, let it run, let it tire, and drove the spike into the earth to pin the echo still.  What he pulled up was not a voice but the hole a voice had left, warm, shaped exactly like a song.  He carried it to his daughter and laid it against her ear.  The garden held its breath.  Above them the stars stopped walking and stood shivering in the road, and Nell's feet stopped, and her eyes came open.  She said, "Papa, you're crying."  He opened his mouth to answer and found the lid gone and nothing under it, no hush, no words, only the quiet he had spent.  So he smiled and hummed the shape of a song instead, feral, toothless, all breath.  Nell took his hand and hummed it back, and the garden, row by row, began to grow.
