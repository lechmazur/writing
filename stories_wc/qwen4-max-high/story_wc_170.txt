The rain stopped an hour before dusk, and the terrace held the light the way a held note holds a room.  Water still ran along the parapet, and its cut-glass balusters broke the last sun into ribbons: red on the wet tile, orange, green, a violet so thin it looked like a rumor.  I had come to give back a heartbeat.  It was not mine; I had borrowed it at birth, when my own chest stayed quiet and the midwife turned away.  The lender was a painter of mirages, and everyone in the city knew his work and no one had seen his face.  He painted wells that were not there, orchards over salt flats, a stair of water climbing out of a dry canal.  Lately his figures had begun to shimmer at noon and vanish by evening, which is what happens to a man who has lent the drum out of his own chest.  He was thinning into his own subject.  I am told I was a strange child, and I was: I noticed things and did not panic about them.  At seven I noticed that my pulse skipped on the fourth beat, a small stutter, like a brush lifted and set down again.  At nine I noticed it matched the tapping of a man's heel on the terrace stones, though the man made no shadow.  At eleven I stopped chasing him, because a mirage runs from a straight look, and I sat down and listened to the children instead.  Their skipping songs and lullabies had odd verses nobody's mother had taught them.  Rain on the rail, seven colors, count them slow.  Hold your glass where the violet goes.  Give back the drum before the bronze.  He could not speak to me straight on; since the lending, his voice reached me only as shimmer, the way his paintings did.  But the terrace threw children's voices back whole, and he had hidden his instructions there years ago, patient as a man planting a tree for shade he would never sit in.  Now the city was in the hush.  Every evening, before the bells, the wardens stop the fountains and the sellers stop calling, and for ninety breaths nothing rings, because the mirrors are being unwound.  Our mirrors show only what is.  That is their whole trade: a woman looks and sees her own tired face, exactly, forever.  But in the hush they hang loose, unhooked from fact, and my song said they can be persuaded.  I wanted a mirror to show a possibility: the painter solid, casting a shadow, complaining about the price of blue.  I carried my grandmother's hand mirror up the wet stairs.  Jasmine had climbed the terrace rail that spring, and the echo kept returning the children's last song, thinner each time, like a coin worn smooth by passing.  I counted the colors on the tile and knelt at the violet.  The glass baluster above me was cold and wet, and the light coming through it bent like something remembering.  I held the mirror in that thin band and hummed the verse.  The song's last line was the one I had never understood: Let it go like a bow, not a wrench.  Grace, then; he had coded grace into a children's rhyme, because he knew I would want to claw the beat out and shove it at him.  He had hidden so I could keep it; that was grace, and mine only answered it.  I put my palm on my sternum and felt the fourth-beat stutter, his signature, twelve years of borrowed weather.  I opened my hand the way a dancer finishes a phrase, and let it go.  The mirror did not show my face.  It showed a man at the parapet with wet brushes in a tin, opaque, ordinary, his shadow lying flat and dark across the tile, and beside him a girl whose chest rose and fell on a beat with no stutter in it at all.  Possibility, held steady in violet light, in the last of the hush.  Then the bells.  They came down over the terrace in bronze waves, and the echo took them and layered them until the whole dusk was one chord.  Fact closed over the mirror like water over a stone.  But a possibility, once shown, is a hard thing to unshow.  Footsteps behind me, with weight in them.  My own heart came in late, uncertain, and then found the count, and did not skip.  He said the blue had gotten expensive.  I did not turn around quickly; I wanted the shadow on the tile a moment longer.
