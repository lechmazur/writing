The lake froze in a single night, and by morning it was a road.  Sela walked out on it to read the horizon.  She was the sanctuary's mapper, which meant she was the one who did not sleep.  Behind her the basalt columns stood in their natural hexagons, a shrine the earth had built before anyone thought to worship in it.  Into those columns nineteen years of notches were cut.  Each one marked where the moon had lifted over the ridge.  The moon wandered the horizon like a slow animal, north for a fortnight, south for a fortnight, and the limits of its wandering widened and narrowed over nineteen years.  No one else on the lake kept such a record.  No one else could give a hauler a line to steer by when the compasses lied. 

They lied because of the viaduct.  The Company had thrown it across the gorge in her grandmother's time, black ribs of magnetite quarried from the same flow as the shrine.  Dry, it was only stone.  After rain it woke and pulled, and every iron needle within a mile bowed toward it like a grass stem in wind.  So the ore sledges crossed the lake by moon paths, sighted from the notches in the shrine.  The Company bought her tables each spring and sold them back to the haulers one crossing at a time.  A man who could not pay went out on the white lake with no line at all, and sometimes did not come back. 

Ketu, her apprentice, wanted to burn the toll house.  He said it every night while he ground the chisel.  He was nineteen, and his father had gone through the ice owing money.  Sela answered him the way she did everything, softly and without stopping work.  She had been nineteen once, and had said the same thing to her own teacher.  Ori had not argued.  He had handed her the chisel and told her to cut the notch for that night's moon, and the next, and the next.  A burning would free one winter.  A finished table would free every winter after it, because a thing carved in basalt cannot be bought back.  All autumn she had taught the haulers' children to read the columns.  That was the revolution she was making: not a fire but a record, one full swing of the moon, wide as the whole horizon.  She was not taming Ketu's anger.  She was carving it, the way the cooling flow had carved itself into columns, pressure into shape, haste into an edge that would hold.  Patience was not the opposite of his fury; it was the shape she cut from it. 

This winter the notches had reached the last column.  The moon had walked out to its farthest north and would come no farther.  One more night of watching and the table would close on itself, nineteen years complete, every rising the sky could offer accounted for.  Ketu wanted to go down to the shore with a lamp and a grievance.  She sent him to the sighting stone with the rod instead. 

Then, past midnight, the wind turned south and it rained on the frozen lake.  Rain in the deep cold: the worst weather the shore knew.  The ice took a skin of glass and began to talk, a long tearing note that ran from shore to shore.  Across the gorge the viaduct woke and hummed.  At the toll house the Company's iron-shod drays stood stuck fast to the wet stone, and the keeper's compass swung to the bridge and would not swing back.  No one could steer.  Then the cloud tore open, and the moon came up at the far notch, exactly where nineteen years of arithmetic said it must, and Ketu called the line down to the haulers waiting on the ice.  They crossed without paying, because the table stood in the rock behind them and could not be sold twice. 

On the far side they unyoked the oxen and did not go on.  They left the Company's yokes on the viaduct's wet stones, where the iron pins took hold and stayed.  No one shouted.  The ice went on groaning in the dark like something enormous turning in its sleep.  Sela laid her hand on the first notch, the one Ori had made her cut the night she wanted fire.  Ketu came in from the cold with the rod over his shoulder and asked what they should do tomorrow.  She told him the moon would rise a hand's width south of tonight, and that someone had to be awake to mark it.  He nodded and went to sharpen the chisel.
