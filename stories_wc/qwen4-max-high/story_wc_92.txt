The fort of Aluza was famous for glowing.  Its walls were washed with lime and crushed phosphor shell, so at twilight they burned a soft green, and ships twenty miles out steered by the smear of it.  The Governor charged ships a toll for the privilege of being guided.  Ilsa Vray kept the sea lock, and she kept it the way other people keep grudges: carefully, and without mentioning it.  Twenty-two years at the gate had taught her to count things: bolts, barrels, lanterns, the seconds between a bell and its echo.  So she noticed the dark before anyone.  All of it went at once, walls and buoys and barracks lamps, with no wind, no storm, and no reason anyone could name.  The dark arrived like a guest who had been promised a room.  Ilsa asked it no questions; questions make a favor, and favors get reported.  Her plan had assumed lamplight, so she revised it in the time it took to blink.  In the courtyard stood the new sundial, half-painted.  The Governor had ordered it to replace the old cannon, which he called ugly and the garrison called useful.  Mera, the lamplighter's daughter, had painted eleven hour lines and stopped.  Not one of them told the truth about noon.  Angles are patient: a wrong angle looks like a mistake for years, and like a sentence for one second, if you know where to stand.  The gnomon was missing, the founder having been paid in promises.  So Ilsa had admired the harbor clerk's penmanship and left with his bronze quill holder in her sleeve.  Six goose quills, a socket snug as a dial's throat, and no weight worth a warrant.  Upright in the dial's center, one quill became a thin black finger.  A finger needs light, and every lamp in Aluza was dead.  Ilsa went down to the cradle of sparks.  That was the old name for the chamber under the lock, where the fort's flint and steel had lived two centuries and every fire in Aluza had been born.  The Governor stored his twice-taxed grain there now, and forty lamplighters sat on the sacks in the dark with their hands on the capstan bars.  Ilsa's mother had died in the queue outside that door.  She struck flint, lit a rush splint, and told nobody to cheer.  A revolution with cheering gets a bell, and a bell gets a gallows.  Hers would be silent: gates open, grain onto the salt barges, the town fed before anyone thought to call it theft.  In the courtyard she held the splint low at the dial's rim, and the quill's shadow leaned across Mera's crooked hours.  Each crooked hour was a letter; the shadow picked them out one by one.  Slack water, third gate, no bells.  Boots behind her.  She pinched the flame out and toed the splint under the dial.  Adjutant Kell had been sent to learn why the fort went dark, and had already settled on sabotage, since sabotage comes with a name attached and names can be punished.  "Sentinel," he said, "what are you doing in the courtyard?"  "It keeps bad time," Ilsa said.  "I thought I'd catch it at it."  He laughed, because she was a dull woman who counted barrels, and dull women are the best camouflage a conspiracy can buy.  He asked whether she had seen anyone near the lamp stores.  She had, and said she hadn't, and offered him her lantern, which was empty.  Kell stayed, sniffing for a crime, and the chamber below needed its door.  So Ilsa set the bronze quill holder on the parapet and asked him to hold it steady while she rubbed her tired eyes.  Six white stems stood against the last gray of the sky, fanned at the third angle.  Below, forty people read a fan of pens and understood it as a door.  "Charming," Kell said, handing it back.  "The clerk's," Ilsa said.  "He'll want it."  The lock took the barges down without a sound, because a careful woman had greased the gates at dusk.  By morning Aluza glowed again and discovered it had already changed sides.  Nobody ever explained the blackout.  Mera finished the sundial in spring, and it told the truth beautifully.  Ilsa kept the quill holder and never put a pen in it.  Some tools are for writing; some are for leaning at the right angle in the dark.
