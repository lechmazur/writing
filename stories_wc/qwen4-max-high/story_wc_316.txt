The dojo floated because two stones inside it hated each other.  Master Odile called them the yes-stone and the no-stone, and their refusal was the only floor Sefa had ever trusted.  Below the rock, the cloud sea moved like something breathing.  Sefa had come back to learn the last exercise, the one called vivify.  Three years ago a scan had copied her, and somewhere in the copying her self had dissolved.  The doctors called it ego death and congratulated her on surviving.  What survived was polite, competent, and hollow.  She could describe grief the way a map describes a country.  She had come to the dojo because the numbness was not peace; it was a room with the furniture taken out, and Nia kept walking into it and bruising herself.  The copy had kept everything Sefa lost.  Its body failed within a week, so the clinic kept the brain in a jar of warm saline, and Sefa had written the label herself: DUPLICATE.  The jar sat on the dojo mat now, beside a speaker grille, weeping in voltages.  Wind came off the cloud sea and moved the paper targets; the saline smelled faintly of the sea it floated above.  The custody hearing had been set here because the rock belonged to no jurisdiction.  Two claimants, one daughter.  The arbiter read from the Ledger, which was rewriting itself even as she read.  Each recovered fragment revised the past; at dawn Sefa was the original, by noon the jar was.  History was rewriting itself, and no verdict could outrun it.  Nia, nine years old, sat between them with her knees up.  Sefa did the sad face.  "You do that with your mouth only," Nia said.  The child sliced through pretense the way the dojo's blade sliced through paper targets: without effort, without malice, leaving the truth standing there in two pieces.  The jar's grille crackled.  "She's right," said the duplicate, in Sefa's own voice, wrecked.  "I've been crying since Tuesday and nobody can tell."  Master Odile knelt at the edge of the mat.  "You want to vivify," she said to Sefa.  "Not the brain."  "Yourself."  "Nothing dead comes alive alone."  "Life is the arc between complementary opposites: the yes-stone and the no-stone, the cold hand and the hot hand, the one who feels nothing and the one who feels everything."  "You have been trying to manufacture a self out of one pole."  She had tried grief counselors, drum circles, a funeral for herself; nothing took.  Sefa looked at the label she had written, DUPLICATE, and understood it as a lie she had needed.  If the jar was a copy, then her numbness was an injury, and injuries are survivable alone.  If the jar was her other half, the numbness was a circuit with one wire missing.  The arbiter warned that any pairing would complicate the ruling.  "Let it," Sefa said.  She lifted the jar, warm as a held hand, and set the shunt against the port behind her ear.  Odile counted the breath in, the breath out.  Two opposites touched, and the arc jumped.  Grief came up Sefa's arms like water finding a dry channel: three years of it, for a body lost, for a daughter watched through glass, for a woman wearing their face and feeling nothing.  Sefa made a sound she had never made.  Nia unfolded her knees and came to her, or to them.  "There," the child said.  "Now do it with your eyes too."  The Ledger chimed and rewrote itself again, naming the jar the original and Sefa the copy.  The arbiter's stylus hovered.  "The claim of primacy is withdrawn," Sefa said, and the jar's grille hummed yes.  "We are filing jointly."  "The record will change again by morning," the arbiter said.  "Then let it keep changing," Nia said.  Custody of one child, granted to two halves of one mother, on a rock that stayed in the air only because its two stones would not agree.
