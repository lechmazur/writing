Mira Sen mapped worlds by smell, sound, and the grain of the air against her teeth, because instruments lied the moment you crossed.  The shipping guilds had paid well for that once.  Now she slept on a cot in the abandoned spaceport repair bay, under a gantry that had lifted nothing in thirty years.  The bay smelled of cold coolant and pigeon dust, and she had charted every draft in it.  She slept because the bay's cracked drive only opened in the murmur of half-remembered dreams, that shallow water where a woman hears her name spoken in a kitchen twenty years gone.  The openings were cyclically spontaneous: a tide with no moon, arriving every eight to eleven days, never twice on schedule.  Two hundred and fourteen times she had gone through.  Weaving between worlds was not travel; it was needlework, and she was the needle.  Her thread was a green glass shard, a palm-sized wedge of viewport she had swept out of the bay's dust the week after the ferry Aludra vanished with thirty-one souls, her sister Ana among them.  Held to her tongue, the shard tasted of cut grass and hot solder, and no other glass in any world tasted quite the same.  So she followed the taste through parallel universes, steering by gradients: air thickening, sound flattening, the floor's grit growing finer under her boots.  Sight was useless there; eyes reported whatever the local light wanted.  She was not looking for the wreck.  The inquiry had ruled drive failure, and Teodor, the bay's last mechanic, had signed the finding with a steady hand.  But a sensory map is a scale as much as a chart, and Mira's bay kept weighing wrong.  There was a mass in it she could not name, a heaviness sitting exactly where the record said nothing was.  Every crossing had one purpose: to discover the weight of that withheld truth.  On the two hundred and fifteenth, the grass taste stopped being a thread and became a room.  Warm glass overhead, soil underfoot, a hum she knew from childhood: someone watering plants and singing badly.  The Aludra sat intact in a greenhouse the size of a county, its hull green with thirty years of vine.  Ana was sixty-one and had her hands in the dirt.  She did not scream; she said, "You took your time," as if Mira had merely missed a bus.  Then Ana told her what Teodor had never written down.  The corridor had not been empty.  Herself at nineteen, cheap and proud, had certified it clean by smell and sound, and had missed the cold seam of a quarantine fleet waiting on the other side.  Teodor had smelled it from the bay, and with eleven seconds left he had done the only thing a mechanic could do: redirect the ferry, punching it sideways through parallel universes into whatever world would take it.  Then he had let the record blame the drive, because a dead crew was a smaller thing to carry than a living girl's ruin.  The bay's drive had cracked in the sending, and had leaked into her sleep ever since.  He had withheld the truth for thirty years and had let her grieve instead of confess.  Mira sat in the dirt and finally felt the mass she had been chasing settle onto her, and it was not grief.  Grief had been the light version, the mercy.  This weighed thirty-one lives saved and one life spent wrong, and it was hers, and no one could carry it for her.  "He is ninety," Ana said.  "He is waiting for you to come back and tell him it was the drive."  Mira woke on the cot with the shard pressed white against her palm and the bay's tide closing.  She could not bring Ana back; the thread carried only the needle, and Ana had grandchildren who tasted of that other air.  So she did the honest work.  She walked to the almshouse where Teodor sat breathing bottled air, and she put the green glass shard into his hand, and she said, "I certified the corridor."  The old man wept, and the weight went out of the bay and into the two of them, where it belonged, divided.  That night she began a new map, and at its center she wrote her own name, which is the only fixed point a map maker ever has.  Then she lay down in the murmur of half-remembered dreams, and let the tide take her, not searching now, only visiting.
