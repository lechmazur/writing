After the birds fly south, the palace stops counting its gardeners, and that is when the tea house opens.  Anselm came through the collapsed orangery with a linen packet under his coat.  Inside the packet was a heel of rye bread, three years old, hard as roof tile.  It was the last loaf sold on Vell Street, and Vell Street no longer existed.  Not ruined; unlisted.  He had a ledger at home, and in it he wrote the small vanishings and the dates when people stopped saying the names aloud.  The bakery in March.  The word bakery by June.  By August his own sister said there had never been anything on that corner but a wall.  Nobody had forbidden the bread; the bread had simply become impossible to remember, which is cheaper than forbidding.  Anselm noticed because noticing was his disease, and because he had helped.  He was a reconciliation clerk.  He was heuristically gentle: offer tea, keep your voice low, assume the petition is honest, never ask the second question.  Gentleness by rule of thumb costs nothing and looks like virtue.  Eleven times he had initialled the correction that turned a street into a wall, and eleven times he had said, softly, that it was only paperwork.  The courtyard had been eating the palace for forty years.  Fig roots lifted the marble, bindweed strangled the emperors, and a samovar steamed in the dry basin of a fountain.  Someone had dragged palace chairs into the weeds and set out cups with the gilt rubbed off.  The woman who poured wore a groundskeeper's cloak, hood up, the badge of the men who were paid to keep this place empty.  He put the heel of bread on the table between them.  She did not ask what it was.  She weighed it in her palm, then broke off a crumb and touched it to her tongue like a woman testing soil.  "Vell Street," she said.  "The starter was ninety years old, and they took the ovens and left the culture to die on the shelf."  He said he wanted to give it back, and he heard how small that sounded.  "I initialled the order," he said.  "I know," she said.  "You brought me tea while you did it."  Then she showed him the beds.  Under the ornamental grasses, in the emperor's rose parterre, wheat.  Rye along the north wall, where the patrols never bothered to look because nothing there was worth looking at.  They had planted it at night, handful by handful, walking like people who belonged, and they had weeded it in daylight under the stolen cloaks.  A millstone from the ruined kitchen, turned by hand, one cup of flour a night.  "Bread needs a bakery," she said, "and a bakery is a list of names, so we do it this way instead."  She soaked his hard heel in warm water and set it in a jar with the first of the courtyard flour.  "If the old yeast is alive anywhere, it is in there," she said.  "It will want feeding every day."  "It does not care who is sorry."  Then he asked to uncloak her, and she let him, and that was the second question, three years late.  The hood fell, and there was the baker of Vell Street, grey now, flour in the creases of her neck.  He had struck her name from a register in his own neat hand, and he wanted it said out loud in a place with weeds and tea.  "Marta," he said, and the name went into the ledger he kept in his head, on the other side of the column.  He had not come for pardon, exactly, or not only.  His grandmother used to touch the doorframe before she cut a loaf, and as a boy he had thought the bread was holy because of the touching.  Now he understood it backwards: the touching was holy because of the bread, because someone had bothered to make an ordinary Tuesday smell like something worth kneeling for.  "I am not forgiving you," Marta said.  "The wheat may fail."  "The patrols may come."  "If the jar dies, none of this happened."  "But the feeding is at dawn, and there are two hands' worth of work, and you have hands."  He came at dawn.  The jar breathed, sour and alive, and he wrote in his ledger, for the first time, an arrival: wheat, rose parterre, courtyard, sown by thieves.  Nothing was undone.  The bread would be ordinary, and they would eat it, and the forgiveness would last exactly as long as the loaf, and they would bake another.
