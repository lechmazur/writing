The mountain had a storm inside it, and the books kept it quiet. 
Sella heard it at dusk in the shelves, a grinding thunder that never arrived. 
She was the seer of the bookstore, and her eyes were mercury-glass, silvered and blind to print. 
All day they held the aisles; at night they gave them back reversed. 
Nightly she substituted reality: ten thousand corridors of shelving turned themselves to match her reflection, left for right, stairs down where stairs had climbed. 
Readers who stayed past closing woke in a different country of the same books. 
Once she had navigated by memory. 
Then remembering became painful, the way a tongue keeps finding the socket of a pulled tooth, and she let the walls lead her instead. 
The stone had been in her pocket forty years. 
Her mother put it there at the mouth of the mine, the hour before the water came, and said a word Sella no longer had. 
The bookstore had grown into those drowned galleries afterward, shelf over shaft, until the whole mountain was one labyrinth of paper. 
The grooves cut across the stone were the lost language, the kind carved rather than spoken, and no living throat could read it. 
Sella was born knowing it, the way some are born with pitch. 
Her fingers could take the grain of a cut word and know it whole. 
She had buried that talent under forty years of shelving, because the first word it ever spelled for her was home, and home was under water. 
But she wanted the shape of home, not its picture. 
Pictures were what her eyes made, and her eyes lied every night. 
Shape was something the hands could keep. 
So in the cartography alcove she went looking for a feather, and found one holding the place in an atlas of vanished villages. 
A heron's, the barbs still oiled, the shaft stiff as a needle. 
She set the stone in her palm and drew the feather's edge along the grooves. 
The texture climbed the shaft into her wrist: not sound, but the cousin of sound, thunder heard through a wall. 
The talent came up out of its burial shaking, like something dug out of a collapsed shaft. 
The language had no noun for home. 
It had a verb, and the verb was two motions: carry, then bury. 
And the stone was hollow, and something inside it rattled. 
A seed, dry as a moth, kept since the hour before the water. 
She cracked the stone on the shelf's iron corner and let the seed fall into her palm. 
Then dusk came, and the mountain began to turn. 
The oldest corridors followed the drowned streets, the builders having been lazy, or kind. 
To find the hearth soil under the foundation stacks, she had to walk her village again. 
She stood in the turning dark and made herself remember: the cooper's door, the bend where the water smelled of iron, the three steps down into her mother's kitchen. 
It hurt the way a broken hand hurts when the splint comes off. 
She walked it anyway, reversed, left for right, counting the doors of a drowned town through a mountain of books. 
The thunder came up through the floor and did not break. 
At the bottom, where the shelves ended in raw rock, there was a hearthstone and a hand's depth of grey soil the water had left. 
She knelt and buried the seed, and pressed the dirt flat with her thumb. 
Carry, then bury: the word said whole, at last, in her hands. 
Nothing sprouted; seeds are slow, and she was not owed a miracle. 
But she knew the shape of the thing now, the way you know a word you have finally said aloud. 
Home was not a room recovered; it was a thing carried and put into the ground, and it would take the shape of whatever grew. 
She would go down every night now, through the pain, to keep the dirt damp. 
In the morning her eyes gave the mountain back, and the corridors turned again, and she found she could still read the grain of the shelf ends as she passed. 
She left the feather in the atlas, marking the page. 
Under the foundation stacks, in the dark, the seed went on being a seed, which is a kind of thunder.
