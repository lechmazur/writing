The jetty was four planks wide, and the sea under it held an entire war.  Cale was the Gloom Hill coachman, thirty years on the box, and every passenger he carried rode down dead.  He learned the old tongues from the headstones and from the songs the mourners sang behind his back on the switchbacks.  Both armies that fought the shallows were gone now, and both their languages were dead, and he was the last man on the coast who could speak them.  Then the reef died.  It bleached in one warm summer, the fish went, the villages emptied, and the water climbed over the sandbars where the fighting had been.  Nine feet of green water lay on the battlefield, so no one could dig it, or fence it, or stand on it and apologize.  The peace was down there too.  They had cut the Concord of the Shallows into basalt and sunk it in the lagoon so neither side could burn it, and the priests promised the reef would grow over the stone and hold it forever.  The reef had kept its word until the water turned hot.  Now the concord lay in a white graveyard of coral, still legible, still binding, and unread.  Cale decided a garden should take the field back.  He had the seeds already.  For years he had shaken dried pods out of the funeral wreaths into a sack, and taken more from the hedges along the hill road, and labeled them in the two dead alphabets, because the mourners had named them that way.  Two hundred kinds, rattling like small bones.  He had carried both armies down the same hill and could not tell them apart in the dark, so their flowers had gone into one sack, and would come up in one bed.  The jetty was the only ground he had: a narrow spine of tarred planks running out above the drowned line of battle.  He carried soil in the coach's boot, box by box, and built beds along the rails.  The horses had died the winter the fish went, so he hauled the boxes himself.  Salt sand kills everything, so he fertilized it with guano scraped from the pilings, wrack, and ash from the hill.  He chose flowers that open at dusk, since after the reef died the afternoons scorched anything that opened at noon.  The birds told him where to plant.  He had listened to them for thirty years of night driving, and he knew what the fisherfolk knew: the terns still called the sandbars by their old names.  The names were words in the dead tongues, worn down by generations of beaks, but Cale could hear them whole.  When a tern circled and cried Keth-amar, it was saying Treaty Sand, and Cale knew which bed to sow.  So the garden crept outward, bed by bed, each one hung above a place whose name only he and the birds could still say.  Four years of hauling, and now the last of the pods.  Tonight the terns would not settle.  They hung over the jetty's end, dropping and rising, screaming one name into the twilight, and the name was Keth-amar.  The tide was low and the water clear, and when Cale leaned out with the lantern he saw the slab down there, black among the white coral, its letters still sharp.  He read the concord aloud in the tongue it was cut in, the first time it had been spoken since the priests went into the ground.  The terns answered him, syllable for syllable, the way they had answered the priests.  Then he opened the last pods over the end bed, the one directly above the stone, and pressed them into the dark crumb of guano and ash he had spent four years making.  The moonflowers opened as the light went.  They came open all down the jetty, three hundred feet of white blossoms on a path four planks wide, and the smell went out over the lagoon.  Under it lay the whole field, the sandbars and the trenches the sea had filled, the stone that promised peace in a language only a coachman and a bird could still pronounce.  The garden was narrow enough to walk without choosing, and vast enough to hold everything that had happened here.  Cale sat down on the last plank with the empty sack in his lap and let the terns tell him the names of everything the water had taken, until the flowers closed at dawn.
