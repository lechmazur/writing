The wind came over the courtyard wall at dawn carrying grit from the demolition two streets down, and everyone kept talking anyway, quietly, the way people talk inside weather.  Vera stood in the middle of it with her hands in her cardigan pockets, listening.  Amina watered the jasmine; the domino men set out their table; the baker's boy ran the bread through the gate while it was still too hot to hold.  Under all of it she heard what the courtyard had argued about for eleven years: paper sliding through the slot of mailbox seven.  Seven was the only box with no key, and the postman skipped it, and the theories had hardened into furniture.  A spy's drop, said the barber; a dead woman's debts, said Amina.  Vera never guessed.  She ran the thrift shop under the stairs, and she was particular: nothing came onto her shelves without its story, told to her out loud, written on a card.  She had sold her father's tools in one lot, fast, without listening, and the silence afterward had taught her what listening was for.  This morning the outside world had arrived as a notice zip-tied to the gate: building sold, fixtures removed at eight, undeliverable mail pulped.  The foreman sat on the wall eating bread, a bolt cutter across his knees, patient as dust.  Vera had three days' worth of hope in her pocket: a locket, brass gone brown, hinge worn soft, sent from another city by a woman clearing her aunt's flat.  Inside, where a face should have been, lay a small flat key.  The donor wrote only that her aunt said to send everything back to the courtyard, where someone would know.  It was the only orphan she had ever let onto her shelves, because a key is a question.  She did not announce it.  Vera led by following.  She sat with Amina and asked about the jasmine, and Amina told her about Ruth, who had planted it and who had died eleven years ago in the spring.  She followed that to the barber, who remembered Ruth collecting the mail because her husband Emil lost keys the way other men lose socks.  She followed Emil's name to the domino table, where the men said he went down to the boxes every morning before light, and had for years.  Nobody had ever asked him why.  She climbed to Emil's door and found him standing in it, wiping his glasses.  She put the locket in his palm and said nothing at all.  He looked at the key inside it for a long time.  "I write to her," he said.  "I locked the box the week she got sick and gave her the key, so I could never take a letter back and make it better."  "She wore it in here, against her, and then her sister took it away to another city, and I could not ask."  "Eleven years of telling her things with no way to know she got them."  Down in the yard the cutter rang once against the wall.  They went down together, and the courtyard came with them: Amina with wet hands, the baker's boy, the domino men, the foreman standing up out of respect for something he could not name.  The key turned as if it had been oiled last week.  Letters, hundreds of them, wedged so tight the last ones had gone in folded like wings, every one addressed to Ruth, apartment seven.  Emil put his hand over them and said he did not want them read.  "Then don't read them," Vera said.  "Tell us one thing you told her."  And because she had followed them all morning, they knew how to follow her.  Amina said Ruth had given her the jasmine cutting and the words to say over it.  The barber said Ruth had cut his hair the week his hands started shaking.  Emil listened, and his face came apart and put itself back together, and he told them about the Tuesday she sang at the sink.  The grit blew through and nobody hurried.  At eight the foreman took the other boxes and left seven hanging, unlocked, and Emil carried his letters upstairs in a bread bag.  Vera wrote a card and wired it to the open door of box seven: Ruth kept the key; Emil kept talking; the courtyard heard him.  By full light the boy had put a note in for the barber, and the barber was reading it aloud.  Vera stood in the middle of the noise with her hands in her pockets, listening, which was the only thing she had ever known to mend anything.
