After the liquidation, the Combine's offices burned for three days, and the dust storms came the week after.  Nine years later, Ada Renn came home and went looking for paper.  She was a professional debunker, and she had made her name proving that curses were arithmetic wearing a costume.  Ninety-nine haunted heirlooms, ninety-nine coincidences, one patient woman with a ledger. 

The town's favorite curse was the Hollow Star, a diamond with a black feather of fractured carbon at its heart.  Every owner had died badly, which proved nothing except that owners of diamonds tend to be old and rich.  Ada had published the aneurysms, the strokes, the one very ordinary car crash.  Now the Star sat in a tobacco tin on the orchard's altar stone, and the town said it had turned kind. 

The orchard should not have existed.  Beyond the slag ridge the storms went by like freight trains, stripping paint off the dead Combine town.  Inside, forty acres of apple trees held unbruised leaves and fruit so heavy the branches needed crutches.  People said the Stone drank the poison out of the air, and they fed the apples to their children.  Ada's mother had planted the orchard, and Ada had let herself believe the tin.  She had built a career excavating truth from comfortable lies, and this one had her mother's handwriting on it. 

But she was sharply observant, and the orchard kept telling on itself.  The wind vane on the packhouse had not turned since the trees went in.  Dust lay in a clean crescent along the ridge's inner curve, like snow behind a wall.  The leaves did not tear; they only turned, all together, as if breathing.  Standing in the middle row, Ada could feel it: a hidden stillpoint, a pocket of dead air where the storms passed overhead and left the ground alone.  No stone made that.  Shape made that, and shape is drawn by somebody. 

At the liquidation the Combine had strip-shredded its correspondence into confetti and dumped it in the lagoon.  Ada fished out three garbage bags of it and dried the strips on her mother's kitchen table.  For eleven weeks she worked by reassembling shredded letters, matching torn vowels and the tails of commas, taping the strips into pages.  She was good at it; a conspiracy is only a story with its middle cut out, and she had spent her life putting middles back.  One strip carried a curve of ink that was not a letter at all but a contour line.  She intuited the rest of the drawing before she found it: a bowl, a ridge, an arrow of prevailing wind. 

The letters came from her mother, forty-one of them, to the Combine's directors.  Her mother had not been a lucky gardener.  She had been a soil chemist, hired to plant a windbreak over the tailings cells so the dust would stop moving.  The ridge was engineered spoil, stacked to make a hollow where nothing scoured.  The trees were a pump.  They drank the cesium and the strontium out of the buried cells and stored it in wood and skin and sugar.  The orchard thrived because it was eating poison, and every apple it made was a small, sweet, patient dose.  The last letter begged them to fence the rows and label the fruit; the answer was the liquidation.  The directors had paid her fee in the Hollow Star, because a cursed stone was cheaper than cash and came with a story attached.  Her mother had said all this at a harvest meeting, and the town had laughed and touched the tin and handed her daughter another apple.  Then she died, ordinarily, of an ordinary heart, and Ada stopped asking. 

Now she stood at the altar stone with the taped pages in one hand and the flawed diamond in the other.  Harvest was tomorrow, and the whole town was in the rows with the crates already stacked.  She held the Stone up and let them see the feather at its center, the crack that leaked light.  It had never killed anyone; it had never saved anyone; it was carbon with a flaw, and a flaw is only a place where the truth gets in.  She read her mother's letters aloud, all forty-one, until the light went.  They burned the crates, and then, slowly, they began to fence the rows.  The trees stayed, because a pump is still useful, and the wind still went over.  She kept the diamond in her pocket, where it belonged: with the evidence.  Ada stood in the stillpoint at the center, in the one honest quiet in the country, and let the dust roar above her.
