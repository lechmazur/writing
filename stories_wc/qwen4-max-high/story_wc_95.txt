The glass atrium held the afternoon like a held breath, and beside it the brook went on saying the same soft thing over and over.  Vesna Adler waited.  She had been told to wait until the shadow touched the fourth hour line, and she had spent eleven years learning to make waiting into work. 

Her right arm was brass from the elbow down, ratcheted at the wrist, and it clicked when she flexed it, a small polite applause.  Rehm had taken the flesh one at the winter assizes, arguing whether a border could be just; she had lost the arm and won the argument, a poor trade.  She was the valley's last licensed philosopher, which meant she was also its last licensed duelist.  She had stopped caring who was right; she wanted questions to become destinations, roads that ended at doors, inquiries a person could live inside. 

In her satchel she carried her grandmother's Danish butter tin, and inside the tin, instead of cookies, a card of sewing needles.  The needles were pinned at odd angles, which her grandmother had called tidiness and Vesna had eventually recognized as an alphabet.  The valley had used it for two generations, when saying a thing aloud could get a house burned. 

The atrium floor was a sundial, and someone had painted only half of it.  Half the hour lines lay in fresh white, the rest bare stone; the gnomon had been unscrewed and taken away.  Vesna knelt, while above her the wasps that had gotten into the glass went on hurling themselves at the panes.  The painted angles were wrong for this latitude.  They were wrong the way her grandmother's needles were wrong. 

She laid the needle card on the stone, lifted her brass forearm vertical, and let it stand in for the missing gnomon.  The shadow fell, the wrist ratchet clicked through its degrees, and the hidden overture read itself out along the wet paint.  Not music; an offer.  The brook as the border, the atrium as neutral ground, amnesty for the valley's smugglers, and Rehm's name signed beneath it.  He could not say any of it aloud; his Margrave would hang him for the syllables.  So he had painted her a question and left half of it blank for her answer. 

Then the tin tipped.  Needles went everywhere, skittering across the dial, into the wet white, into the moss between the stones, and Vesna said, quite calmly, "Well."  A wasp landed on her wrist joint and stayed there, considering the brass.  The brook went on being quiet, the only thing in the atrium behaving itself.  And Rehm came in early, with two seconds and a lantern, because he had never in his life been able to wait. 

"Madam," he said, and bowed to her, and then to the scattered needles, as though they were a third party to the negotiation.  "Colonel," she said, rising, "you are standing on an amnesty."  She offered him her left hand, which was courtesy, and kept her blade drawn, which was honesty.  Her answer was yes, and in this valley yes had to be said with the feet. 

They fenced.  They fenced because the law required a treaty to be argued with the whole body, and because two men with a lantern were watching, and Rehm could accept only a peace that had visibly cost him something.  So she gave him a beautiful, expensive fight, and spent it walking him backward across the dial.  Parry: the fourth line, the brook.  Beat: the ninth, the atrium.  Her brass wrist locked at thirty-one degrees, the exact angle of the third needle in her grandmother's card, and his boot came down on the amnesty.  A second stepped in the paint pot and stood very still, dignified, with one white foot. 

Rehm read his own footwork.  She saw him understand, and then decide to keep understanding, which is the art of fierceness practiced diplomatically: leave a man a road he can take without kneeling.  He dropped his point into the paint pot and knelt to draw the missing half of the seventh hour line.  The seconds wrote it all down as a wound, a yield, an honorable defeat. 

Vesna gathered her needles out of the moss and the drying white, wiped each one, and pinned it back into the card at its angle.  Outside, the brook went on being the border.  Inside, the question had become a room with glass walls, a floor that told the hour, and a door two armies would have to knock on.  She closed the tin.
