Nine men died because two calendars could not agree on the word Tuesday.  That was the mistake Sova inherited, along with her teacher's ledgers and the antique brass drawer pull he had wrenched from the chart desk the night he learned the names.  Now she negotiated prisoners for the mainland, and she set every exchange by the moon, the only clock the two peoples shared.  Third quarter for the parley, new moon for the handover; no side could accuse the other of hiding behind a date.  They met on the Widow's Tooth, a drowned lighthouse halfway across the strait, where ninety winters of storm had grooved the parapet like an old face.  Neutral territory: no flags, no garrison, no god but weather.  Sova laid the pull on the coping stone, as the custom required, each negotiator leaving behind something he would hate to lose.  Through the mist, Colonel Beren Ash set down a pocket watch stopped at the hour his brother was taken.  He was uncommonly thoughtful, which in an enemy is worse than cruelty; cruelty you can predict.  He had brought her a second blanket, and he had learned the island word for sorrow so as not to misuse it.  Eleven days ago her government had apologized, in writing, for the nine.  The islands' assembly would vote on forgiveness at the new moon, and not one hour sooner.  Everything tonight happened inside that gap, after the apology, before the pardon, where nothing could be enforced and everything could be spoiled.  Her orders were folded in her coat: sixteen for sixteen, and the forgiveness clause signed on the parapet before the boats left.  Her ministers wanted the wound closed on their schedule.  Ash read the clause twice, his brow furrowing, the wind taking the pages sideways.  "You are asking us to sell what we have not finished feeling," he said.  "I am asking for a signature."  "Then you will have a signature and keep the war."  Below them the hostages waited, both boats' painters knotted to the same iron ring, sixteen men in each, corded at the wrist and silent.  Sova had spent nine years turning failures into clauses: the moon instead of the calendar, one ring instead of two docks, a surety instead of an oath.  She kept every mistake and made it into a rule; it was the only alchemy she trusted.  She had never made a rule for this hour, when a wrong has been named and not yet absolved.  Sign it, said the fold in her coat.  Ash's thumb moved over the stopped watch.  She thought of Hallam hauling the drawer open, the brass coming away in his fist, the small useless click of it.  He had kept the pull because he could not keep the men.  Wind came over the parapet and laid the mist flat, and for a moment she saw both boats, and both were the same gray.  Sova folded the clause small and put it in her pocket unsigned.  Her ministers would call it weakness; she had learned that a pardon squeezed out of grief expires the moment the pressure lifts.  "The exchange stands," she said.  "Forgiveness is not a price; vote at the new moon, or don't."  Then she went down the stair and untethered the mainland boat first, before the islanders had loosed theirs, giving away her only leverage in the dark.  Ash watched and said nothing, the hush of a man revising his opinion.  Sixteen men climbed past her; a boy with a burned hand stopped and asked whether the apology had been true.  "It was accurate," she said.  "Truth takes longer."  Ropes fell; the ring stood empty; the boats went opposite ways into the same mist.  On the parapet the watch and the drawer pull lay in the grooves the storms had cut, waiting for a word nobody had said yet.  No one would touch them; that was what neutral ground was for.  She would climb again at the new moon, and at every new moon after, until the islands spoke or refused; either way she would carry the answer home and make something of it.  The brass stayed on the stone, small and cold and patient, holding nothing shut.
