The thunder-echo range kept every sound it was given, and gave it back for hours.  Sefa had learned to walk in the gaps between returns.  After the guardianship fell, the passes emptied, and the only traffic in the canyons was noise coming home.  The order had ended in a single afternoon of bad music, and the canyon still repeated it, faintly, on wet nights.  She was the last relic keeper, and she had not come to guard anything.  She had come to read the wreckage. 

On the gravel bar below the narrows lay forty-one pieces of driftwood, bleached to bone.  No river ran here now; the wood had come through the seam, spat out by crossings that failed.  Each log had surfaced at a bearing she recorded, so she arranged them as directional markers, tips aimed the way the weave had pulled.  Forty-one arrows on the stones, and not one pointing where the guardians swore the other world lay.  They fanned downward, into the floor of the canyon, as if the far shore were underfoot rather than across.  That was the first insight, and it cost her a winter.  She wanted the rest.  She had traded sleep and a warm house for the ledger, and would have traded more. 

The case on her back held the gilded reed flute, the last instrument capable of weaving between worlds.  Reed, once; the guardians had laid gold leaf over its split to keep it whole, and it had not made a true sound in ninety years.  Playing it in noise tore the weave sideways, which was how the driftwood came, and how her sister went.  So Sefa waited for the range to cancel itself.  Twice a season the stacked echoes met crest to trough and swallowed one another, and the silence came down the canyon like a snow slide, avalanchingly quiet, a weight you could feel on the ears.  In that hush a single note could reach the seam unbroken. 

She knelt among her arrows and began to uncase the flute.  Oilcloth, then felt, then the lead sleeve the order had sewn to smother it.  Her hands shook; a keeper's whole training was to keep a thing cased, and she was undoing it.  The gold caught the low sun and looked, as it always did, like a small holy lie. 

Then the echoes ate each other.  Sefa put her mouth to the reed and blew one thin note.  The seam opened along the ground, the way the driftwood had told her, and she looked down into a river of pale light.  Weaving between worlds was not a door but a stitch: her breath went through, and something on the far side took the thread and pulled.  She saw her sister's shape in the current, keeping station, patient as wood.  One more note and the stitch would tighten enough to haul her up. 

Sefa held the flute away from her lips and watched instead.  This was the harvest she had come for: not a rescue, but the shape of the failing.  Where the gold leaf crossed the reed's split, the note frayed; the thread went through, but nothing breathable came back.  The split was not damage.  It was the flute's second mouth, the one that let the far world exhale into this one, and the guardians had sealed it out of love.  Every failure in her ledger, every arrow on the gravel, was a thing smothered by being preserved. 

The hush began to thin; far up the range, thunder was already loading its next return.  She let the seam close, and her sister with it.  Then she took her knife and scraped ninety years of gold off the split until the reed showed raw and open.  The flakes went into an envelope, labeled, because a keeper keeps even what she destroys.  She wrote the insight beneath the forty-first entry: the seam is a breathing, not a holding.  She turned one driftwood arrow to point straight down, and set a new log beside it, blank, waiting for a bearing.  Next season she would play it uncased in the quiet, and let the canyon breathe back.  The thunder came home over the ridge, and she stood in it, unafraid of noise now that she knew what silence was for.
