My mother kept the night garden in the amber pavilion at the edge of the park.  In autumn the low sun came through those panes and laid the color of steeped tea over her sleeping flowers.  I asked her once why flowers bloom at night.  She said she would tell me when I could see in the dark.  She died in February, and left me a shoebox of old postcards.  Forty-one, from growers she had traded seeds with: a night flower on the front, one line on the back.  Opened at eleven.  Worth staying up.  The moth came.  The last card was unsent, addressed to me in her slanted hand, and it said only: ask the glass.  I paint murals, and my color is loud: marigolds ten feet tall, a fish market that looks wet.  But I am not loud; I am quietly driven, the way water is driven downhill.  That March the city asked me to paint the pavilion's blank wall, and I said yes before they finished asking.  I could not paint night until I understood it.  The pavilion's panes were plain amber; the only glass in the city with anything hidden in it belonged to the cathedral.  The verger let me in because my mother had grown the white flowers for his wife's vigil.  Across the melting of winter's last snow I climbed the cathedral scaffold every morning at five.  The snow went gray, then went to water in the gutters, then was only a damp smell in the stone.  I was learning to see through the hidden layers of etched glass in a cathedral window.  The amber was a skin flashed over clear, and the maker had eaten it away with acid to draw a white lily.  The lily was not paint.  It was absence, and it burned.  Under four centuries of soot I found a second lily, scraped deeper, an earlier attempt the glazier had buried and never mentioned.  Light came through both at once.  That was the lesson: you make brightness by taking away, and one pane can hold two versions of a thing.  On the last morning, in the dawning hush before the sparrows started, the window went from black to bruised to gold.  In that hush I had to reckon with what I had held against my mother.  For years I had counted her night work as absence.  But the flowers did not bloom at night to hide.  They bloomed then because the moth that loved them woke then, and because in the dark scent travels farther than color ever does.  They were not making a spectacle.  They were keeping an appointment.  Her love had opened while I slept, and asked me for nothing, and was simply there in the morning, like scent.  I painted the pavilion wall all spring, on a ground of black.  Then, like the glazier, I took away: scraping, etching, lifting the dark until night flowers stood in the bare plaster, white as absence.  Under the first cereus I left an earlier one, half scraped, the way the cathedral kept its buried lily.  I pinned the postcards along the sill, forty-one small windows.  Now it is autumn again, and the amber glass does what it does.  At four the low sun comes through, the wall's darkness turns warm, and the flowers I cut out of the black begin to glow like things remembered.  The city wanted a bright mural.  I gave them a dark one that gives light back, which is harder, and I think truer.  Tonight I will unlock the pavilion at eleven, put out the lamps, and let the cereus open.  Nobody will see the mural.  They will smell the flowers, and stand before a wall they cannot make out, and feel something looking back at them kindly out of the shadow.  Ask the glass, she wrote.  I did, and it took the whole melting of the snow to answer.  Some things bloom in the dark because the dark is when they are needed.
