The moon crossed the shaft at the third hour, and the cavern orchard went silent.  All day the stalactites had tapped into the basins around the pear roots, a hundred small clocks disagreeing; now the flow held its breath, as it did whenever the light came straight down.  The pears were pale as knuckles and tasted of cold stone, and they fed forty people who never thanked the dark.  Sefa waited for that hush the way other people wait for a witness.  She was a conjurer, and secretive about it, not from shame but because her work needed numbers nobody else was patient enough to check.  Her ledger lived under the floor.  Conjuring, in her hands, was mostly measurement, and she hid it because the village called unfinished hope a haunting, and hauntings were cleared out.  What she wanted was not power.  She wanted the mathematics of emotion, some proof that grief was a series with a sum, a feeling you could carry to its end and set down.  Her brother Teodor had walked into the lower gallery three winters ago, when the seep rose, and the water had kept him.  No body, no word, no last footprint.  The village buried a coffin full of his coats and called the column closed.  Sefa could not make it close.  In the workshop she kept his last order from the glassblower: a taxidermist's pair of glass eyes, amber, one a fraction wider than the other, meant for the fox he had left half-mounted on the bench.  Three years she had refused to set them, because a finished fox would be a finished sentence.  Meanwhile she was gently inventive.  She had cut the basins so no tree thirsted, hung a bell with no clapper so the wind could not lie to her, and trained the pear branches to hold their fruit through the cold.  Small kindnesses, disguised as engineering.  Her instrument was the stepping stone at the orchard's center, where the moonlight landed.  Teodor had laid it as a map of the ceiling, one tessera for every stalactite he had counted.  She kept the map honest: each silent hour she went along the basins with a pipette, drew one bead from every living drip, and set it on its tile.  Tiles whose drips had died went dry, the glaze chipped off in a crescent, and no chip ever healed.  So she read her loss via the chipped patterns on the mosaic stepping stone, and the reading was arithmetic she could survive.  Eleven dead in the north, four in the west, a slow subtraction with edges.  She had not watered the lower gallery quadrant since the flood; she could not go down there, and she would not lie on the map, so she let those tiles go grey.  Tonight the corner tile was wet.  Not misted, but beaded, fresh, holding a lens of moonlight.  Someone had come up from the drowned gallery with a drop of living water and laid it exactly where she would look.  Only two people had ever known what the corner tile meant.  She sat back on her heels and did the only mathematics that mattered.  Water falls only into air.  If a stalactite still dripped down there, the gallery had drained, and the flood had not kept everything it was said to have kept.  That was not a sum.  It was an open series, and someone else's hand in it, and either thing was more than she had held at dusk.  She had spent the autumn making a case for the eyes, pear wood, cutting each corner to dovetail into the next so the box held without glue.  Two pieces cut to keep each other, no adhesive, no proof, only shape.  She had thought it was a coffin.  Now she took the eyes out and carried them to the bench at the orchard's edge, where the fox waited with its empty sockets full of dust.  She set the amber pair, the wider one in the left socket, as he would have, and left the mount's seams open.  Let it stay unfinished; let it look.  She turned the fox toward the gallery mouth so it could watch with her.  Then she went for the lamp, and the rope, and the pipette, because the next term was down there, and the moon would keep the orchard silent a while longer.
