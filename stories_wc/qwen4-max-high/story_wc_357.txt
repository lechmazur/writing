Gravity let go at dawn, and the rivers stood up.  Nima Vess cut her skiff's engine and let the orchard planet turn beneath her, silent as a held breath.  Above her, a thousand rock arches hung in the sky like the ribs of something too large to bury.  For one hour, water would climb them.  For one hour, she would have to find the tree that refused to die. 

Her orchard was dying of abundance.  The up-water accelerated plant growth, and everyone on Tessel praised it.  Pears swelled to fists in nine days; wood went soft as pith; roots could not hold the weight they had made.  Her grandmother's rows had collapsed in a single season, sweet and hollow and rotten at the heart.  But every cutting on Tessel came, generations back, from one mother tree, and the mother tree had never rotted.  Nima wanted the source of that resilience, not another fertilizer, not another graft. 

Her father had wanted it too.  Orchard pilots shot line-arrows to string irrigation across the gaps between arches.  He had flown the inversion twelve years ago and come home feverish, mute, holding a broken arrow tip.  The point had struck bark and split; his half he kept, and the other half stayed in whatever tree he had found.  The fragment rode in Nima's breast pocket now, cold against her ribs. 

Instruments lied during inversion; gyroscopes tumbled, compasses swung with the sky.  So pilots navigated by water flow, reading the climbing threads the way sailors read a tide.  Nima drifted under the first arch and watched the streams braid upward, silver and quick, fat with the stuff that made trees hurry.  She turned away from them.  Her father's half-told lesson, scratched on the back of a seed packet, was the only map she had: follow the water that hesitates.  Arch after arch she went, choosing the thin seep over the torrent, the trickle that took its time.  Nobody flew the slow threads; nothing grew fat enough there to sell. 

The silence whirled.  That was the thing nobody could explain about the inversion hour: the air kept moving, leaves spun, dust devils walked the arches, and none of it made a sound.  Sound went into the stone and stayed there.  Old pilots called it the gathering of echoes: an hour of swallowed noise, pooled somewhere in the rock, waiting to be let out.  Nima had heard it once as a girl, a whole season of axes and laughter falling out of a cliff at once. 

Now she let the skiff drift, unpowered, into a hollow where the water crawled.  The silence there was not empty; it was full, and turning.  Then the stone let go of one sound.  A crack, a snap of iron on wood, and a man's breath caught in surprise.  She knew that breath.  She had heard that breath at her bedside every night of her childhood. 

She followed the echo along the crawling water, and the hollow opened, and there it was.  The mother tree stood under the arch's crown: squat, black-barked, ugly, a broken limb showing rings tight as thread.  The seep that fed it barely moved.  It had refused the quick water for a thousand years, and grown slow, and kept everything it had ever survived.  Fire scar, frost crack, the healed socket of an axe.  And in a burl low on the trunk, a shard of iron with a fracture face like a tiny cliff. 

Nima took out her half and fitted it home.  The break matched, edge for edge.  He had discovered it and lost the words; she had found it again and would keep them.  She did not take the fast, pale shoots at the crown.  She cut one scion from the burl, from the slow wood the tree had made around the wound.  The stone gave her back his laugh, then his engine, then nothing. 

Forty minutes gone.  She rode the last of the climbing water down through the arches, reading the flow backward, and came into her own rows as gravity turned over and the rivers fell.  The sound of the world came back all at once, water hammering stone, and she let it. 

She grafted the scion into her grandmother's ruined stock and watered it with slow cistern rain, not the quick.  It would take nine years to bear.  Resilience, she wrote in the ledger, is not speed; it is what the tree keeps of the arrow.  She had nine years.
