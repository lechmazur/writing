The grotto held its heat all night, so the moss stayed green above the clouds.  Ines climbed into it before dawn with a bundle of willow rods on her back and her mother's hymnal in her coat.  Below, the reservoir lay under cloud, flat and patient, covering ninety-one households the county ledger called uninhabited.  She called herself an esoteric realist, which meant she believed in signs but refused to believe one until she had measured it.  The sign she was measuring this morning was the migration of the last monarch butterfly.  Bel, the ranger's son, was already at the counting stone with his notebook.  Eleven in October, he said.  Then three.  Then, this week, one.  He said it the way you say a number you have checked twice, quietly, hoping the arithmetic will change. 

Ines set down the rods and began to build.  Not a monument.  A signal: a willow tripod lashed at the crown, a crossbar, a square of undyed cloth strung between the poles so it caught the light and could be read from the far ridge.  Temporary structures, always.  Willow rots; cloth frays; by spring the whole thing would sag into the moss and feed it.  That was the point.  Bel asked why she did not carve the names in stone, like the war memorial in the town.  Because stone lets you stop, she said.  Because a thing you must rebuild every year is a thing you are still remembering.  Her hands were slow and certain, and her face was warm, and she was glad the light was still low. 

The grotto's plants were transiently rooted, holding to a finger's depth of moss over rock, thriving because the vents breathed warm and the clouds came in at dusk to water them.  Nothing here had to be permanent to be alive. 

She opened the hymnal to the page her mother had marked.  A moth wing lay there, brown as tea, the size of a thumbnail, kept flat for forty years.  It marked the last measure of the drowning hymn, the one the village sang the night before the water came.  The phrase climbed, leaned toward home, and then turned aside into the minor chord, the interrupted cadence, so that the ear kept waiting for a floor that never arrived.  Her mother had explained it once, shelling beans: you cannot leave a song hanging in someone else's ear and walk away.  Somebody has to answer it. 

Ines had come up here intending to answer at last.  One tower, one cloth, one final measure sung straight through to its ending, and then she could be an old woman who had finished.  She had told no one this, and her cheeks went hot as if she had. 

The sun came over the ridge and turned the cloud sea rose, and the grotto went absolutely quiet, the kind of quiet that feels like being looked at kindly.  The monarch arrived in that stillness, low over the moss, and settled on the crossbar with its wings opening and closing like a slow hand.  Bel stopped writing.  Neither of them spoke; there is a shyness that comes over people when the last of something trusts them enough to rest. 

Ines took the moth wing out of the hymnal and laid it on her palm, and the wind off the vents lifted it once and set it down again.  Then she did the thing she had come to do, only not the way she had planned it.  She tied the wing into the crown of the tripod with a single knot, the quill caught in the loop, the brown blade hanging free against the cloth.  A knot does not end a rope.  It turns it back on itself and makes it hold. 

She sang the phrase, the ninety-one names folded into its verses, and at the last measure she let it lean toward home and turn aside.  The unresolved chord sat in the grotto, warm, unfinished, unbearable.  And Bel, who had never heard the hymn, who had only been taught to count, hummed the next line.  It was wrong, and it was an answer, and Ines put her hand over her mouth and laughed with her eyes wet. 

She taught him the knot, and the lashing, and the names in their order.  By spring the willow would soften and the moss would take it, and someone would have to climb again.  The monarch lifted off the crossbar, circled the cloth once, and went south over the rose-colored cloud.  They watched until it was a number again, and then until it was nothing, and the grotto kept its blush and its quiet, and the song stayed open.
