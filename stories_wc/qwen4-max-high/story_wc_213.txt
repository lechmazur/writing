Nell Farrow tested storm sirens for the district, grade two, which meant she climbed to the horns and nobody climbed to her.  Her office was the sanctuary of Saint Anselm on the col, the smallest cathedral in the diocese, where the wind never stopped and the cloud sea lay below the windows like a second, quieter earth.  Solstice morning, zero hour at seven: the whole network would sound for two minutes, and she would sign the coverage certificate for the valley.  She had a problem with the certificate, and the problem was written in three winters of notebooks.  Nell tracked microclimate variations the way other people kept grudges: temperature and wet bulb at the col, at the mid pasture, at the river bridge, wind at each, four times daily, in a hand like small nails.  The notebooks said that on nights like this, cold air pooled under the cloud and warm air rode above it, and sound went where the layers told it to go.  The horn's tone would leap over the hamlet of Vellin and land, thin and useless, in the next valley, where the cows were already nervous.  Vellin, which flooded first, would hear nothing at all.  She had asked the district engineer twice, politely, and he had tapped his model and told her the model was newer than her thermometers.  It was a fair answer, so she had gone home and checked her thermometers against a borrowed standard, twice, and then checked the standard.  Curiosity, in Nell, was not a sparkle; it was the habit of assuming she might be wrong and finding out.  She had never wanted to be the loudest thing on the mountain; she wanted to know what the mountain was already saying.  Now she had one minute of the engineer's patience left, and a bell.  The cathedral's bell had hung silent since the hammer motor burned out in the eighties, and its rope had frayed to a whisper above the ringing chamber.  She had spliced it on Thursday with hide glue and a leather sleeve, and hide glue will not set in a cold church.  So the splice lay against the stove all night in a hot cloth, and Nell's whole plan had a cure time.  Everything waited on the glue: after it dried, the rope would hold her weight, and not one minute sooner.  At six she unwrapped the sleeve and pressed a thumbnail into the seam and felt no give, and the hush of the solstice settled over the col like snow that had decided not to fall.  The engineer's voice came up the radio line, tired and not unkind.  "One minute, Farrow, then I sound the network."  "Don't sound it," she said. "Switch the horns to listen."  Every horn in the district doubled as a microphone for calibration; that was how she had learned what the layers did.  "Switch to listen, and let the valley tell us what it hears."  "Your minute," he said.  She threw her own panel from tone to listen, and the little lamps went green down the board, one valley station at a time.  Then she went up and took the cathedral bell rope in both hands.  She hung her weight on it slowly, the way you ask a question of something old.  The splice held.  The bell spoke once, low, a note beneath the horn's register, and the sound went down the slope into the cloud and kept going.  On the board, Vellin's needle lifted.  Then the bridge, then the mill, then the far station in the next valley, faint, the way she had predicted, and the engineer said nothing for a long time.  In the headphones she could hear what the horn would have drowned: the river under ice, a dog in Vellin, a door.  "Zero hour," the engineer said at last, and she heard him write it down.  "Log it as a bell test," Nell said. "Two minutes of listening, if you can spare it."  He could spare it.  She rang the bell nine times for the hamlet, slowly, and between the strokes she stood in the ringing chamber with her hands open and let the mountain be quiet.  Afterward she warmed the splice again, because glue keeps curing long after it holds, and wrote the seven o'clock temperatures in her small nailed hand.  The certificate would take a year to change; the rope would take a winter of careful splices; the cloud sea went on lying below the windows, and above it the wind, and under it, awake now, the valley listening.
