In the ice cavern beneath the plaza, quiet came in tides: a long blue calm, then a shiver of light falling from the roof.  Sena waited inside that rhythm with her hands folded, breathing when the cavern breathed.  She was the last prism-song bell ringer, and she had been born synesthetically blessed, which the Bureau called a defect and her teacher had called an instrument.  Bells spoke to her in colors; colors, when she struck true, spoke back as bells.  Above her, the city had voted wonder ornamental, and the eight bells hung fettered, clappers bound in felt and wire.  In her lap lay the amber music sheet, a slab of old resin with the score trapped inside it like a wasp in honey.  Her teacher had poured it the winter the aurora last ran green to violet, so the notes were not ink but color, and only color could read them.  Footsteps came down the stair, unhurried, then stopped.  Auditor Corvin arrived with his ledger, his stamp, and the cold patience of a man who had never needed anything useless.  "You may play nothing," he said.  "I came to certify the silence."  Sena did not argue; argument was noise, and noise would ruin what she meant to make.  At dawn the sculptor Halden would strike the first chisel into the marble block for the Monument to Necessity, and the whole city would listen for it.  Halden's craft was famous: he said marble was only the crust, and that what he truly carved was a hush, a silence shaped around a shape.  Sena had copied that sentence onto her wrist in charcoal.  A sculpted hush, she understood, is soft only once: at the moment the chisel first strikes, before the silence learns its edges.  Fill it then, and the hush would set around the sound forever.  Leave it empty, and the city would have its proof that wonder could be done without.  She did not want a rebellion; she wanted a signature.  "One minute," she told Corvin.  "Then you may stamp whatever you like."  He looked at the fettered bells and, after a while, sat on the ice with his ledger closed, which she took for permission.  The cavern settled; the light shivered; her heart went loud and then quiet, loud and then quiet.  She took the wire cutters from her coat and began to unfetter the clappers, one felt binding at a time, slow as unbandaging a wound.  Corvin's pen moved, then stopped; he wrote nothing.  Overhead, through the shaft the old ringers had cut, the aurora woke.  Sena lifted the amber sheet into that falling light and let the colors of the aurora run through it, green over rose over a violet so deep it tasted of iron.  The trapped notes lit in order, and she read them the way she had been taught: not as symbols but as hues she owed the world.  Through forty feet of ice, down the light shaft, a small hard sound arrived.  The chisel.  Sena struck the green bell into the newborn hush.  Then rose, then violet, then the whole prism-song unfolding, each tone matched to a color still moving in the sky, so that the light above and the sound below were one instrument played by two hands.  The ice took the song and gave it back multiplied.  Corvin stood without deciding to.  He was seeing it, she realized; the blessing was not a private defect but a door, and she had held it open.  His lips moved on a word he had not used since childhood, and his eyes were wet and green.  The song ended; the hush closed around it and hardened, and the cavern was quiet again, but the quiet now had a shape.  Corvin opened the ledger on his knee.  The form offered two boxes, ORNAMENTAL and ESSENTIAL, and he did not tick either; he wrote a sentence instead.  "Wonder is essential," he wrote, and then, because the form demanded a reason, "It bears load."  He stamped it, and his hand shook so badly the seal came out crooked.  Above them the second chisel stroke fell, and the third, Halden carving his marble around a hush that already had bells in it.  Sena sat down on the ice beside the auditor and let her hands go still.  The aurora thinned to a pale green wash; the cavern breathed out, breathed in; the amber cooled in her lap, holding its colors for the next hundred winters.  "Will they listen?" Corvin asked.  "They will hear the monument," she said, "and wonder what is inside it."
