The storm had been arriving for eleven years.  Lightning opened beyond the ridge, and in the lull while its thunder traveled, Sallow moved.  When the sound reached the valley, everything stopped, including the rain, including Ivo Brant.  He had been tutor of chess to a prince; the court exiled him here with one clause, that he remain inside his consequence.  He had taught the boy zugzwang, the art of leaving an opponent no move but ruin.  The boy grew, besieged this valley, and applied the lesson to people.  A dying weaver answered with a woven hush over Sallow, holding the soldiers' sabers an inch above the bread they were cutting.  Mercy, and a prison.  Rain hung in the air like nails; the millrace stood in a rope of glass.  In his pocket lay the platinum labyrinth, a palm-sized maze under glass, the prince's parting gift.  A steel seed sat in its channel; tilt, and it ran; tilt wrong, and it wedged.  Ivo fidgeted with it always, and the fidgeting was work.  His hands learned what his eyes could not: the maze had a grammar, false turns breaking left before every true one.  The hush hung in the church as a tapestry, Sallow rendered in wool, every villager mid-gesture.  Its border was fraying, and Ivo had come to believe the fraying was writing.  Knots, gaps, knots: dead ends, and one path.  Each lull gave him a hand's width of border, no more; then the thunder took him mid-breath.  Eleven years of lulls, and he had copied the border into memory like a man copying a board.  To read it he had to winnow: he blew across the fray, the rot flew off as chaff, the knotted strands stayed.  Grain from chaff, meaning from decay; the prince had learned only the sacrifice, never that patience.  The border decoded into one instruction: pull the weft at the church door, and the hush unravels, and time resumes.  Time resuming meant sabers finishing their inch.  It meant the bread cut, the child's laugh completed, the village killed on schedule.  Outside the hush, the siege camp had aged eleven years, waiting for the weaver's knot to slip.  For years he left the thread alone and called it penance.  Penance was only another way of keeping them mid-breath.  So he began to play the position instead of mourning it.  One lull, one move: a woman carried three steps toward the cellar, a boy set beside her, the miller's arm folded out of the saber's arc.  Between thunderclaps he stood statue-still with a child in his arms, and the stillness was not peace but a drawn bow.  He winnowed his choices the same way, blowing away the grand rescues, keeping the small possible ones.  Forty-one villagers, four hundred lulls, his hands never ceasing to tilt the labyrinth and rehearse routes.  The maze's last trick was a deliberate dead end: lodge the seed, and the gate springs.  He had taught the prince to spend pawns; the border asked for the piece he had never offered.  On the last lull he laid the miller's wife in the cellar, counted forty-one breaths below him, and walked to the door.  He set the platinum labyrinth on the sill, seed lodged in its dead end, for whoever came after.  Then he pulled the weft.  The hush came apart with a sound like a long exhale, the tapestry fell as thread, and the thunder arrived.  Sabers came down on empty air and bread.  Below the floor, forty-one people found the cellar's far hatch and went out into the orchard rain.  The soldiers found a man in the doorway with a board on his knees, the pieces set from memory.  He did not plead; he had stopped needing pardon from anyone but himself, and that argument was finished.  He could not unmake the lesson; he could only refuse to leave the board as he had left it.  "You taught me the quiet move," the prince said from the saddle, older, terrible, familiar.  Ivo sat still as a stone in a river and moved a pawn one square.  "Your move," he said.
