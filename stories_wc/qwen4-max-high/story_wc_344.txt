The library stood above the clouds, frozen mid-breath, its windows furred with ice.  Maren had climbed nine hours to reach it, and she had until the eclipse ended.  She was a lunar-eclipse locksmith, which meant her trade only worked in shadow.  Moonlocks softened under a bitten moon and hardened at the first true light.  Her grandmother had taught her opening, never mending.  She had opened ninety-one doors in her life and never once left one better than she found it.  The sea took the village when Maren was eleven, and left driftwood.  Silvered sills, a boat's rib, her grandmother's door bleached the color of bone.  She had kept that door for twenty years without knowing how to make it whole.  That was why she came up: to learn the rhythm of repair.  In her coat pocket she carried her grandmother's clockmaker's gear assortment, a tin of brass wheels sorted by tooth.  Her grandmother had bought it the year before the water came, and never once opened it. 

The moonlock at the library's gate gave with a sigh, like a held breath let go.  Inside, frost had stopped the shelves in the act of being read.  Books lay open, their pages stiff as sailcloth.  At the library's heart waited the hall of mirrors.  Forty panes in a ring, and in each one the drowned coast, still dry.  Gulls hung motionless above the harbor in every reflection.  The hall was hauntingly bright, brighter than any eclipse had a right to make it, because the mirrors kept every morning they had ever been given.  At the center stood the clock that turned them, and it had stopped.  Three of its wheels had shed their teeth.  Without the turning, the mirrors would frost over, and the coast would be lost twice.  Maren knew how to open the clock's case; she did not know what to do afterward. 

So she went where her grandmother's last letter told her to go: the corner shelf, third bay, behind the tide tables.  There, folded into the wood like splinters, were scraps of prophecy.  Every locksmith who came before her had written one and hidden it there, calling it prophecy so the librarians would never dare throw it out.  "The hand will shake at the third tooth," one read.  "Let it shake."  "Repair is not a straight line; it is a tide."  "Set, rest, set, rest; the resting is the mending."  She read them all by the copper light of the eaten moon. 

Then she opened the tin.  The wheels lay in her grandmother's careful rows, each in a twist of paper, unused since the water.  The paper had gone soft as driftwood pulp.  Maren fitted the first wheel.  It fouled.  She fitted it again, and her hand shook at the third tooth, exactly as promised.  She let it shake.  She rested.  In forty mirrors her hands rested too, forty grandmothers' hands, patient in the salt light.  Set, rest, set, rest.  Somewhere in the fourth repetition the rhythm arrived, not as knowledge but as weather, the way the sea had always arrived at the door.  Her hands stopped asking permission.  The escape wheel caught, hesitated, and turned. 

The hall began to move.  Mirror by mirror the coast came around: the harbor, the fish sheds, the door of her grandmother's house with the paint still on it.  Frost let go of the shelves with a sound like a net being hauled.  Far below the clouds, under the soft hush of a distant morning, the sky was going gray at its seam.  The eclipse was ending; the moonlock would harden soon.  Maren did not run.  She knelt at the corner shelf to uncap the little bottle of ink she had carried up the mountain.  On a scrap torn from a tide table she wrote her own prophecy.  "She will come with the tin and think she is too late."  "The hand will shake."  "Let it."  "Set, rest, set, rest."  "Nothing the water took is mended in one turning."  She folded it small and pushed it into the wood beside the others, where it fit like a splinter returning to its board. 

Then she capped the ink and went out through the turning hall, past forty mornings that had not been lost after all.  At the gate the moonlock was stiffening, and she left it a finger's width open for whoever came next.  Below the clouds the morning came on softly, and she thought about a door.  She would go home and mend it, slowly, with rests between.
