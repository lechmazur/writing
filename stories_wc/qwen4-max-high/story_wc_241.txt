The rain had washed the library clean and left it smelling of wet stone and crushed clover.  Midsummer light came through the tall windows the color of weak tea.  I had come to build a syllabus.  Luck, Risk, and Reason: eleven weeks in which undergraduates would learn that a number can be certain and a man can be sure, and that these are different animals.  The dean had approved the models and refused the gamblers.  Widen perspectives responsibly, I had written in the proposal, which meant: let the ruined speak, and do not let ruin look like romance.  I am a professor of probability, and my father is a compulsive gambler.  Six weeks after the heart attack, he stopped betting and started riding.  The doctor had ordered him to walk, so he walked the loop line one stop a day, thirty-eight stops in all, and rode home on the train.  He told me none of this.  He left it on the seats.  Carpenter's pencil on cracked vinyl: 9/38, a three-lobed clover, an arrow.  For twenty years he had scribbled odds on every soft surface in our apartment, and I had built a career proving those scribbles worthless.  I was rebellious in theory only.  I had never placed a bet, never raised my voice, never asked him a single question about why.  Now the denominators were all thirty-eight, and the numerators climbed by one each morning, and I understood that my father was counting himself back to life and leaving me the tally.  So I traversed the line backward, riding car after car, reading seats.  At Kessler Street I found 22/38, the clover drawn with care, the same three leaves he used to press into my library books.  At Bellweather, 27/38, and under it a line in his bookmaker's shorthand: even money you come.  Yesterday I had found 37/38 on a seat by the door, and today the arithmetic said thirty-eight.  Thirty-eight was the library.  He had been the night custodian here for nineteen years, mopping under the reading lamps while I did my homework in the stacks and he called the track on a pay phone in the boiler room.  Nineteen years of wages, gone on horses.  The storm took the power at four.  The librarian brought out the emergency cabinet, and inside it, in a foam cradle, was his old candle lantern with the mica pane.  He had never trusted the wiring; he had carried that lantern on his cart, and I had learned fractions in its amber.  Mica is not glass; it cleaves into sheets, and it splits one flame into two.  You can see through it and not see through it.  He came up the aisle at four-fifteen, walking slowly, one hand flat on the shelf ends.  Thirty-eight of thirty-eight.  "You read them," he said.  "You left them where I would look."  I set the lantern on the table between us and asked him to speak to my class.  Not to warn them, I said, and not to charm them: to be accurate.  He wiped the mica with his thumb.  "I knew every number," he said.  "The house edge, the variance, the ruin problem."  "I could prove it to you on a napkin, and then I would go and bet, and both things were lit up in me at once, and I was not asleep for either one."  That was the thing I had come for, though I had told the dean it was testimony.  A lucid contradiction: two truths awake in one man, refusing to settle.  I have spent my life collapsing them into one clean answer, and calling it rigor.  Outside, the rain stopped, and the clover lawn let up its smell through the open transom.  He asked if I wanted to hear about the worst night.  I said yes, and took out a pen, and did not forgive him.  Both lit, neither asleep.  On Monday I wrote his name onto the syllabus, under Week Three, and sent it to the dean without softening it.  I paired his hour with the ruin problem set, so that no student could hear the story without the arithmetic.  Theoretically rebellious for twenty years, I had finally risked something small and real.  In the dark of the stacks he had held the lantern steady while I wrote, and the mica had given me two flames out of one candle.  I let them both stand.
