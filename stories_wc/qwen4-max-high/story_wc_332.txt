The library on Tallow Island opened at three in the morning and closed before five, and Vela was its only librarian.  The islanders called her the odd-hour librarian, and they came only when they could not sleep.  They came to have their whispers read.  The Whispering Archipelago earned its name honestly: wind combed the hollow basalt of eleven islands and came out sounding almost like speech.  Vela had learned, in nine years of odd hours, that the whispers were letters nobody had sent.  They were encrypted, and the key was never a word; it was a wanting.  A fisherman's whisper opened only for someone who also wanted a son to answer a letter.  The old cataloguers called it the cipher of longing, and their index cards filled forty drawers. 

Iona, her sister, had left behind a watercolor palette with every pan scraped down to the tin.  Iona had rowed toward the outer islands nine winters ago and the sea had kept her.  For a year afterward Vela dreamed of Iona's kitchen window, lit amber, waiting.  Then the dream stopped coming, and the ferryman reported a light out past Little Breath where no house stood.  A dream that leaves its sleeper does not die at once; it goes wild, and thins, and takes the wanting with it when it goes.  Vela had felt herself going flat, unable to want breakfast, or morning, or anything at all.  So she meant to catch the runaway dream before it guttered out. 

Iona had never painted with pigment.  She painted with light, and the palette was only her way of carrying it.  Vela had taught herself the rest from Iona's margins: photograph the sunrise, print the exposure onto the empty pans, and the light dries there into cakes you can load with a wet brush.  Painted light does not depict a thing; it vitalizes it.  That was the whole difference between a picture of a window and a window that burns. 

For a month she listened for the dream's own whisper and could not read it, because the cipher opens only for a matching want.  On the last night of the month she stopped transcribing and simply wanted her sister back, aloud, badly, and the wind resolved into a bearing: two points west of Little Breath.  She rowed out in the hush of improbable beginnings, that minute before dawn when the water holds still and anything might start.  The camera hung on her chest; the palette sat open on the thwart. 

The bothy on the skerry had lost its roof fifty years ago, and in its wall stood a kitchen window, lit amber, with nothing behind it.  No Iona, no table, no kettle; only the light her sister would have left on.  It was thin as a breath on glass, and it flickered when she raised the camera.  She could photograph the window and keep it, a picture of a thing gone, or she could turn the lens east, take the sunrise instead, and spend that light to vitalize it.  Keeping it would have been a small, cold ownership.  She turned the lens east. 

The sun came up over the outer water and she took one exposure, and the film drank the whole gold minute of it.  She printed it into the pans as Iona's margins said, and the light dried there, warm as a held hand.  Then she wetted the brush and painted the window with light: the frame, the mullion, the amber, the empty room.  It stopped flickering.  It steadied, and warmed, and began to burn on its own, the way a lamp burns after you have walked away from it.  The room stayed empty; she had not painted her sister, because her sister was not a thing longing could make. 

By the next week the ferrymen were steering by it, and they had begun to call it Iona's window.  The library still opened at three.  Vela still read the whispers, but the old key would no longer turn in her; she could not decode a wanting she had stopped having.  She was learning a new one from the fishermen: that the boats come in, that the light holds, that morning is worth wanting.  The palette was empty again, and she kept it on the desk where the sun crossed it at four, and for an hour every morning the tin held a little light she had not put there.
