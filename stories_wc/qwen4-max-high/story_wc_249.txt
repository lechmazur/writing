The consent form called me Subject Eleven, and beneath that, in smaller type, healthy volunteer, which is a polite word for guinea pig.  I signed it in the corridor outside the sleep ward, where my sister Ada had been breathing through a machine for nine weeks.  The trial was named Entanglement: two minds tuned to one frequency until their memories crossed and became a single thing held in common.  They needed a waking mind to do the pulling.  They needed someone who would not let go of the rope. 

They put me in the sound wave garden, because resonance travels better through soil than through concrete.  It was a courtyard of tuned beds, sand rippling into stars and lattices above each buried speaker, reeds cut to lengths that hummed a scale when the wind moved.  Ada had helped plant it the summer before the lagoon took her. 

Protocol said: lie flat, palms up, swallow the blue pill that stilled involuntary movement.  My chart called me compliant, but Ada knew better: quietly rebellious, she used to say, like water finding the seam under a door.  I kept the pill under my tongue until the orderlies turned, then planted it in the sand.  Instead of lying flat, I perched on the low wall of the resonance basin, heels hooked on the hollow tile, where my hands could reach the whole garden. 

I have drummed on everything since I was four: table edges, bus windows, Ada's shoulder blade in the dark.  She taught me the lagoon rhythm before either of us could read.  Three quick, one slow, two quick: the way water slapped the shale at the old gold mine, where the tailings had turned the pond a strange, beautiful yellow.  We called it the auric lagoon, and we swam in it under starlight, and the next day the levee broke and she went under and came up breathing nothing. 

The entanglement worked, after a fashion.  I would close my eyes and find her memories in my hands, cold and not mine: a dog she never told me about, a boy, the taste of a peach.  She got mine, and the technicians got graphs, and nobody got Ada back.  On the eleventh night the doctor used the word they had all been avoiding, and I understood that I was not pulling her out; I was only being let in. 

So I stopped pulling.  There were twelve sleepers in that ward, and the garden's speaker array ran beneath their beds as well, and the entangled net had never learned to keep a thing to itself.  If memory was common property now, then I could give some away.  I could not return her body, but I could breathe life into a dream the whole ward could stand inside. 

The synchronizing pulse came at 3:33, and between pulses lay a null interval the technicians called the inhale: four seconds of absolute quiet, the hush of the universe inhaling.  I waited for it.  Then I drummed.  Heels on the tile, palms on the basin rim, fingertips on the reeds, three quick, one slow, two quick, until the sand rose in its little stars and the whole garden rang like water on shale.  I gave them the yellow water and the cold shale and the stars lying flat on the surface, the smell of Ada's sunscreen, her laugh coming across the pond.  Behind me, in the ward, a monitor changed its note. 

In the morning Ada's heart stopped, quietly, without argument.  But the boy in bed four, who had not spoken in two years, asked his mother for a swimming lesson.  The woman in bed nine said gold, and then said stars, and then wept.  The doctor asked what I had done to his garden, and I told him I had planted something. 

They will not run the trial again.  Eleven people who never met my sister now carry the auric lagoon under starlight, and when the wind moves through the reeds they hum, all of them, three quick, one slow, two quick.  She is not alive.  She is held in common.
