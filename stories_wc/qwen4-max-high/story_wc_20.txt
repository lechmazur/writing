The search party disbanded on the ninth morning, folding their lanterns into the rain.  They left the foundry to me and the water.  Rain had washed the slag heaps until the yard glittered like something forgiven.  Inside, the crucibles held cold sky, and the molds smelled of wet iron and old smoke.  I am a balanced alchemist: nothing given that is not measured, nothing taken that is not entered twice.  My brother Teodor was my apprentice, and I entered him too.  In our craft the mystical is factually so; a name has mass, and any apprentice with a brass balance can prove it.  Teodor's weighed four grams and eleven hundredths the day we cast his zodiac coin, the Ram stamped at the rim, his letters struck beneath.  Every apprentice got one at naming; the twelve signs turned around the rim like a year you could hold.  He was fourteen and proud of being a fact.  He used to set the coin on the balance himself, to watch the beam bow, to be sure the world had him.  In spring I needed weight to fix a transmutation, so I shaved two grams from his letters and told myself the ledger would balance by autumn.  A lightened name does not vanish; it only stops holding its body to the world.  When the millrace broke and the flood crossed the casting floor, Teodor went up instead of down.  They dragged nine days and found one boot.  The searchers said the river keeps what it takes, and they said his name gently, as though gentleness were weight enough.  His coin, cracked where I had cut it, stayed in my apron.  Before I wanted him back, I wanted to understand the weight of names: what, exactly, a gram of my brother had been.  So I went to the limits.  I shaved a hundredth from my own coin and spent the afternoon gripping the bench while my heels lifted.  I shaved another, and my hair rose like flame, and the beam trembled, honest as arithmetic.  At the third hundredth I could read the rain through my hand.  That was the edge: below it, a person stops being a fact and becomes a rumor.  Two grams had not drowned him; they had made him arguable, and the water had argued.  I wept in the rain-washed dark, and the tears went upward, and they shone.  My teacher had taught kintsugi, though not for bowls.  You cannot mend a boy, she said; you can mend the intention that broke him, and the seam must show.  Gold in the crack, so the repair is never a lie.  I lit the furnace for the first time since the searchers left.  To redeem his coin I melted my own into gold, every gram of my name but the last legal hundredth, and poured it into the crack.  It ran into the shaved letters and filled them; the Ram stood up out of the seam.  I set the mended coin on the pan.  Four grams and eleven hundredths, then five, then six, then more than any boy has ever weighed.  I wrote the numbers down, because a fact is what you can hand to someone else.  Tomorrow I will take the coin to the town clerk, who believes nothing, and let him read the beam himself.  The ledger will never balance now; the gold seam is the difference, and it is visible forever.  That is the repair: not a boy returned, but an intention made honest and heavy.  I understand names now: their weight is how much of the world agrees to keep holding you.  His name is anchored; the river cannot lift it, and whoever reads my ledger will find him measurable, and therefore impossible to dismiss.  I am the light one now.  My heels leave the floor when I breathe, so I have tied a rope from my belt to the anvil and I work at the length of it.  Some nights the rain comes through the roof and I hold his coin up, and the seam throws its bright scar across the wet walls.  I say Teodor aloud, and the name does not drift, and I regret everything in a way that gives off light.
