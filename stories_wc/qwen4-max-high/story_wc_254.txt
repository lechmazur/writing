The ban lifted on a Tuesday, quietly, the way frost lets go of a window.  Nobody rang a bell; a clerk simply peeled the seal from the atrium door and went home to his soup.  Vera heard the news at dusk and walked to the cobalt atrium by the frozen lake.  Lanterns hung along the colonnade, and moths pressed themselves to the glass, hushed, patient, willing to burn for a little light.  They called her the cloud-shadow poet.  For nine years she had written her poems where no warden could confiscate them: in the shadows clouds dragged across the ice, line by line, gone before anyone could copy them down.  The registry demanded her words anyway, so she gave them paraphrase.  She flattened every banned line into safe prose: the poem about her mother's hands became a statement regarding domestic labor.  The clerks stamped it and she walked out with her mouth tasting of tin.  It was not the danger that had worn her down; it was the ugliness.  She had never once been brave for a cause, only for a cadence, and she knew this about herself the way you know a crooked tooth.  Now the seal was gone and the atrium stood open, cobalt glass holding the last blue of the sky, and she stopped at the sill.  Twelve quartz pillars ran from the hall to the shore, the humming colonnade her teacher had tuned by ear before the wardens took him.  They were humming still, faintly, the way a struck bowl hums long after the strike.  Beyond them the lake lay white and sealed, and the sill under her boots was the old sacred line: no poet crosses to the ice.  The law was dead; the reverence was not.  Nine years of not-crossing had worn a groove in her deeper than any statute.  She wanted to stride over it, not creep, not ask.  She wanted the boundary to stop being holy.  Her teacher had a saying about certainty: it does not arrive like a trumpet, it arrives like a tremor in the breastbone, small, and then you are already moving.  She had felt nothing at the sill for years, only the groove.  So she did the only thing she knew how to do beautifully.  She spoke the poem about her mother's hands, exactly, every vowel restored, no paraphrase, into the nearest pillar.  The quartz took her voice the way ice takes a stone.  The hum climbed to meet the note she had chosen, and the twelve pillars answered one another down the hall, and the sound went out through the open doors and onto the lake.  This was what the wardens had actually forbidden, not the words: the colonnade dissolved things.  Sung true, it thinned the seam between hall and shore, between the voice inside a woman and the air over a frozen lake, until neither could say where it ended.  The frost along the sill went to water and then to nothing.  The moths lifted off the lantern glass and drifted out past her into the cold, and the hum went with them, and they did not fall.  And there it was: a tremor of certainty, low under her sternum, no trumpet, no permission.  She did not step over the sacred line.  There was no line left to step over; she had dissolved it, and so she simply strode, the way you walk out of a room into a garden.  Cobalt light lay behind her on the ice like a spilled inkwell.  She kept speaking, and the pillars kept carrying it, and the hush did not break so much as widen to hold her.  A cloud shadow slid over the ice and over her boots, and her words went on existing outside it, aloud, in the air, where anyone could hear them.  The clerk had come back to see whether anyone would use the door; he stood in it now, hands at his sides, writing nothing down.  Later she would be asked what the crossing meant, and she would refuse to paraphrase.  She would say: come to the atrium at dusk, when the lanterns are lit and the moths are quiet against the glass, and stand at the sill until you feel the small tremor, and then walk.
