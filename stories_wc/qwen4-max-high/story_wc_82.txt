After my shadow detached, it stayed in the tea garden, kneeling between the rows as if it still had a wife to mourn.  I watched it every dawn from my bench, that dark patch shaped like a man, while I grew brighter and could not weep.  Grief in the high isles does not darken a man; it lights him from inside until his hands are the color of weak tea held to the sun.  I am the lantern maker, and the village calls me crafty, which is a polite word for a man who sells other people's dreams back to them at a markup.  The glass basilica hangs beneath our island, root-down in the mist, and its nave is where dreams are recorded.  You sleep on the cold floor, the engine draws the dream out of you, and it sets in a bead of glass no bigger than a plum stone.  Polishing is the priests' work.  They cut facets until the dream inside can be thrown by lamplight onto rice paper, and that is how the archive reads: light through glass, and the dream arrives as handwriting.  Mira's handwriting, in the one dream I could never keep.  Every night she came to the foot of the stairs with a crane in her palm, and every night I woke before she opened her hand.  She used to fold cranes out of the archive's spoiled transcripts, and she taught me the trick: fold grief small enough and it can fly.  I could not fold mine because I could not read it; it stayed inside me, glowing, unspeakable.  So I went down to the basilica at dawn, when the isle climbs.  Our island rises with the sun, and the only stair to the nave winds down through the tea terraces, so you descend while ascending, brushing wet leaves at every landing, the mist going past your knees.  The recorder took my tithe of tea, looked at my shining hands, and told me the truth: a dream that is only grief records as a blank.  So I cheated.  Under my head I put a lantern pane I had never sold, a scrap of an old recording: Mira in the tea rows, laughing, alive, ordinary.  The engine took the bait, and my grief closed around her like a hand around a coal.  The bead came out white as a cataract, and the priest said the polishing would take a month.  I left a tourist's dream of flying in its cradle, matched to the grain, and carried mine up the terraces in my mouth.  At my bench I did what I have done to glass for thirty years.  Ash of tea leaves for the rough cut, rouge for the finish, water to keep the glass from screaming.  Twenty facets, because twenty sheets of paper make twenty cranes, and a crane is a thing that can leave.  Every pass thins the dream; polish too hungry and you wear the loved face away.  By the twelfth facet my hands were lanterns themselves, and the torment was not darkness but light, light with nowhere to fall.  I set the finished bead in my best lantern and threw it onto the paper, and there was her handwriting, twenty pages of it, saying what I had always woken too early to hear.  Fold me small, Senne, and then let me go.  That is the whole of my craft, the only transform I trust: sorrow into glass, glass into light, light into paper, paper into wings.  I folded nineteen cranes on the terrace while the isle climbed, and each one lifted a piece of the burning out of me.  They went off the edge into the mist and did not come down.  With the nineteenth I saw the terrace boards through my own knees, and my feet stopped pressing the ground.  My shadow had knelt in the rows for a year holding everything heavy that the light burned out of me, and I had been giving away the last of it.  I left the twentieth sheet flat in my pocket, unfolded, a small square weight.  Then I went down through the rows to where my shadow knelt, and it stood, and it came home to my heels.  The isle kept climbing, and I knelt in the wet tea, and I wept, which is heavy, and being heavy I stayed.
