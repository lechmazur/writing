The moon stood over Sill Crater like a lamp left burning in an empty house.  Ilsa climbed the summit rim with a plate camera on her back and her father's hourglass in her coat.  Below her the crater floor lay pale and perfect, a bowl the space age had stopped needing.  Crews had trained here for forty years, walking the slopes in suits, learning the Moon on Earth.  Now there was a real Moon to walk on, and the analog was scheduled to be filled and graded into a launch apron at sunrise.  Ilsa was the crater inspector who had to sign it spent.  Her father was already at the summit, sitting on the watch stool with his lamp unlit.  She had carried the glass up because his knees no longer trusted the switchbacks.  Forty-one years of nightwatch, and the dust had worn his lungs to paper.  He timed his rounds by sand because the crater's iron made every clock lie.  One grain had hung in the hourglass neck since the night of the first launch, wedged, refusing to fall.  "Watch isn't over till the last grain goes," he said.  Ilsa had been purposefully curious all her life, the way other people are stubborn.  She had measured this crater two hundred times and asked it two hundred questions, and it had answered every one in slopes and silence.  Tonight she had one question left, and it was not for the crater.  "Can you hold still for a full turn?"  The camera took glass plates, and a plate needed three minutes of light.  Three minutes of moonset and first grey on the rim, on the stool, on the last nightwatch the world would ever keep.  Data would survive in the archive; a man on a stool would not.  Any wind would grain the air with dust and blur him into the ground.  So they waited for the dawning hush.  Her father knew it the way sailors know weather: the moment the downslope wind dies and the upslope wind has not yet been born, when the crater holds its breath and the dust settles straight down.  It lasted four minutes, maybe five.  He would have to outlast the sand.  "I've outlasted worse," he said, and set his hands on his knees.  Below, at the rim road, the graders idled with their lights off, waiting for her mark.  Agency forms had to be signed in slow gall ink, pale at the nib, blackening over one turn of a glass as the iron in it took the air.  A signature could not be backdated; the chemistry testified to the hour.  Ilsa had learned to write her closures so they bloomed black exactly when the machines were allowed to move.  She had the form in her pocket, unsigned.  The moon touched the western rim and went out like a coin dropped in water.  The wind thinned, faltered, and stopped.  In the hush she could hear the sand.  She turned the glass and opened the shutter.  Her father did not move.  His breath came shallow and fast, the paper lungs bargaining for three minutes.  Grey light rose in the east behind him, and the crater became a face she had known all her life.  The sand ran.  At the end the last grain hung in the neck, as it had hung for forty years.  Ilsa tapped the glass once with her fingernail.  The grain let go and fell, and she closed the shutter.  "Watch is over," she told him.  He laughed, which cost him, and then he breathed.  On the plate, invisible until she developed it, he would be sitting on the rim forever with the whole bowl of the crater behind him, the last man ever paid to watch a night go by.  She signed the form in pale ink and pinned it to the survey stake where the graders would read it.  Under her name she wrote a line the agency had not asked for: Nightwatch ended here, all quiet, he outlasted the sand.  The words were nearly invisible now.  They would blacken in three minutes, in the sun, while she was walking him down the road and the engines were starting behind her.  A message timed to arrive too late to save anything, and exactly in time to be read.  The hush broke.  Warm wind came up the slope and lifted the dust, and the first light lay along the rim like something spilled that nobody would ever wipe away.  In her pocket the hourglass lay on its side, empty above, one grain at the bottom, keeping no more time.
