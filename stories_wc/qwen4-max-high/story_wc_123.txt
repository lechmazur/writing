The man in grey had followed her from the registry, so she did not take the stair.  Instead she went sideways, along the old irrigation ledge, the way the water went, terrace to terrace, until the town's noise thinned behind her.  Progressing laterally was slower and safer than climbing; the checkpoints sat on the roads, and the roads all went up. 

Her courtyard held its usual dusk: swifts stitching the air above the cistern, the drip of the tap, and, from the valley, the quarantine loudspeaker reading the evening rules in a voice like a lullaby.  Calm here was not one thing but layers, and she had learned to hear through all of them.  Ilse Varga's workroom held thirty-one years of storms: wind roses, pressure curves, gust times logged to the minute, in ink she mixed herself so it would not fade.  Nobody audited her anymore, which was precisely why the numbers had to be right.  Integrity, she thought, was a crop you grew in solitude, and it needed tending every evening. 

The zone below had been lawful in March.  By June the district office had closed, the blight had passed, and the committee kept the wire up anyway, because wire made them the ones who handed out the flour.  A quarantine with no authority behind it is only a habit with guns.  Their justification was the wind: spores, they said, had blown up from the sealed hamlet of Brede, and Brede must stay sealed.  Her ledgers said the wind had come down from the committee's own granary for nineteen days running.  She was curious about one missing thing, and her curiosity was the credible kind, built from instruments rather than appetite: the date the first rows in Brede had yellowed.  Without that date she had a pattern; with it she had proof. 

The pigeon came in over the wall as the light went copper.  It was Tomas's bird, the grey hen he had trained before the wire, and birds do not read checkpoints.  She knelt, held out her wrist, and felt the small hot weight of it settle.  She noted the hour out of habit, nineteen forty-one, because a storm recorder notes the hour of everything, even grace.  The message tube on its leg was aluminium, scratched, sealed with wax she recognised as candle stub.  Inside: April second, first yellow rows, west field; they read your logs to us on the square, please let them be true. 

April second was eleven days after her wind arrows turned.  She sat back on her heels and let the layers of quiet come back: swifts, drip, loudspeaker, and under them her own breathing. 

Then the gate latch lifted.  The man in grey stood there, out of breath from the stair, holding his hat like a man entering a church.  She had already intuited most of him on the ledge: shoes worn along the outer edge, a terrace walker; eyes on her satchel, not her face; and he had stopped when she stopped, not to close the gap but to keep from being seen by the checkpoint above.  A frightened man, then, sent by frightened men. 

"They want the book," he said. 

"They want nineteen days changed," she said. 

He looked at the cistern and said nothing, which was its own answer. 

"I keep the record alone," she told him, "so nobody can lean on it, and that includes you and it includes me."  "But a true thing can be copied." 

She lit the lamp, and while the swifts went quiet and the loudspeaker began its second reading, she drew the nineteen arrows again, exactly, and the pressure curve, and the hour of every gust.  She wrote April second beside them, and Tomas's name, so the source could be checked.  The copy went into the message tube; the tube went onto the grey hen's leg; the hen went over the wall and turned toward Brede, where somebody would read it aloud in a square. 

He watched it go and did not try to stop it, and she understood that the worn shoes had walked out of Brede in March and never walked back. 

"Go home the way I came," she said, "along the water, terrace to terrace; nothing on that ledge faces you." 

He put on his hat, and went.  She sat in the courtyard until full dark, tending nothing but the truth, which grows best when no one is watching.
