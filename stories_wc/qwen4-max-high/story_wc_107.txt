The ice went gray in the third week of April, and Marta heard it before she saw it, a long sigh under the floor of the abandoned control room.  She had lived up here eleven years, since her father's heart stopped at this console.  The dam ran itself now from a town far away, and the control room was hers, and she had made it a furnace of solitude.  Now the melt was coming, and with it the village.  Every spring the reservoir drew down; this spring it would fall far enough to show the drowned church, the orchard stumps, the streets her father had flooded when he shut the gates.  People would want to ask why.  They had never dared ask her, because she was the operator's daughter and because she answered the door with a blowpipe in her hand.  So she had begun blowing question lamps.  Thin globes, wide as a head, with a small throat for a slip of paper: hang one in a room and light it, and under that light anyone may ask anything, and no one may answer with anger.  That was the whole rule, and she had written it in charcoal on the schoolhouse wall.  She thought best with the pipe turning in her hands, a slow quarter turn, a slow question.  She swept the catwalk and announced it to the dead dials: "Behold, the clearing of the sacred ash, that the vessel may be worthy!"  Her father had narrated his shifts that way too, solemn as a bishop, while buttering bread.  It was how they both carried heavy things: out loud, and slightly absurdly, so the weight could be set down for a breath.  On the console lay his logbook, and in the logbook a leather bookmark, cracked clean across the middle where she had folded it too many times.  It held the page for the night of the flood, the last night he ever wrote.  She had read those six lines perhaps four thousand times and had never let another soul read them once.  The lines said he telephoned the district office twice and was told to wait, waited forty minutes with the lake rising, then shut the gates alone and saved eleven thousand people downstream.  The last line said he had not sounded the second siren, and that three had stayed in the orchard, and that their names were Kern, Kern, and Aldt.  She had kept the bookmark there like a finger pressed to a wound.  On the morning the ice let go, a girl climbed the access road carrying the first lamp Marta had blown for the schoolhouse, now cracked.  "My grandmother is Aldt's sister," she said.  "I want to hang this in the hall on Sunday, and I want you in the room when I ask."  Marta said the crack could be mended, that a hot bit of glass and a steady hand would close it, and she narrated the mending at the top of her lungs, the gathering, the turning, the marriage of edges.  The girl waited until the noise ran out.  "You made a rule for everybody except yourself," she said.  Something in Marta went soft at the center, the way the lake had gone soft, gray and moving under its own crust.  She had thought a safe room was a room with no one else in it.  She let the furnace go out.  She abandoned the control room that afternoon, which she had sworn at his grave never to do.  She carried down the logbook, the cracked bookmark still holding the page, and eleven lamps packed in straw, and she left the dead dials to the swallows and the drip of the thaw.  On Sunday the hall smelled of wet wool and coffee, and the mended lamp burned over a table with the logbook open on it.  Marta took the bookmark out and set it beside the book, a small broken thing offered along with the rest.  Then she read the six lines aloud in her father's ceremonial voice, the voice for opening valves and sweeping floors, and did not make them smaller than they were.  "Did he forget the siren, or choose not to sound it?" the girl asked.  "I do not know," Marta said, and the not knowing sat down among them and was allowed to stay.  Glass, she told them afterward, is sand that agreed to be honest; it cracks where it is held too tight.  The bookmark stayed on the table, holding no page at all, and no one thought to mend it.  Outside, the ice went on rotting beautifully, all night, a sound like a crowd quietly agreeing.
