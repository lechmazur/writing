The fog came into Sennet every evening like a held breath, and the library at the top of Wharf Street was painted the cobalt of a mussel shell.  Vera Solt ran the after-school chess club in its basement room: four boards, eleven children, no funding, and she had never once missed a Thursday.  She taught take-backs first.  Before openings, before clocks, she taught them to lift a hand and say, wait, I want to see it again.  Vera had a knack she never advertised: she heard the words people did not say.  In the checkout line, under the mayor's hello, she heard the sentence he kept folded in his coat: the grotto is already sold.  The grotto was a sea cave under the east headland, its walls veined with magnetite, and on clear nights starlight came straight in off the water and the ceiling glittered like wet salt.  She had taken the children there in October with a bag of compasses.  Every needle swung away from north and pointed into the rock, and the children laughed the way you laugh in church.  June Aldabra pried a lump loose, and Vera carried it up the cliff path cradled in both palms, like a bird found stunned.  The quarry company wanted that headland for a deep-water berth, and the council would sign during the centennial Hush.  Sennet kept one strange custom: at midnight of the hundredth year the bell stopped for a single minute, the minute between the founders' charter and the sealed letter they had left for the next century, the hush where past meets prophecy.  Business done in that minute was said to be done by the whole town, since no one could speak against it.  Vera could not outshout a quarry.  She had eleven children and a jar of iron filings from the hardware store.  So she planned a humble rebellion, the only kind a chess coach could afford: not to win the argument, but to plant forests of second thoughts.  In the basement she set the stone under a pane of glass and shook filings over it, and the filings stood up.  Black needles bristling in ranks, a forest an inch tall, every tree leaning toward the same buried heart.  June said, quietly, that it looked like the woods behind her house before the surveyors came.  June's father brought up a lobster crate of the stone, and Vera spent three weeks in the fog, cutting glass and dividing filings into envelopes.  Each child asked one adult for one window, and those adults asked their neighbors, the way a quiet thing travels in a small town.  Thirty-one windows: the barber, the bakery, the bait store, the insurance office, from the harbor to the church.  She told nobody to protest, sign nothing, shout nothing; she asked them only to light one lamp and make a forest stand up behind the glass, then let it fall.  The centennial came in mist so thick the streetlamps wore sleeves.  The charter was read on the library steps, the cobalt wall behind the mayor shining like the inside of a wave.  Then the bell stopped.  In the hush, thirty-one panes went black and bristling.  The council walked to the town hall past the bakery, the barber, the bait store, and in every lit window a small dark wood stood up out of nothing, leaned, waited, and lay down again.  Vera stood in the crowd with her hands empty and listened to what nobody said.  She heard the quarry man think about his grandson.  She heard the mayor's folded sentence come unfolded: we could wait a year.  She heard, in eleven chests at once, the small brave click of a hand lifted over a board.  The sealed letter was read; the prophecy proved to be a wish for good water and good neighbors; the lease was not signed.  Nothing was won outright, which is how anything humble is won: the vote was postponed, and a postponement is a seed.  In April the children carried the crate back down the cliff path and returned every stone to the grotto, and the starlight came in off the water and found them at it.  Vera cradled the first lump and set it against the wall where the compasses swung.  June asked whether the town would ever change its mind for good.  Vera said that minds grow back like trees, slowly, in rows you cannot see from inside the fog.  Then she taught them, on the wet stone, with all the needles pointing home, to take a move back.
