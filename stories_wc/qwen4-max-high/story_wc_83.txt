The breakers trip at 03:40, the way they always do, and the observatory's screens die for nine minutes.  Nadia unclips her harness and lets herself drift toward the instrument desk.  Below the floor grating: four kilometers of empty night, then the plateau, then the villages that have no lights.  Above her the balloon shifts in the wind, a sound like someone turning pages. 

With the breakers open the processors sleep, but the raw line still runs, needle to paper, unfiltered.  No smoothing, no confidence masking, no polite truncation of the terms the consortium calls noise.  She has waited all week for this dark. 

On the desk sits a geode the size of a fist, split in half, its cavity crowded with quartz.  Karim bought it at a roadside stall three days before the wedding, because she loved that a plain stone could hide a room.  Both halves were on the gift table when the missile came, after the drone had circled the tents for eleven hours.  One half survived; she does not know how, and has stopped asking.  For two years the reports said accident, and she had repeated the word until it fit her mouth like a stone.  Eleven hours of surplus power, she thinks, to watch a marriage, and no cable ever reached the well.  That arithmetic is why she took the contract up here. 

The consortium hunts pools of unused potential, places where the field stacks up and nothing draws it down, and sells the maps to whoever can tap them.  She hunts one pool in particular. 

The smooth model is solvable, and the pipeline solves it beautifully, and the answer is always almost right.  Perturbation theory is her crowbar: start from the clean solution, add a small disturbance, and let the corrections tell you what the clean solution hid.  First order gives the weather of the field, the broad honest slopes.  Second order gives the pooling, the small stubborn surplus that the pipeline throws away as noise.  She computes the second term by hand, in pencil, on the back of a maintenance log. 

Years ago she wired a quartz shard from the geode into a cheap oscillator, a probe the consortium never approved.  It sings when the potential thickens, a note she feels in her teeth before she hears it.  The pool has no mass and no surface, and still it leans on her, intangibly tangible, the way grief leans. 

Four minutes left.  The pencil moves, the needle climbs, and the correction resolves into a shape she has been chasing for two years.  A pool, wide and shallow and enormous, hanging over the plateau, over the valley, over the exact grid square where the tents had stood.  The village below never drew a watt of it; the relay mast on the ridge drew everything it wanted.  Enough to lift water for a century, and the sliver that was drawn went to keeping a link open for eleven hours while something above the tents decided. 

This is the awakening she was never warned about, the filter coming off the world: not a malfunction, an allocation.  Somewhere a man had priced the surplus and sold it, and the buyer had spent it on her wedding.  She laughs, one flat sound in the cold gondola, and her hands keep working. 

Two minutes.  She copies the coordinates onto the geode's specimen tag, then onto her wrist, then into memory, three redundancies against the coming filters.  In the official map this square is blank, marked reserved, which is how the company writes a name it will not say.  She will send the numbers down the mountain to the well committee, with the second-order terms attached, so they can prove the pool exists and ask who signed it away.  It is not justice; it is arithmetic, and arithmetic can be checked by strangers. 

The breakers snap closed.  Light, fans, the pipeline waking, the filters sliding back over the world like a sheet over a face.  On the screen the valley smooths into nothing much, a broad honest slope, noise removed.  The oscillator goes quiet, but her teeth still hold the note.  Nadia closes her fist around the geode half, crystals against palm, a room inside a stone, and waits for the next dark.
