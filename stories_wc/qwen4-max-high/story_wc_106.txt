Emerald Station had ninety-one portraits and only nine minutes of usable light.  For those nine minutes the sun came through the west window like a struck match, and every painted face remembered the afternoon it was made.  That was when Ilse worked.  The papers called her the golden-hour thief, which was wrong in a way she never bothered to correct: she did not take canvases.  She took the illuminated moment inside them, the way you lift a moth off a window without touching the glass.  The frames stayed hung, the paint stayed dull, and the stationmaster kept complaining that the cleaners were ruining his founders.  Stolen light will not keep in daylight; it thins, it evaporates, it goes back to being an afternoon nobody had.  So she carried a jar of captured moonbeams, stoppered with wax, cloudy as a pearl.  She had found it on a bench at eleven years old, the night her brother Teo took the 9:14, and had always believed the moon filled it because she asked politely.  The train left, and it never arrived anywhere.  Twenty years of stolen afternoons later, she had a cellar full of other people's light and not one minute of him.  She had never wanted money; she wanted to press her ear against destiny's door and hear who was breathing on the other side.  She was not a mystic but quietly savvy, a woman who tested things and wrote down what worked.  Moonlight keeps a moment the way salt keeps meat; that was in her notebook, underlined twice.  Also in the notebook: the flower stall on platform three was not selling flowers.  Old Vellie arranged them in the porters' code, the one signalmen used when the wires froze: stems for numbers, blooms for verbs.  For twenty years every arrangement had said the same thing, and Ilse had read it the way the whole station read it: white chrysanthemum, mourning, let the boy go.  Paint keeps more than the sitter; it keeps whatever stood in front of it, waiting.  Tonight she wanted the founding portrait on platform three, because in its background, small as a fingernail, a boy stood holding something round.  The sun arrived at 7:51 and would be gone by eight.  Ilse laid her palm flat on the canvas and felt the moment come loose, warm as bread, and folded it into the dark of her coat.  Then the gold went out of the window like a held breath let go, and she waited in the cold for the moon.  By moonlight Emerald Station becomes what its name promises: green tile, green glass, every bench and rail drowned in emerald, the whole place lit like the inside of a bottle.  She sat under the departures board, unstoppered the jar, and let the stolen afternoon out into all that green.  It rose and opened like a sheet of water standing up.  There was platform three, and there was Teo, nineteen and impatient, and he was not boarding anything.  He was on the bench with a jar held up to the moon, talking into it.  "Keep this for Ilse," he said.  "Tell her the lamp is lit and I am listening."  Then he set the jar on the bench, went to the lost-property door, and pressed his ear against the green paint, and the moment ran out.  The jar in her hands was the jar on the bench.  Quietly, without moving, she began to reinterpret twenty years of white chrysanthemums.  In the porters' code chrysanthemum was never mourning; it was the lamp kept lit in a signal box, a train held on the line and not lost.  Eleven stems had never been a date; they were a door number.  Vellie, widow of the signalman who held the 9:14 on the line, had never once told her to let go.  She had been telling her where to listen.  Door eleven was lost property, and it was painted green, and Ilse crossed the emerald platform and put her ear against it.  Inside, faint and unhurried, a train was still coming.  She believed it the way you believe snow landing on your hand: entirely, and with no idea what to do next.  At dawn she bought eleven white chrysanthemums from Vellie and arranged them herself on the bench: lamp lit, still here, listening.  She never stole another moment; she had one now that had been left for her, and she meant to be there when the door opened.
