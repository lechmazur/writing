The spring went bitter in the ninth drought year, and the beekeepers of Ras Ayn left in one night, abandoning forty hives under the palms.  Mira stayed, because someone had to keep the covenant, and because she had nowhere left that wanted her.  Traders passed along the ridge twice a year and never came down to her, and she preferred the arrangement.  The covenant was old and simple: tell the bees everything.  Births, marriages, deaths; speak them at the hive mouth, or the swarm sickens and goes.  Then the ghosts became visible.  They came up out of the cracked flats at dusk, mute as smoke, and stood among the hives with their faces turned to the entrances.  Mira, who had not spoken aloud in three years, learned to read them the way she read a comb: through contextual clues.  A ghost hovering at the wrong hive wanted its own name remembered; a ghost with pollen on its wrists had been a keeper; the tilt of a head told her which palm it had planted.  She was tranquilly perceptive, and the dead preferred her to the loud towns.  Only one ghost she could not read.  Deb, a glazier before she was a beekeeper, had gone to the coast in the fourth year, where the doctors were, and died there.  Mira had never said the sentence that might have kept her: put down the hives and come with me.  Unspoken, it stayed heavy; Mira believed that words, once breathed, weighed nothing, and she meant to carry this one whole to her own death.  When Deb's ghost came back and laid its palm flat on the hive lid, Mira promised her, in the only language left, never to speak it.  A plea would have anchored the dead woman to this sand forever.  But the bees had not been told of her dying, and the last nine hives were failing, and the covenant knotted: she could not keep the dead free and the living fed with one silent throat.  For three seasons she carried the tangled covenant to the hives at dawn and stood there, saying everything without a sound, changing nothing.  Deb's ghost never looked at her.  It stood east of the hive stand at first light and gazed past her shoulder at the shelf in the hut, at the stained glass box from Deb's tool chest.  Mira had assumed it held jewels, or nothing.  Now she fetched it and read it the way she read the dead.  The lead lines were too thick for the pattern; the panes were amber, cobalt, smoke, the colors of the church window Deb had salvaged; the box weighed more than glass should.  Deb had always signed her windows in lead, hiding her name in the cames where no one would look.  Mira set the box on the hive stand and turned it to the rising sun.  Light came through and laid the lead's shadow on the white hive front, and the shadow was letters: Deb's name, the year, the word dead, and on the fourth panel Mira's own sentence, the one she had never spent.  Mira, who had not been astonished since the water turned, was astonished.  Deb had told the bees her own death ahead of it, and had known Mira's words without being told them, and had built them a body that needed no breath.  The bees came out and walked over the colored letters, reading with their feet, and the hive hummed the way a hive hums when it has been told.  She carried the box from hive to hive as the sun climbed, nine times, telling the death in light.  The ghosts turned to watch, and one by one they thinned, satisfied, the way smoke goes when the wind forgets it.  Deb's ghost gazed at the lit hive, then at Mira, and the look was the whole apology, and Mira answered by holding the box steady so the light would not shake.  Then she went east into the flats, unbound, and the sentence stayed where it was, in colored light on wood, unsaid and undiminished.  By noon the bees were working the one living flower at the bitter spring, and Mira sat in the palm shade with the box in her lap, keeping the silence that had finally learned to speak.
