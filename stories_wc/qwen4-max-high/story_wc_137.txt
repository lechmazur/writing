The tower had no roof, and Teodor Beck had never tried to give it one.  Mira climbed the ninety steps with a hydrophone case in one hand and her survey contract in the other.  Below the cliff the grey whales ran south, four hundred miles of them, singing at a pitch too low for human ears.  She had come to record a symphony nobody in the county would ever hear.  Beck was waiting at the top with a ceramic tile in his lap.  Sixteen numbers, glazed blue, every row and column and diagonal adding to 34.  "A magic square," he said.  "Ana pressed it before the kiln, and I pressed it after, and now what I remember she remembers."  Mira did not believe in entangled memories.  She had watched her mother's mind come apart like wet paper, and she had learned that memory was a witness who lied under oath.  But she sat down on the cold floor beside him, because he had asked her to, and because his hands shook.  "Ana was my wife," he said, cheerful as a man reporting weather.  "Dead twenty years, and the tile still keeps her side of things."  The stroke had taken his nouns first, then his years.  He could not say what happened in 1974, but if she said "eleven," he could tell her about the moth.  Ana had indexed her star logs to the square, an old memory art out of a book on medieval rhetoric.  Any three cells told you the fourth.  Mira opened her notebook and began to transcribe.  She worked through the forgotten pattern, cell by cell, and the old man's life came up out of the numbers like water out of a pump.  Cell twelve: the night the dome jammed open in a gale, and Ana on the stairs with a lamp.  He told it from the stairs.  He told the lamp's weight in her wrist, the moth burning on the glass, the thought that he would stand out there until he drowned in the sky.  He had never fixed it.  Mira wrote it in a second column, beside his other version, and said nothing about which one was true.  Later she found Ana's log for that night: the moth, the wrist, the same drowning sentence.  He had read those logs a thousand times; she kept that explanation the way you keep a coat you hope not to need.  Her contract was three weeks, the length of the migration.  She spent the first week lowering the hydrophone, and the second week noticing that the tower's bare plaster threw the sea's low voice around the room until it cancelled itself out.  A room could be shaped like an ear.  So she asked what he wanted built, and he said, a place where a person can sit and be thought at.  She understood it as an acoustics problem, which was the only way she could afford to understand it.  In the third week she sculpted the alcove: a curved niche of wet plaster, troweled by hand, angled to fold the whales' infrasound onto one stone bench and scatter the road noise into nothing.  She called it a space for reflection, and let the pun stand.  She bolted a transducer under the bench and set the blue tile where the sound would focus.  The last pod passed on the night of the gale.  The cable had come loose on the gallery, ninety feet above the water, and the rail was a rumor of rust.  Mira went out anyway, flat on her belly on the wet stone, one arm over the edge, and seated the connector by feel.  Then she went down and brought Beck up the ninety steps, one at a time, his weight against her shoulder, an hour of it, saying eleven, twelve, thirteen, because numbers were the only stairs he had left.  She put him on the bench with his palm on the tile.  The song came up through the stone into the bones of his hand, too low to hear, loud enough to feel, a symphony played entirely under the floor of the world.  "She's on the stairs," he said.  "She's got the lamp."  Mira knelt and wrote it down, both columns, and did not correct him, and did not believe, and stayed.  Afterward, alone in the niche, she found she was remembering her mother's hands wringing a dishcloth, a thing she had not been able to call up in nine years.  She could not prove where it came from.  She sat in the space she had made and let it be entangled, listening to nothing, hearing all of it.
