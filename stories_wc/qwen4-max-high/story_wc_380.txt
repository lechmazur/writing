After the whale beached, the harbour went quiet in a way that felt polished.  For sixty years the town had fed its unwanted memories to that whale, spooning them into its mouth at the low tide like medicine into a child.  Now it lay on the shingle with nothing left to swallow, and the memories had nowhere to go but down.  They settled under the town in pockets of gravity.  Oren felt them on his rounds.  He was the dock-side lantern lighter, and he knew the weight of every lamp on the pier, so he noticed when the flame in his lighting taper leaned sideways toward nothing, or when a coil of rope on the boards suddenly weighed like a sleeping dog and then, an hour later, light as a gull's feather.  The pockets fluctuated mysteriously; no one could say where they would surface next.  But the brine mistress could. 

Each morning she poured the dregs of her coffee onto a white plate and let them settle.  The grounds crept toward whatever mass lay beneath the floor, drawing ridges and fans and small dark deltas, and she read the patterns in the coffee grounds the way her mother had read weather.  Oren brought her a cup of his own, black and thick, and she turned it under the window until the sediment drew a long spiral with a hook at its end.  "Bay four," she said.  "Under the salt chamber, tonight, before the tide turns."  "Whatever it is, it has your weight in it," she said, and did not look at him. 

He had paid for the deletion himself, eleven years ago, in the shed behind the fish market.  He remembered paying.  He remembered the whale's mouth opening.  What he had given it was gone, and in its place he carried a flat square of wax paper folded in his coat, and inside the paper, pressed wildflowers: yarrow, sea pink, a small blue thing he could not name.  Someone had picked them for him.  Someone had lain them between the pages of a ledger until they were flat as coins, and he had loved that person, and he had fed the knowing of her to a whale.  That was why he wanted to find where deleted memories go. 

The salt crystal growing chamber lay under the old works, cut into the rock where the brine seeped.  He went down with a spade and a lantern and stood in the gleaming silence.  Crystals hung from the strings in the vats, pale cubes catching his light and passing it along, so that the whole room seemed lit from inside itself and made no sound at all.  Salt grew around whatever the pockets pushed up.  Since the beaching the vats had grown faster than they had in sixty years, and the works had been shut for a week.  A wedding ring hung in one vat, a child's shoe in another.  Tonight the floor of bay four rose and fell under his boots like breathing.  The spade struck something at three feet, and the weight let go all at once, so that he staggered upward with the tool in his hands.  He knelt and began to exhume it. 

It was a crystal the size of a loaf, and inside it, unpressed and unfaded, floated a handful of the same small blue flowers.  He broke it against the vat.  The brine ran out over his wrists, cold as the harbour in March, and with it came the name Ilsa, and the weight of her asleep on his shoulder, and the morning the tide brought her back to the same pier where he lit the lamps every dusk.  She had picked them on the cliff path in June.  She had been seven.  He had fed it all to the whale so that he could climb the ladder each night with steady hands. 

He sat in the gleaming silence until the tide turned, holding the flowers, letting the grief have its full weight back.  Then he climbed to the pier and lit the lanterns, all forty of them, and the flame of his taper stood perfectly straight.  Under the boards the pocket had emptied, and the coil of rope weighed like rope again.  In the morning the brine mistress would find her coffee grounds lying flat and meaningless on the plate, and would know that someone had gone down and brought his sorrow back up.  Oren said her name to every lamp, and the harbour let him.
