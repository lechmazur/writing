Fog took the canal town before dawn, as it did nine mornings in ten, which is why the town told time by water and not by sun.  Ilse walked to the water clock repair shop inside that hush, hearing a hundred sealed bowls drip behind the walls.  In her fist, wrapped in linen, was a screw.  It had come out of her mother's birthing clamp, and a smith had drilled it through the shaft the winter the labor clock lost its jet.  It had pinched a hundred cords first, and afterward it had metered the water that measured a hundred labors.  Now it was choked with lime, and up the hill her mother's apprentice sat with a woman whose pains had gone quiet and too far apart. 

Anselm turned the screw under his loupe.  In the street they called him the Regulator; he spoke in tolerances and kept his repair tags numbered.  "Hand-cut thread," he said.  "Nothing in this shop matches it."  "Ream the bore wider and your clock will run fast and lie to you."  "Then clear it as it is," Ilse said. 

He set it in vinegar to soak, and wiped a clean place on the bench, and wiped it again.  Ilse read him the way she read everyone now, between the lines.  A fever three winters ago had burned the smell out of her, and the shelves of her aromatherapy shop had gone mute: bergamot, vetiver, the cold blue chamomile she remembered like a chord.  She still practiced, blending by recipe and by faith, and she mapped the emotional terrain of a room by other means: the shoulder held high, the breath caught at the top, the pause before the word fine.  Anselm's wife had died in April; his ledger recorded it only as gaps, days the shop stayed shut. 

Ilse put a small bottle beside his rag.  "I made this in May," she said.  "Cedar, smoke, orange peel."  "Things that stay."  "I can't tell if it's right."  He unstoppered it, and his face did something complicated, and then settled.  "Warm," he said.  "Like a coat left by a stove."  The tremor went out of his hands. 

He lifted the screw from the vinegar and worked a horsehair through the bore, turning it, listening.  You could not see inside these clocks; the floats rode in sealed bronze, the scale was cut where no eye could reach, and you read the level by the drip and the ring of the water.  He threaded the screw into a test bowl, and they waited, and the fog pressed the window flat and grey.  A drop fell.  Then another, and another, spaced like a resting pulse.  Ilse laid two fingers on her throat and counted the drops against her blood, and the intervals held. 

"It's true," Anselm said, and wrote forty-four on a tag.  "Write her name," Ilse said.  "Marta."  "Not a number."  "She's up there with a girl who has never caught a baby."  He looked at the tag a long moment, then crossed the number out and wrote Marta, and beneath it, smaller, his wife's name, as if a ledger could be taught. 

"I used to sell rare things," Ilse said.  "Resins that cost more than bread."  "Now I want people to see the plain miracle: a hole the width of a hair, water, a woman breathing, a child arriving on time."  "I can't smell a thing anymore, and still the world keeps doing this." 

He wrapped the screw in its linen and laid it in her palm, and his hand shook once, and steadied.  Outside, the fog had not lifted.  The hill was a rumour, and the lamp in Marta's window was only a smear of gold, a thumbprint on glass.  She could not see the door she was walking toward.  She climbed anyway, past the dripping walls, counting the spaces between the drops.  Somewhere above her a woman breathed, and a girl held a bowl she could not see into, and waited for the small honest sound that would tell her whether there was still time.
