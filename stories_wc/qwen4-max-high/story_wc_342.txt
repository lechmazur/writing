The whistle had gone, and the tree stood in the hush that comes just before a condemned thing is pulled down.  Nine centuries ago the scholars of the Guild of Grinders had hollowed this oak into a library, shelving its rings, and now the rings had rotted through and the fellers had chalked their numbers on the bark.  Inside, on the plank scaffold, Vera held her lamp low against the founding coin.  It was centuries old and worn blank, but under raking light the faint indentations of an older strike rose like breath on glass: a shelf mark, and under it the word psalter.  The guild had given it to her when they made her their last limner, and it had never bought anything.  Dust hung in the lamplight without moving.  Every book had been carted out to the street except the one no catalogue listed. 

She had painted faces for forty years and had never once known one.  Sitters returned to her studio and arrived as strangers; she knew her husband by his cough and her apprentice by the way he set his left foot.  So she had asked the guild for the hollow trunk and spent eleven years crafting a mirror of the sky on its inner walls, one thing in the world she could recognize indoors.  The zenith above the reading desk was still bare wood, and bare wood needed the guild's blue. 

The psalter stood where the coin said, fat and false, its pages a hollow box.  Inside lay a lead pot and a ledger.  The blue in the pot had set to stone three hundred years ago; her thumbnail left no mark on it.  The ledger was a record of grindings.  Each generation had halved the grain of the last, and each page repeated the same diagram smaller, fractally precise, a coastline drawn inside a coastline.  Nine hundred years of halving, and then a final hand, shaking: here the light is coarser than the powder, and we can go no further. 

Vera sat down on the boards.  She had done that to faces: split each cheek into planes, each plane into pores, each pore into its own small weather, certain that if she went fine enough, recognition would be waiting at the bottom.  It was not there.  The guild had reached the floor of the world and written it down politely, and she had reached hers. 

Outside, the hush held.  Even the birds had gone to the far hedgerows, and the ropes hung slack, and the whole street waited with its hands off the machines.  Her apprentice was keeping it for her.  He had promised to fill the fellers' listening quarter hour, the silence they leave for a great trunk to settle, with his coughing, so she could find him afterward among strangers. 

She could not finish the sky.  No one could; blue ends, and paint is a wall, and a wall cannot be weather.  Above the bare zenith sat the bung the guild had driven into the crown to keep rain off the readers, shrunk now to a loose gray tooth.  Vera climbed and set her palette knife into the seam and worked it the way she worked a jawline, patiently, by fractions.  The bung dropped into the sawdust without a sound worth naming.  Morning came through the hole and lay down on her painted morning, and the two were not the same, and the true one made the painted one beautiful anyway.  The zenith was finished, and she had not finished it. 

She laid the coin in the hollow book and closed the false pages over it, giving the key back to its lock.  Then she went down the ladder into the coughing.  Her apprentice took her elbow, and she knew him by the grip, which was the only way she had ever known anybody, and it was enough.  They walked out past the cordon. 

Behind them the hush broke, the winches took up, and the oak went down with the sound of a long breath let out.  For the instant before the walls came apart they held the sky exactly as she had meant them to.  Then the walls were gone, and the sky stood open over the street, and Vera, among faces she would never know, recognized it.
