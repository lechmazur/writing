The 6:14 to Kestrel Slip carried forty-one people who all believed they were going home.  I was the deckhand, and I loved them the way you love weather: helplessly, and out loud.  Bo on the rail with his crossword, the twins who traded gloves, Aiyana and her thermos of cardamom.  My daughter Junie waited at the slip every evening with the other waiting people, and I waved before the horn. 

Then the clocks ran sideways.  It happens on certain tides: the hands stop climbing and slide flat, pointing at the horizon instead of the numbers.  The harbor lights went out like a held breath, and the Marigold slid into a grotto that had no business being under the sea.  Starlight came through the rock the way light comes through a curtain.  Pale walls, black water, constellations overhead and underfoot, and our wake stitching them together.  The passengers kept lifting their coffee, kept almost sipping, kept almost arriving. 

The captain showed me the hourglass from the wheelhouse, its bulbs scoured clean.  No sand at all, not a grain.  "It's not empty," he said.  "It's all in the neck."  Then he sat down and became part of the almost. 

I did not sit down.  I have never been good at despair; it slides off me like spray off oilskin.  I got the radio.  Channel sixteen, static like frying fat, and under it a voice reading harbor traffic for a Kestrel Slip that had lost a ferry eleven years ago.  Eleven years, and they still logged us as due. 

That is when I understood there were two crossings, and only one of them had ended.  Somewhere a Junie had stopped waiting.  I wanted the two channels braided into one river: our ferry arriving inside their arrival, the vanished and the delivered made the same boat.  Merged, not chosen. 

So I looked for the pattern, because everything here was rhyming.  The stars on the ceiling matched the harbor's light grid, buoy for constellation.  The ripples off our hull matched the shape of the static on the radio's little green screen.  The coffee rings on the rail matched the tide chart's loops, which matched the hourglass: two chambers, one narrow throat.  Two worlds, and the crossing between them, and the sand was us.  We were the grain stuck in the neck, which is why nothing here ever finished. 

I keyed the mic and told the voice on the other side to find their hourglass and turn it when I said.  The voice said my name the way only Junie says it, and eleven years went through me like a swell under the keel.  She had grown up waiting; she had taken the harbor job to keep waiting professionally.  I laughed, because of course she had, because that is our family's whole method.  "Channel sixteen," I told her.  "Count the ripples with me." 

We matched the star grid to the buoy lights, the static to the swell, the loops to the tide, and I turned the empty glass as she turned hers.  Empty on both ends, so the sand had somewhere to be.  The grotto folded like a paper lantern.  Forty-one coffees finished their sip.  The horn sounded, and I waved before it, the way I always do. 

Here is what merging costs: the seam has to be held.  Something has to stay in the neck forever, falling both ways, or the two shores drift apart again.  The Marigold runs now, always, mid-crossing, never docking, and the passengers step off onto both docks at once and go home to dinners that have been waiting eleven years and eleven minutes.  I stay aboard.  Somebody has to swab the deck, somebody has to keep the coffee hot, and honestly, I have the better end of it. 

Every 6:14 I pass the slip in both worlds, and Junie is there, nine and twenty at once, thermos in hand.  She raises it; I raise my rag; the horn goes, and we are neither late nor early.  Nothing aboard lasts except the leaving, and the leaving is enough.  The hourglass stays empty on the shelf, and I dust it every watch, and it never needs turning again.  We are the sand.  We are still falling, and falling, it turns out, is a way of being home.
