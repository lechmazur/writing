The sea left Saltwharf twelve years ago, and Maren kept weaving anyway.  Nets for boats that would never come back, sails folded in a loft above the stranded pier, verses knotted into every mesh because her hands had always thought in rhyme.  Only the tide pools remained, three green basins at the canyon's foot, and every dusk they drained through a fissure with a sound like a throat clearing.  After the tide pools empty, the town walks in its sleep.  It began the year the water went, as if the sleepers were still out looking for it.  They climb into the labyrinth the ancestors carved into the red canyon walls, eight centuries of chisel work stacked above the dead harbor.  The law of the maze is precedent: walk only in the grooves your dead have worn, turn where they turned, grieve where they grieved.  Maren's groove belonged to Bel, her wife, the printer, who died the winter the sea withdrew.  Twelve years of walking Bel's rut, ending at the niche where mourners lay a storybook illustration plate, copper, engraved with a woman climbing a stair toward an orchard.  Bel had cut that plate for the town's children, so they would know what leaving looked like.  Maren carried it up every night and every night brought it home, which was itself a broken precedent, the first of many.  Because the sleepers do not know the law.  Asleep, they cross.  They cut between galleries, step out of their mothers' ruts into their neighbors', and the crossings multiply, a lattice of scuffed dust laid over the old obedient stone.  Maren followed them with the plate held flat, its polished back catching moonlight, and in that small copper dawn she read the overlaps.  Where two paths crossed she tied a thread to a chisel-scar; where three crossed she tied two.  By day she sewed.  Sailcloth on her knees in the loft, orchid thread from the pale blooms that grew in the canyon's seeps, every crossing stitched as a knot from her net-making days, the rhyme still moving in her fingers.  She sang while she sewed, the old knot-verses with new endings: the water is gone, the water is gone, and look what the leaving made room for.  The neighbors called her voice hauntingly optimistic, a hope with a ghost in it, and they came to listen at the loft door without admitting why.  The map grew: not a maze but a weave, the sleepers' overlapping paths resolving into one continuous route from the ruined wharf, up through the broken precedents, to a ledge no ancestor had ever named.  Maren had walked it awake, alone, in daylight, which no one had done in eight hundred years.  The ledge held orchids, a whole hanging garden of them, fed by the seep, roots in the chisel scars.  Grief had cut the stone; something else had planted it.  Old Sesk and the elders found her sewing and called it desecration: a route with no dead in it.  Maren inked the plate with orchid dye and pressed it into the cloth's center, and there was Bel's woman climbing her stair, arriving.  "She isn't leaving," Maren said.  "She's getting somewhere."  That night, after the tide pools emptied, she went up with the cloth over her shoulder, and the sleepers rose behind her.  She had sewn thread markers at every crossing, and the sleepers' hands, remembering nets, took hold of them.  Where the paths overlapped, the walking became a single file; where everyone had always turned back, the thread went on.  They woke at dawn on the ledge, all of them, among the orchids, their feet worn white with canyon dust.  Not one of them had walked a precedent, and the ledge was wide enough to hold them all.  Sesk stood with petals in her hair and asked, very quietly, to be taught the crossings.  They hung the sewn map at the labyrinth's mouth, orchid crossings on white sail, and the new rite took: walk where the sleepers crossed, carry nothing, bury nothing.  Maren printed the plate on scraps for the children, so they would know what arriving looked like.  She could not carve stone; she had sewn a path instead, from the ruin of her wharf to a ledge people climbed now just to look, and the looking became reverence.  The tide pools still empty every dusk, and the town still walks in its sleep, but now the walking goes somewhere, and Maren sings the endings while she sews.
