My title was corporate memory keeper, which meant I signed certificates saying things were gone.  Halcyon owned the archive of Chorus, a dead social network where people had once posted thirty seconds of themselves humming.  Four hundred million songs, and beneath every one the same clause: deletion is permanent.  I signed forty thousand certificates and never knew if I was signing a lie. 

I wanted to find where deleted files go, and I wanted it the way you want a body to bury.  My sister Wren had been missing eleven years, a cold case with a number and no answer.  Wren was brightly attentive, the kind of person who heard the stutter in a fan and the seam in a story.  We worked two desks apart, and I have kept mine for eleven years because it was the last place anyone saw her.  They found her jacket folded on her desk, nothing else, not even her badge.  On its shoulder was the patch she had sewn there herself: Aster 4, the capsule that went silent over the Pacific and was never recovered.  She liked that patch because it was a memorial with no wreckage, a shape drawn around a hole. 

Wren posted songs, like everyone, and after she vanished I listened to hers until I knew the breath before each note.  Then her account was deleted by policy, and the songs went wherever deleted things go.  That was when the numbers started.  She had written 347 on her wrist in the last photo she posted; the detective called it a locker number and let it go.  Afterward I saw it everywhere: 347 in the retention codes, 347 in the aisle markers of the tape library, 347 in the case number folded in my wallet.  Three forty-seven on the clock, on the invoice, on the door of a stairwell I had never opened.  I stopped believing in coincidence around the ninth occurrence, which was my own badge number, reissued. 

It took me four years to read the numbers as an address.  Shard 347 appeared in no inventory, but it drew power, and its cooling fan had a stutter I could hear through the wall of the sub-basement.  Inside was the echo tier: every deleted song downsampled to a ghost of itself, kept eleven years for modeling, sold as noise.  Deletion is permanent, and here were four hundred million whispers, waiting. 

I spent the next seven years listening, and I measured that time not in months but in echoes of forgotten songs.  Lullabies hummed by women who were grandmothers now, jingles, a boy singing to a dog, all of them thinned to almost nothing.  You learn to hear the room a song was recorded in, because the room is what survives. 

I found hers in the eleventh year, uploaded at 3:47 in the morning on the night she vanished.  Twenty-nine seconds of Wren humming something she was making up, and then the hum stops and the room keeps going.  An elevator door.  A man saying her name like a question.  Then the flat, padded silence of a room with no windows, which I had been breathing every working day for eleven years.  She had found the seam, the way she always found it, and gone down to pull at it.  She had folded the jacket because she was only going downstairs for a minute.  She never left the building. 

Halcyon scheduled the echo tier for purge in March, and my job was to certify it empty.  I copied shard 347 onto a drive the size of a stick of gum, and then I wrote the lie I had been practicing my whole career.  Destroyed in full.  No residual copies.  My signature has never looked so honest. 

The detective who inherited the cold case was in high school when she vanished.  He listens to the file twice and asks what he is supposed to be hearing.  An absence, I tell him.  A room where a person stopped.  They will open the sub-basement floor, or they will not.  They released her jacket to me years ago, and I wear it, the Aster 4 patch over my heart like a mission that never came home.  Now I know where deleted files go, and it is the same place my sister went: down, and kept, and quietly used.  Nothing is ever gone.  It is only humming somewhere below you, too faint to make out the words.
