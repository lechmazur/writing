The Archive of Extinct Tongues held four hundred dead languages and one that was only dying.  Anneke was its arcane linguist, and she had three hours before the tide turned and the roads diverged.  Sisters Marit and Oda, the last two speakers of Vesh, would take separate ships at dawn and never speak again.  Vesh had no word for a solitary truth.  A claim sung by one voice was only noise; a claim sung by two, answered by a third, became a symphony of truth, and could not lie.  That was the whole architecture of the language, and Anneke loved it with a joy she kept folding smaller.  Eleven years ago she had stood at the inquest into the burning of the Vesh granaries and said nothing.  The magistrate had heard the word hesht and translated it as surrendered.  Hesht also meant entrusted, and Anneke had known it, and had kept her mouth shut because her post depended on the magistrate's favor.  Marit was blamed; Oda believed the blame; the village stopped speaking Vesh aloud, and the silence went on for eleven years.  The testimony survived only as a knotted cord in the cold room, and it had snarled shut the day she said nothing.  She set it on the table between the sisters, alongside her mentor's glass ink well.  Glass, because in Vesh a scribe must be able to show that nothing is hidden in the ink.  There was an inch left in it, black and clear to the bottom.  "I cannot untie this by pulling," Anneke said.  "A knot like this only opens from the inside, via interconnected promises."  Oda's mouth thinned.  "You want us to trust each other first."  "No," Anneke said.  "I want you to trust the shape of the promises, and let trust come afterward, slowly, the way it does."  She dipped the pen and wrote the first clause on the ledger, her hand steady, her pulse absurdly fast.  Marit would promise to name what she carried out of the granary, but only if Oda promised to say what she had seen from the hill.  Oda's promise would hold only if Marit's held; each clause leaned on the other, so neither woman could be the only one exposed.  That was the careful part, and Anneke checked the intervals twice, the way she checked a declension.  Then came the reckless part.  A symphony needed a third voice, and the third voice had to be the one that had failed.  She wrote herself into the lattice: she would speak hesht aloud, in both its meanings, before the tide turned.  If she went silent again, the chord would close on her throat and take her voice for good.  She had priced it in the cold room, alone, and found she could pay.  Marit read the clause, then looked at Anneke for a long moment.  "You were the translator," she said.  "I was the silence," Anneke said.  The ink went down another quarter inch as the sisters signed, and the cord on the table shifted, loosening one loop.  Marit sang first: a low, cracked note, the word for what she had carried, which was the ledger of grain, not the grain.  Oda answered, and her note bent toward her sister's, and the interval settled.  Anneke let out a breath she had been holding since the inquest, and then she sang hesht, surrendered, entrusted, both meanings braided in one held tone.  The chord took, and did not close on her throat.  The cord unsnarled itself across the table like something waking, and every knot in eleven years of testimony lay open to be read.  Oda was crying, furiously, and Marit was not touching her yet, but she had turned toward her, which was the whole distance the morning could ask.  The ink well stood empty, and the lamp shone straight through it onto the open knots.  "The ships still leave," Oda said.  "They do," Anneke said, "but the promises are written to reach across water, and you each hold half a symphony now."  She wanted to laugh out loud, and instead she capped the empty well and wrote the hour, because a truth needs a witness.  Outside, the tide began to turn, and two women kept talking in a language the archive would have to relabel: living.
