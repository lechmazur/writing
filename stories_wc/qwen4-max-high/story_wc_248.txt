The tapestry fragment came to my salt hut with no letter, only a thumbprint of red wax.  Nine years off the grid, and someone had still found the one road the maps forgot.  The weave was a thousand years old, salt-stiff, faded to the color of weak tea, and I read it in four seconds: a floor plan, stacks in rows of nine, one thread of madder red marking a vault.  I have always been this way; patterns arrive whole, the way heat arrives before light.  My name is Adra, and for six years I painted the faces on borrowers' cards, because the Republic required a likeness beside every name.  I was good at it; I loved the people I portrayed, the crooked eyetooth, the fear under the chin.  Then the vans came for the names, and I understood what my tenderness had built.  So I burned nine lending libraries, ledgers first and shelves after, and I did not weep where anyone could see.  The plan on the fragment belonged to Ashem, the drowned republic under the salt, whose archive the diggers opened last spring.  Our Republic hides its recall ledgers down there, among the dead people's clay, where every stolen thing can be catalogued as ancient.  I went down with the water truck at dawn.  The lift dropped through strata of ash and salt, and the air came up warm, as if the ground still remembered its volcanoes.  In the first corridor, a charcoal hand on the plaster, fingers spread: wait.  In a niche, a clay bird with its beak shut: say nothing.  Then a poem scratched in salt crust, then a fresco patch of a woman carrying fire in her apron, each new-made and dressed in dust, each pointing deeper.  The overseers read every word that enters the dig; they do not read pictures.  Someone was speaking to me in the only language the censors call decoration.  The fragment in my pocket was the first piece of the trail; the map had been the invitation.  Vault nine held a small painting under glass, tagged as ancient, and it was my own face.  I had painted myself at twenty-six for my borrower's card, the one likeness I never burned.  Now the face was gouged out, scraped down to the ground, vandalized past any matching.  I stood with my hands shaking, angry at the ruin and grateful to it, which is the only way I have ever known how to love.  Then the gouges resolved, the way patterns always do for me, and I saw that the strokes were letters.  "YOU PROMISED TO TAKE MY FACE OUT OF THE WORLD."  "IT IS IN VAULT ELEVEN, AND I HAVE TAKEN YOURS."  Beneath the words, a name: Nel.  Nel, eleven years old, sitting for her card at the Marsh Branch, asking would they find her.  I had said, no, child, I will take your face out of the world, and then I burned that branch and forgot which cards had already gone down to the deep registry.  Nine years of salt silence had worn the promise smooth, the way a stone forgets it was ever a knife.  She had not forgotten; she had grown up, learned conservation, and taken work among the dead to find one small living face.  Vault eleven: her card, my brushwork, the child's chin trembling exactly as I had caught it.  She could not touch it herself; the overseers log every conservator's hand.  I could have burned the archive; I still carry that habit like a coal in the chest.  But Ashem had already lost its city, and I would not take its memory too.  So I did what I was trained to do, which is to portray.  I opened my old kit, and over that child's face I painted a woman of Ashem, third dynasty, in madder red and salt white, with the tapestry's weave behind her.  I gave her Nel's eyes and a dead queen's mouth, and I labelled her Anonymous, weaver, undated.  No matcher will ever find a living girl inside a thousand-year-old artifact; the catalogue itself is the fire now.  Nel was waiting at the shaft head with dust in her eyelashes, and she did not thank me.  She put her forehead against mine, hard, the way people do when they have been angry for years and are finally allowed to stop.  I kept the tapestry fragment; she keeps the gouged self-portrait, my face scraped into a promise.  Under the salt a whole people waits to be read, and above it two women go on erasing each other gently, which is how we keep our word.
