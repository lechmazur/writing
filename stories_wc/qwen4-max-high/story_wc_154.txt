The mist came down the mountain at dusk and filled the hospital atrium until the ferns swam.  Sera translated for the mission hospital, four languages across the island's war, and she had no word for that hour.  On the ward below, Joses lay with his fist shut around a fragment of brain coral.  Nine years old, his arm broken in November when the copra shed burned, set crooked by a medic with no X-ray.  Tonight the surgeon would break it again to set it right.  The nurses could not open the boy's hand.  "It's my grandfather's," he told Sera.  "He tied pieces like this onto the dead reef where the dynamite boats went, and they took."  "If I let go it will forget how to grow."  Sera promised to hold it, and to keep the old plaster when they sawed it off. 

The cast was crowded with signatures.  Joses had asked every man who carried him down the coast to sign it, the way footballers sign shirts, and every man had laughed and done it.  He told them it was for luck.  Sera saw the shrewdly innocent arithmetic: a boy making witnesses of three armies, so that if he vanished, someone would have to admit to having held him.  His aunt had scrubbed at one line with her thumb until it blurred.  "That one is a curse," she said.  "The other side's language. I will not have it on him." 

In theatre the mask went down, and the boy's eyes followed Sera until they stopped following.  After the anesthetic kicked in, Sera unknotted his fingers one by one and lifted the coral free.  It was grey-white, grooved in wandering lanes, a stone brain that had thought one slow thought for a hundred years.  The plaster came off in two warm halves. 

She carried them into the atrium, where the mist had pooled and the lamps made small brown halos.  Seawater and blood had smeared the ink, so she read the way she read everything now: sideways, by pressure, by the ghost a pen leaves in plaster.  Nine men, three uniforms, one coast road.  She decrypted them slowly, sounding out the mountain spelling, the police Pijin, the letters written by a hand shaking over a boat's engine.  Elias: "Pass him gentle, he is heavy with sleep."  Tavio: "Do not let him die on me, I have three of my own."  A name she could not resolve: "Tell them I only carried him."  Enemies, all of them, writing the same fear in different hands.  And the blurred line, the curse, in the language across the strait: "He is not the enemy. Carry him easy."  The same sentence, twice more, in the same language, from men the aunt would have called killers. 

Sera sat with it while the mist wet her hair.  Two years ago she had translated a man's answer at a checkpoint, word for word, exactly, and the boys had shot him anyway, because exact is not the same as understood.  Since then she had wanted words to do what the old man did to the reef: take a living piece and bind it to the rubble until the rubble grew.  Below her, a surgeon was reopening a healed wound to make it heal honestly. 

When they wheeled him out, the fresh plaster was still soft, and she asked the nurse for a marker.  She wrote the nine names in a ring around the boy's forearm, and under them, in both languages, the sentence they had all been saying.  His aunt read the enemy's hand three times, and then sat down on the wet tiles and put her palm flat over the words, as if over a mouth, or a wound.  "They were afraid too," she said, not forgiving anybody, not yet.  "Yes," Sera said. "That is the whole message." 

When he surfaced, groggy and cheated, looking for what had been taken from him, Sera folded his fingers back around the coral.  "It didn't forget," she told him.  "It grew while you were asleep."  Outside, the mist went on filling the atrium, and the ferns went on swimming, and under the white shell of his arm the broken bone began to knit again, straighter this time.  Out past the strait, in water nobody had bombed for a year, the old man's fragments were holding to the rubble and growing over the scar.
