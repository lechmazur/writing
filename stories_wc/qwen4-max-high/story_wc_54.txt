The carnival left on Sunday, and by Tuesday the town had stopped pretending it was still Saturday.  Nadia Okonkwo arrived Wednesday, twenty-two, an intern in civil engineering, carrying a level, a notebook, and instructions to certify the passenger terminal for demolition.  No ship had lifted from the apron in thirty years.  The carnival had rented the concourse for two weeks, and its generators had left oil moons on the terrazzo.  Outside, the tent peg holes were still weeping water, though it had not rained for nine days.  Nadia knelt and looked: a line of small dark mouths running straight across the field toward the terminal's south wall.  Water does not queue for tickets; it goes where it is invited. 

Inside, the terminal was a maze of intentions.  She read them like strata: a customs hall from the tariff war, a security corridor bolted on after the hijacking, a nursery of gates for a fleet that was never funded.  Every wall was somebody's plan, and none of the plans had met.  The corridors did not connect; they argued.  The demolition contractor was booked for the first of the month, and her supervisor wanted a load schedule and a cost by then.  Nadia wanted the reason the floor sweated in the same three places every morning. 

In the flooded baggage room she found the museum case the carnival children had jimmied, and in the silt beneath it a brass arc and a silvered mirror, cracked at one corner.  The placard, floating face down, said the sextant had belonged to the explorer who first charted this valley.  He had measured angles to learn where he stood; the piece in her hand still had graduations, still had a mirror.  She used the arc as a clinometer and the mirror to throw daylight down the dead corridor.  The floor fell one in three hundred, west, toward the marsh.  Under the slab, a culvert; under the culvert, a stream the builders had buried and forgotten to kill.  It had spent thirty years pressing up through the construction joints, patient as arithmetic. 

Her grandmother had sold bread at the gate here in the years of the timetable, and still called the terminal the living room.  The carnival had promised arrivals too, and had taken its promises down the highway in trucks.  Nadia did not want to restore anything; she wanted to puzzle out new equations for hope, ones whose inputs she could actually weigh.  The old equations had asked for launches per hour and had returned zero.  Hers asked for gradient, water, and hands. 

She drove the carnival's abandoned tent stakes as grade pins, graded a trial channel with a bumper car rail, and laid a ticket booth on its side as a weir.  Nothing she made was decorative; everything she made carried load.  On the ninth day she opened the south wall's plugged culvert mouth with a jackhammer signed out for concrete cores.  The stream came through the broken mouth like something remembering its name.  She did not fight it; she redirected it.  Through the customs hall, along the security corridor, out the gates that had never opened, she gave the water the maze to run.  Every dead intention became a channel, and the arguing walls agreed at last on one thing: downhill.  By dusk the floor was dry where it had sweated, and the concourse held a bright thread of water that had not moved in thirty years. 

Her supervisor came on Friday with the demolition forms, stood a long time on the dry terrazzo, and put them back in his truck.  He called the work functionally creative, which from him was a medal.  Her report ran four pages: a survey, a gradient, a bill of hands, and one sentence he read twice.  "You cannot resurrect a timetable, but you can resurrect a stream, and a market will come to stand beside it." 

She kept the cracked mirror, and on her last morning she used it to flash a beam down the concourse, the way an explorer signals a shore he has not reached.  The light went along the water and did not stop at any wall.  Nadia wrote the angle in her notebook and felt, without smiling, the size of what she had begun.
