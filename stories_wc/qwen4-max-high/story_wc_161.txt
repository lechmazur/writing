The attic above Ferris Books had one skylight, and Marta read it like weather.  Twenty years of training courier pigeons had taught her that air has grain, and that a bird comes home along it.  Now she sorted dead people's paper for eleven dollars an hour: ledgers, letters, sheet music soft as cloth.  The shop below was dying, which was why the attic kept blooming with other people's endings.  Downstairs, customers asked what she had done before, and she told them bookbinding.  She kept a second life ready, with no birds in it, and it went down easy as tea. 

The truth was that the valley had no phones until the year of the flood, and songs traveled by bird.  Her sister Noni sang, and Marta's pigeons carried the verses town to town on onion-skin slips, and that was how a song survived a winter.  Noni had been dead nine years, and only the paper trail of her singing remained.  Marta copied every slip she found into a ledger, keeping the echo the only way she knew.  One song was missing, the last one, and Marta told people a hawk took the bird that carried it.  She had told it so often the hawk had feathers. 

In an April lot she found a chip of Mars, black as a burnt match, with a lab paper naming the crater it came from.  A stone from another world, and it could prove its own history.  She set it on the left pan of a brass balance from a pharmacist's estate and laid paper on the right until the beam came level.  Nineteen slips of Noni's songs weighed the same as a crumb of Mars. 

Bo, who ran the register, kept climbing up to ask about the roof birds.  Three left of forty, and they would not come in through the trap.  Marta said the loft was a hobby, that she had never trained anything, that the birds were strays.  Bo wrote the answers in her notebook, which is what people do when they intend to keep you. 

In August Marta finally did the thing she had been avoiding: she lay on the boards under the skylight and mapped the swirl of dust motes.  Air is legible if you have spent your life releasing birds into it.  The motes rose, hesitated, and slid sideways at the height of her knee, and she followed the slide with a wet finger to a crack behind the poetry shelf, just above the trap.  Cold air fell there, and a homing bird reads a cold column as a hole and will not enter it.  Wedged behind the shelf, where she had shoved it the night of the flood and then trained herself to forget, was the release log. 

Her own hand, the morning of the flood: wind south, visibility poor, bird held.  No hawk.  She had kept Grey Ninety in the basket because she was afraid of the weather, and Noni went into the water with her last song unsent.  Nine years of invented weather, and the paper had kept the true account anyway. 

Bo came up with the lamp and found her holding the log like something hot.  Marta could have said the pages were water-damaged; she could have said anything.  Instead she read the line aloud, and her voice broke on held, and Bo did not look away. 

They plugged the crack with wax and paper pulp in September.  Under the skylight the motes turned in one clean slow wheel, no sideways slide, and Marta taught Bo to read it.  In October she let Bo hold a bird; in November she let her keep the log.  They copied Noni's nineteen songs onto fresh slips and sent them out to the valley towns, one bird at a time, so the echo would never live in only one attic again.  Some mornings the trap stayed empty and the old fear bloomed; by evening it withered when a grey hen came down the clean air and folded her wings. 

The chip of Mars stayed on the left pan, and the slips on the right grew until the beam swung, tipped, and settled level.  Downstairs, a customer asked what she had done before, and Marta said, "I trained pigeons, and once I was afraid, and the songs came home anyway."
