Provenance is the art of asking an object who owned it, in what order, and whether anyone was killed along the way.  Ilse Novak had asked it of eleven thousand objects, and never once of a violin made of ice crystal.  It stood in the velvet observatory at midsummer, on black felt, transparent as a held breath.  The dome's curtains were mulberry silk, drawn back for the one night each year the empire let the thing sweat under stars.  Beads ran down its strings into the felt, and every bead was a note it would never play again.  Beside it lay the warrant, written in imperial phosphor ink, promising Ilse a directorship, a pension, and a house with a garden.  The ink died at dawn; a promise unsigned by then was a promise unmade.  She had until the glow went out to certify the violin as a gift from the valley of Sarn, freely given.  The ledger said otherwise in three places, all of them scraped.  She had certified nine hundred imperial gifts, and Sarn was the valley she came from, which was why they had chosen her: a signature from a Sarn mouth would close the file forever. 

Old Vesna, who kept the telescopes and the coffee, tipped her cup onto a saucer and read the stains.  "Here is your morning," she said.  The brown ring was not a ring but a line of small holes, like the perforation between stamps.  "Destiny comes perforated," Vesna said.  "The empire prints it in sheets so it looks like one piece, but you may tear it wherever the holes allow."  Ilse laughed, and the laugh came out mulberry-colored, the ache she had carried since she was nine and the orchards burned.  Her hands shook, and she spilled her coffee into the potted mulberry by the dome wall.  The tree said, "Thirsty," and then, "Oh, you are the one who writes things down."  Ilse sat down on the cold floor.  Afterward she blamed the grounds, or the phosphor on her fingers; that night she only listened. 

The mulberry was a cutting of a cutting of a tree that had stood outside a luthier's workshop in Sarn.  It remembered the luthier's hands sticky with fruit, the spring he drew his crystal from, and the soldiers who took the violin and the orchard both.  A plant keeps what it is given and gives it on; it is the only archive that cannot be scraped.  "They burned the rest," Ilse said.  "They kept me," said the tree.  "They liked the silk."  The empire had wanted leaves for its worms, so it planted the valley with this same tree ten thousand times and called it a gift.  Every one of them was the witness the ledger had lost. 

Ilse held the warrant in one hand and a leaf in the other and tried to weigh a dream against an empire.  The dream was small: a garden, her name on a door, her mother's grave paid for.  The empire was enormous, and it had a hole in it, right at midsummer dawn.  She looked at the saucer and saw the perforation ran through the exact place where she was meant to sign.  Tearing an imperial warrant was ten years in the salt works.  So she tore the warrant along its printed seal-line and kept the half that still glowed.  On its back she wrote the true chain of custody: Sarn, the workshop, the spring, the soldiers, the three scrapings. 

Then she took cuttings.  Forty, from the dome tree and the corridor trees and the nursery, wrapped in the wet black felt that had caught the violin's melting.  She addressed them to archives, seed libraries, a school in Sarn.  A cutting propagates; you cannot unplant it, and no clerk can scrape a leaf.  Vesna read the saucer again and said the holes had moved. 

Dawn came up green over the observatory, and the warrant's glow went out in Ilse's pocket like a coal in water.  The violin was thinner by a morning's worth of music.  She squeezed the last of the pedestal's meltwater into the mulberry's pot, and the tree said, "Good, now we both remember."  Her fingers were stained mulberry, and would not wash clean, and she did not want them to.
