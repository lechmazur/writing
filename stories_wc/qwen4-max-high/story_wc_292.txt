Three years after the jet stream shifted, the fog came inland and stayed, and the peat under Station Nine went soft as a soaked sponge.  Wren Halloway kept the map library in the experimental weather station's old balloon shed, where charts hung like damp laundry and the walls sweated rust.  She believed a map was a promise: name a place and you owed it something.  The directors, who were seeding clouds to drag the stream back, believed a map was a forecast, and forecasts could be revised.  Every week the sea took another field, and every week Wren inked the loss.  In March the survey office ruled that Kettle Island, unpopulated and unprofitable, would be dropped from the new edition.  That was where they had buried her great-great-grandmother's name. 

Exile on this coast had always been reversible, which was the cruelest part of it.  The board wrote the condemned name on a plank, nailed the plank into a coffin of black oak, and buried the coffin in peat, which keeps everything.  Copper, so the nail would never rust; any descendant could pull it and bring the name back, given proof and a place to return to.  Alis Halloway had kept the light on Kettle the night the Perpetua broke apart, and the board ruled that her lamp drew the ship onto the teeth.  Eleven drowned, four lived, and the four were told what they had seen. 

After the shift, the new currents began handing things back.  Wreck timber, a brass porthole, three sailors' journals swollen to the thickness of loaves.  The prose had run, but the men had written their testimony in stanzas, and the stanzas held.  Rhymes about lanterns and lambs and a woman counting waves; nonsense, unless it was a cipher.  Wren copied them onto chart paper and worked at them for five months, and her dread grew soft and porous, absorbing every sound she made. 

In November she carried the pages to Director Sable, whose great-grandfather had signed the exile order.  She had rehearsed until her hands shook and her voice did not.  "When the board asked the survivors what they saw, did anyone write the answer down before telling them it was wrong?"  Sable's office smelled of wet wool.  He was quiet long enough that she heard fog dripping off the anemometer outside.  Then he unlocked a drawer and handed her the harbor board's cipher book, its spine furred with mold.  "We kept it because we were ashamed," he said, "not because we were proud." 

The stanzas opened like a hand.  The first letter of every line, read down, spelled LAMP LIT AFTER WRECK.  Four men had hidden it in a nursery rhyme because plain words were confiscated at the pier.  Alis had climbed ninety steps with oil on her back to relight a lamp the board had quietly let go dark to save money. 

She filed the proof at midnight; the edition went to press at noon.  At four she took the island boat out with a spade.  The peat gave under her boots like wet sponge, and each footprint filled slowly with black water.  She dug where the old chart said the exile ground lay, three feet down, and struck oak.  The plank read ALIS HALLOWAY, FALSE LIGHT, and beneath the letters the copper nail stood proud and green.  She pulled it with two fingers and it came out easily, the way a tooth comes out of soft gum.  Nothing thundered; the fog took the small sound and kept it.  She sat in the wet until her shaking stopped, holding the nail like a lit match. 

Back at the shed she trimmed the island's edge to what the sea had left and wrote the name across the empty interior.  A named place with a living claim cannot be dropped from an edition; the board had written that rule to punish, and it ran both ways.  Sable signed the correction at ten, and did not look at her, and did not need to.  The island would drown anyway, in twenty years or five.  But it would drown with her name on it, spelled correctly, in the hand of someone who had asked.  Wren laid the nail on the chart table, where it left a small green ring, and went out to read the instruments in the fog.
