The last summoner of her order lived alone beside a quiet brook, in a house with no bell and no clock.  She had not spoken aloud in eleven years, and the silence made her house a nocturnal haven rather than a hiding place.  Between midnight and dawn came the hour of whispered secrets, when the village upstream traded them into the water, and she let them pass unlistened. 

But among the whispers came one voice that was not human.  It tapped.  Stone against stone, in intervals, in the old cipher of her order: a prophecy asking to be summoned.  Prophecies are homeless until a mouth takes them in.  This one had been knocking for eleven years, and lately it had learned to spell a name.  Sela.  Her sister's daughter, six years old, asleep downstream beside a mill.  Given another season, it would not need a summoner at all. 

Her sister died the night the tapping began, leaving an origami crane unfinished: one wing, no body, no throat.  The summoner kept that wing on the windowsill, its creases sharp as a small skeleton.  Now she took it down and lit the lamp, and the flame stood up straight and did not crackle.  Everything she did burned that way, quietly, at full heat. 

She could not outspeak the code; any sound she made would be a mouth for it.  So she would answer it with its opposite.  She went out barefoot and began mapping silence.  Along the brook she listened for the gaps, the rests where the tapping failed.  Every cipher has holes, places its arithmetic cannot reach.  At each hole she set a pebble, and by the third hour she had a lattice of pale stones in the wet black ground. 

It matched the wing.  Her sister had folded the negative of the prophecy, the shape of its holes, and died before she could finish it.  Her order wrote its prophecies in constellations, and a crane's wing is a constellation folded small.  It was a terrestrially cosmic chart, heaven's arithmetic laid in mud where hands could reach it.  The map was not of the prophecy but of everything the prophecy could not say.  She carried the stones inside and folded the wing along their pattern, tightening each crease until the paper held the whole shape of the quiet. 

Then she opened the door and summoned.  She had no words, so she offered the wing instead, holding it out over the water like a cup.  The tapping stopped.  She felt the prophecy come up the bank toward her, eager, eleven years hungry, and enter the paper because paper is a mouth that cannot speak.  The wing went heavy and warm in her palm, and the creases began to lift.  It was trying to unfold itself into a crane, into a throat, into a bird that could fly downstream and say Sela's name over a sleeping child. 

She smoothed it flat.  Palm against paper, slowly, the way you calm a horse or a fever, until every crease lay down and the map inside it blurred into a single unbroken hush.  A smoothed hush: not emptiness, but a fullness with no seams left for a code to catch on.  The prophecy had nowhere to stand.  She could feel it searching the flat paper for an interval, a rest, a difference, and finding only the same quiet everywhere.  Nothing could be spelled there.  Nothing could be summoned there, not even by her. 

She knelt and set the wing on the brook.  For eleven years she had kept the wing instead of using it, because using it meant giving up the last thing her sister's hands had made.  She let it flow.  The current took the paper past the stones she had mapped, past the holes, out of the haven and down toward the mill, carrying a prophecy that no longer had a language.  Lamplight went with it, a slow orange blaze on black water, tranquil and unhurried, until the bend took the light away.  The brook tapped once, ordinarily, the way water does. 

Downstream, a child slept through the hour of secrets and woke at dawn to nothing prophesied.  The summoner sat on the bank until the stars went out, her hands empty, her hearing full of ordinary water.  Then she went inside and, for the first time in eleven years, said her sister's name aloud, into a room where nothing was listening.
