The rain stopped at dusk, and the cobalt dock held the sky in its puddles.  Every plank was painted that blue, forty years of it, layer on layer, so the whole wharf looked like a piece of fallen evening.  The lockman walked it twice a night and never spoke.  A fever had taken his voice at nine; the town called him the guardian and left him the keys.  Before the canal he had held mirrors for the dentist on Bridge Street.  There he had learned to angle light into the dark of strangers' mouths, and that darkness is only a room nobody has lit yet.  He kept one mirror, the small round one on its steel stem, in his coat.  With it he inspected the underside of the lock gate, the rot nobody could see straight on. 

That night a boy came down the steps with his shoes in his hand.  He sat where the blue paint ended and the water began, and he looked down the way the guardian had seen men look down before.  The canal lay flat and patient, holding nothing.  Thirty years ago he had watched a man look down like that, and the black water had given nothing back.  Since then the guardian had been teaching the dark what stars were worth. 

The boy said, "I was there when my brother," and then stopped, the way a foot stops at a step that is not there.  The rest of the sentence hung over the canal unfinished, and everything that happened next happened inside it.  The guardian did not ask, and did not wait to be told.  He sat down two planks away and took up his basket, the osier rods soaking in a bucket since morning.  He began at the center and worked outward, over and under, over and under.  He was spiralingly calm, and the calm went round and down like water over a drain, drawing the boy's shaking inward until it settled.  He did not reach for the boy; a hand at the wrong moment is a shout.  After a while he held out a wet rod, and the boy took it.  The guardian set the boy's hands in place and let him weave: over, under, pull.  Three times the rod slipped; the fourth time it held.  The unfinished sentence stayed where it was, over the water, and neither of them touched it. 

When the basket had a bottom, the guardian rose and went along the warehouses with his keys.  He lit the lamps inside, one room after another, as he did every night.  Then he worked the shutters.  Open, closed, closed, open: the old barge code, which he had long ago stopped using for barges.  Light fell through the gaps in slabs and bars and lay down on the wet blue planks and the flat black water.  He had been laying that pattern for thirty years, so that anyone who looked down would find stars instead of an open door.  The boy looked down and saw them, and leaned an inch, and the water waited. 

But under the dock, in the mouth of the culvert, the light could not reach.  The guardian knelt and took the dental mirror from his coat and gave it to the boy.  He showed him how to lie flat, reach into the gap, and tilt the little glass until it caught a shutter's star and carried it under.  The boy's arm went into the cold to the shoulder.  In the round glass a small light appeared in the last dark place, and turned, and held.  There was nothing else under the dock, and the boy looked for a long time anyway.  Then he made a sound that was not the rest of the sentence.  The guardian nodded once, knowing the whole sentence and needing none of it, and let the boy keep the mirror. 

The boy pulled his arm out and sat back from the edge and put his shoes on, which took a long time.  They finished the basket together while the shutters held their pattern on the water.  Near morning the boy began the sentence again, and again stopped, and this time the stopping was a different thing: not a fall, but a place to stand.  The guardian shut the last shutter, and the stars went out of the water one by one.  The boy went up the steps with the mirror in his fist, carrying one star out of the canal into the dark, where it would be needed.
