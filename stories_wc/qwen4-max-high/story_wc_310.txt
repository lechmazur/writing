After the universe contracted, light had nowhere left to go, so it thickened.  It set around the observatory deck like resin around a wasp, and Sela worked inside that amber, in the honeyed last afternoon of everything.  She was the station's caster of lots, which was serious work now.  Prophecy ran backwards.  Since the contraction the sky's messages arrived ending-first, so nobody could learn anything before doing it; you learned, afterward, what you had been going to do.  Decisions therefore fell to chance, and chance fell to Sela. 

Her instrument was a jar of foreign coins.  Thirty-one, salvaged from countries drowned long before the stars went out: a heron, a wheat sheaf, a queen with a broken nose.  Nobody left could read their faces, which made them perfect lots; you cannot bribe a coin whose language you have lost.  They were the last foreign things in existence, and she liked that the universe still contained a face it could not read.  Sela was quietly enthusiastic about them.  She never raised her voice, but she polished them on her sleeve and hummed while she worked, and the hum was the loudest thing on the deck. 

The still stood at the ambered deck's cold end.  Static bursts came down the antenna and she distilled them, noise into water, water into one amber drop holding a single syllable of the sky.  Understanding the bursts had taken her eleven years.  You laid the drops in a row, read them right to left, and heard a sentence about something already finished.  The finished thing was usually tomorrow.  Once the drops told her she had already sealed the west hatch; she walked there and found it sealed, her own handprint in the frost.  She had never decided anything since, and she had never stopped wondering whether that was faith or hiding. 

Tonight the bursts came thick.  She condensed nine drops, laid them out, and read the backwards prophecy twice.  "She will have opened the valve, and the amber will have gone out of the world, and she will have been the one who wanted it." 

Below the deck, in the ballast tank, sat the last of the contracted light, distilled to a seed.  Open the valve and it would spend itself outward, unbearably, and there would be a sky again, and no Sela in it.  Keep it shut and the amber held, warm and honey-colored, forever or until the seals failed.  This was the doubt she lived in, the size of a star's absence.  She had cast lots for smaller things and slept fine afterward.  Now she shook the jar and stopped, because the prophecy had already given her the ending, and a lot cast under a known ending is not a lot; it is theater. 

For eleven years she had wanted one thing: to sit at the same wavelength as her purpose, to hum and find the deck humming back.  She had assumed alignment meant obedience.  Let the coins say valve, and she would open it, and be a hand, and never learn whether she wanted anything at all.  But the sentence ended on wanted.  Not chosen for, not permitted: wanted.  The sky, running backwards, had bothered to record her appetite.  That was the alignment, then: not the coin's answer, but her own hum matching the sky's.  She let herself want it, loudly inside, hands still. 

She picked the queen with the broken nose out of the jar, set her face up on the console, and did not throw her.  A choice, recorded, not a lot.  Then she opened the valve.  The amber went out of the world the way a held breath goes out.  The coins went with it, thirty-one unreadable faces scattered outward, still foreign, still refusing to decide anything for anyone.  She felt her wavelength stretch, and stretch, and match. 

In the last of the light the antenna caught one burst, and she distilled it out of habit: one drop.  She read it left to right, because there was no right to left anymore, no finished thing waiting behind her.  It said: In the beginning.  She hummed, and something enormous and new hummed back, exactly on her wavelength, and she doubted it, and went on humming.
