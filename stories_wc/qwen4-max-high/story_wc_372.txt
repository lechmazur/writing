The library stood above the cloud line on a spine of black rock, and all its shelves were ice.  Snow that came through the roof gaps stayed snow.  The silence had a grain to it, a quartz hush, and a turned page sounded loud as a snapped bone. 

Hesper Ain had been an orderly in the fever wards below before the water took them; now she was records chief of the last archive.  She worked the way she had once washed the dying: gently meticulous, one body at a time, no haste anywhere in her hands. 

The archive held luminous vows.  When people below still had a future to promise each other, they climbed the goat stair and spoke their promises into the lantern glass, and the glass kept them as light.  Four hundred and six slides remained in the ice, each a magic lantern slide the size of her palm, each holding a marriage, a debt forgiven, a soldier's word to return. 

She could no longer let anyone believe in them.  That season the light had begun to settle.  Projected, a slide had once filled the wall with a woman's face and the smell of her hair; now it gave a pale rectangle and a fact.  The illusions were fading to memory, and Hesper marked the difference in her ledger: a vow you could still be fooled by, and a vow you merely knew. 

Her apprentice, Tobin, wanted a final showing.  All four hundred, one after another, the lamp burning until the oil was gone, so the valley refugees climbing up for the winter could see what their parents had promised.  His mother's slide was in the third drawer; he had not seen her face since the flood. 

"The lamp's heat opens the shelves," Hesper said.  "And each showing spends what is left."  "We would be burning them to look at them." 

So she measured instead.  She built a comb of brass slits, set a prism behind the lantern's throat, and measured by sampling frequencies: a narrow band at the red end, then the green, then the violet, where the promising lived.  The slits took a sliver of the light instead of all of it, so a vow could be read without being spent.  She counted the cycles that survived in each band and wrote the numbers down.  A slide with three hundred cycles in the violet had a decade left.  A slide with nine had a week, and she copied that one into ink first, in her small even hand, before the light went flat and took the words with it. 

His mother's slide had eleven cycles left, so she copied it out that night and gave him the page to keep in his coat.  Forty slides a day, the warmth rationed, and after that night Tobin copied beside her without being asked, because the ledger needed a second hand. 

On the ninth day she came to slide 112 and knew the hand on its paper sleeve, because it was hers.  She had made it in the ward, the winter the water rose: I will keep their names.  She had been twenty-two and certain, and the light in the glass was the color of a lamp seen through tears. 

She set it in the lantern and slid the comb to violet.  Nine cycles, perhaps eight.  She could burn it now on the wall and have her own face back for a minute, young and sure.  Tobin watched her hand on the shutter. 

Instead she wrote the vow in the ledger, and beneath it the names of the ward dead, one hundred and nine of them, and she read the list aloud to the boy in the cold until he could say the first fifty without her. 

Within the week the glass went grey in its sleeve.  The names did not.  That was the work, she had decided: not to keep the light, but to conjure meaning from the edge of oblivion, one measured and remembered thing at a time. 

Outside, the clouds lay flat and blue as a frozen sea.  Inside, the quartz hush, the stylus, the boy's voice getting the names right.
