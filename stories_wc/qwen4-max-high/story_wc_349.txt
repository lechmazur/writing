On my first opening day, the rush lasted eleven minutes.  Then came the lull, and the glass orchard library ticked around me in the light.  Below the floor, the sea of clouds turned over and over, swallowing its own weather.  Above it, the trees grew books.  Glass branches, glass leaves, and at the end of every twig a fruit with pages inside, ripening toward a story.  I sell clean slates.  That is the whole sign, painted small: CLEAN SLATES, and under it, in smaller letters, FOR THE PAROLED.  You have seen them walking the rows.  The paroled carry their release in a bottle at the throat: bottled moonlight, glimmering.  Glimmering parole, the librarians call it, because the light is the condition.  Go back to your old plot, take up the same grief in the same order, and the bottle goes dark and the story takes you again.  So they come to me for blankness.  I keep my stock mindfully scattered.  Nine slates, one to a row, hidden under glass leaves where nobody trips over them.  Blankness handed over is only forgetfulness; blankness walked to, chosen, is a decision.  I learned that from my own bottle.  Yes.  Mine is thin as a moth's wing, and I do not remember the story it let me out of.  They lift the plot out to let you go; only the light stays.  That is why I opened a shop in the only library that floats.  I am looking for the place where stories begin.  Somewhere in these rows there must be a first blank, a seed, a shelf where nothing has happened yet, and if I stand there I will know what I am allowed to start.  I search by quantifying absence.  The gauge is a brass caliper that measures how much narrative is missing from a thing.  A ripe book reads almost zero: everything is present in it, whole and accounted for.  A clean slate reads high.  For nine months before this morning I logged the rows, hunting the highest number, the purest emptiness, the origin.  Row forty reads sixty-one.  Row twelve, eighty.  The lull is when I do this work, because in the lull nobody watches me measure.  At the far end of row one, where the clouds come up through a gap in the floor and wet the glass, a girl was waiting.  Her bottle was nearly out.  Not dark, but low, the way a lamp is low.  "I keep almost going back," she said.  "I want a slate."  I brought her one from under the leaves, and out of habit I set the caliper on it.  Ninety-four.  Higher than any row I had logged, higher than the gap where the cloud comes in.  She held it and nothing happened.  "It's very clean," she said, "but I don't know what to put first."  And there it was, the flaw in nine months of arithmetic.  Absence is not a beginning.  Absence is only the shape of what was taken away, and you can measure it forever and never arrive anywhere, because it is a hole, and a hole has no first page.  A story does not begin where nothing is.  It begins where someone cuts.  I took the slate back and my knife out.  To incise glass you must commit; the wheel scores once and the whole pane agrees to break along that line.  I incised a single line across her blankness, one shallow groove, a place for a first sentence to sit.  When I measured again it read eighty-one, and I let it.  Then I did the thing I had been afraid of for nine months.  I incised my own slate, the one I kept wrapped in cloth beneath the register, the perfect one, the ninety-nine.  Not my old story.  A new cut, a new groove, and my bottle at my throat took the moonlight and brightened until the girl stepped back.  Parole, it turns out, is not a permission to stay empty.  It is a condition that you keep beginning.  The girl wrote in her groove with her finger, and her low lamp steadied, and the orchard ticked, and somewhere a fruit ripened toward a story that had not existed that morning.  The lull ended.  I sold her the slate for a promise, and hung the caliper on a nail, and left my stock mindfully scattered, and went out into the rows to meet the afternoon.
