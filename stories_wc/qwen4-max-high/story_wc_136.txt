Vera mapped the city's tram lines for the transit office, and she mapped them beautifully, because she could not help it.  Her hand hatched the hills in silver; her mind held the timetables to the second; her supervisors said the two halves of her never argued.  Only one map hung unfinished in her apartment: the route her father walked the night he stopped coming home. 

He had been a night conductor, and her mother said he left, and the family closed over the sentence like water over a stone.  Vera was seven, and she learned the household's bad habit: never ask.  Twenty-nine years of not asking taught her that silence has an address, and she meant to find it.  Old conductors had a name for that address, passed down in depot gossip: the fortress of unspoken truths, a terminus on a line no timetable admitted. 

She found the punch in a lost-property crate, brass worn smooth, stamped with the number of a train that was never built.  The crate's tag said PHANTOM SERVICE, and the depot men laughed at her for keeping it.  All week its jaws closed on nothing with a dry click; it would not cut paper, or card, or her thumbnail.  But at midnight, held over her father's map, it bit a star-shaped hole clean through the page, and through the hole she saw a light that was not her lamp. 

She had been copying pavement cracks for years, out of the same compulsion that made her hatch hills.  In November she noticed the cracks were making arrows.  They pointed, block by block, and each midnight she looked through the star-hole and saw one crack shining: the next arrow.  She tested them the way she tested a junction: three nights of walking backward, and the arrows held. 

On the last night of the year she walked them, coat over her pajamas, the map folded in her fist.  Behind her the city was drinking and promising; ahead lay the hour that divides bad habits from new starts, and she had one of each to settle.  Mist came off the river and ate the streetlamps.  The final arrow was a crack in the floor of a railway underpass, wide as her wrist, aimed at a blank wall. 

The wall had a door in it, and the door stood open.  Inside was a vault of ribbed stone with no roof, or a roof made of sky: stars burned between the ribs, close and wet-looking.  Mist pooled at her ankles, and in it stood racks of tickets, thousands, every one punched with a star.  A window waited in the far wall, and behind the glass there was nobody, only a slot. 

She understood the fare the way you understand rules in dreams: to take a truth out, put one in.  This was the dare she had walked eleven miles to refuse.  Her hands shook; her voice, when it came, did not. 

"He came back," she said.  "That night I heard his key in the lock and his breathing on the other side of the door, and I was seven and angry and I stayed in bed and did not call out."  "He waited until the hour turned, and then he went away." 

The punch went warm in her palm and cut a star out of the empty air, and the little disc of nothing fell ringing on the stone.  A ticket slid from the slot, soft as cloth, punched once.  Her father's cramped conductor's hand, the one he used for destinations: I could not come in unasked.  I stood there and waited for my daughter to say my name. 

She read it twice, the way she measured curves.  Then she said, into all that starlight and mist, "Come in," and the mist moved like something turning over in sleep.  Nothing came; the fortress keeps truths, not men. 

At twelve-forty she knocked on her mother's door, and when the chain came off she asked, straight out, what had happened to him.  Her mother stood a long moment in the gap, trembling and certain, and then she opened the door.  It was not an answer yet.  It was a door, which is how answers start.
