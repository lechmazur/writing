The canyon did not answer everyone, and Sela had built a life on that discourtesy.  She was an echo singer, hired to stand at the braided mouth of nine gorges and sing the names of the dead back to the families who waited below.  Some names came home.  Some went into the rock and never came out, and the families paid anyway, and went home lighter by a coin and heavier by nothing they could name.  Sela wanted to know why some people echo.  Her mother had gone into the rock eleven years ago, mid-verse, on the night the stone hummed.  All she left was half a seashell, a conch split lengthwise, its spiral cut open like a small pink ear.  Held against her own ear it whispered, always, a sound like breath through a reed, and Sela had spent eleven years failing to make words of it.  Tonight the nexus lay under starlight, nine canyons coiling into one another like snakes drinking at the same pool.  Tonight, too, the universe would hum its secret song, the low note that came once a year out of the deep stone and made every wall a throat.  During the Hum, nothing said in the nexus could be unsaid.  Sela was richly perceptive; it was her only wealth.  She heard the wet click of a tongue inside an echo that was supposed to be stone.  She heard which returns arrived a half breath late, the delay of a person waiting to be paid.  There were galleries behind the walls, and in the galleries there were answerers.  The canyon was not a miracle; it was a market.  Houses bought echoes for names that still opened doors, and bought silence for names that opened investigations.  Her mother had been an answerer who began repeating the wrong names, and the web had closed over her the way water closes.  Sela had come to ensure that the ninety-one names in the Concord's silence-ledger reached the mourners at the nine mouths before dawn.  She could not shout them; the gallery men would smother a shout.  So she would give them away slowly, the way her mother had taught her to feed a fire in wet weather.  One name to a verse, and each verse a little nearer the truth, so that no one could say where the song had turned.  The Concord's factors sat below with their ledgers open, expecting the usual licensed grief.  She began with a fisherman.  The wall gave him back, bored and obedient.  She sang the clerk who had copied the ledger for her, and the return came late, and she heard a man swallow before he spoke.  She sang a surveyor, and two galleries answered at once, arguing.  By the ninth verse the answerers had stopped waiting for orders and were simply answering, because a name in the Hum pulls at a throat like a hook.  Then the universe settled into its secret song, and while it hummed, every wall in the serpentine nexus carried her verses down to the mourners.  A factor rose at the eleventh verse with his hand up, and the stone was already carrying, and a hand cannot stop a note.  Ninety-one names went out under the stars, and no house could buy them back.  Last, she sang her mother.  She held the broken conch to her ear while she sang, and this time the whisper resolved into words, because the shell was only ever half a listening tube, and somewhere in the galleries a stranger held the other half and had finally leaned close.  "She is not here," the shell whispered.  "I have breathed into this half for eleven years, waiting for someone to come to the wall for her."  That was the whole secret, and it was small and terrible: people echo because someone on the other side decides to answer.  Her mother had not been swallowed by stone; she had been unpaid, unassigned, unanswered.  Sela sang the name again.  The stranger in the wall, who had a mother too, gave it back.  It came home thin and breathy, a reed note bent by grief, and the mourners at the nine mouths lifted their heads.  Sela sat down under the stars with half a shell in her lap, listening to the hum fade, learning the price of being heard, and knowing she would spend her life on the other side of the wall, answering.
