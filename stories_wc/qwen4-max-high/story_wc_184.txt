Mira Solvang catalogued things that were not there.  The university called her an archivist; the porters called her the woman who could find a missing thing by describing its hole.  She was warmly methodical: tea for the night winder, numbered pencils, a label for every drawer, and a habit of learning a new trade whenever a record defeated her.  That autumn the record that defeated her was a shelf of folk tales the folklorists had titled Fables of Absence.  Stories about a bell that never rang, a fourth guest at a dinner of three, a door that was never hung.  Students laughed at them; Mira read them twice and noticed that each one ended with an instruction.  Braid the fen last.  Let the fourth strand carry the weight.  Her other puzzle was smaller and heavier: a diplomat's wax seal impression, no document attached, catalogued in 1804 as an orphan.  The wax showed a rope knot of four strands, and beneath it the diplomat's pressed initials: the man who had signed the university's founding concord with the fen villages, and whose signature was later scraped from the treaty.  Three cities had signed; the fourth party had been unmade, and the fables were the only witnesses left.  Mira's whole career had one motive, which she wrote on her intake forms: to translate confusion into clarity.  So she went up the tower.  The mechanism room smelled of hot iron and pigeon dust; the great wheel turned, the escapement clacked, and the strike train hauled its leather cord over three grooved barrels.  On the north wall, where every plan since 1690 showed solid stone, there was a door.  It had no handle, only a slot, and above the slot a groove worn by a rope that no longer ran there.  Anselm, the night winder, gave her the tower's one rule: the north door could only be argued with during the eleven minutes of the equinox winding, when the door shouldn't exist, because the charter calls the room the clock's interior only while the clock runs.  She brought the ceremonial concord rope up in a cotton sling: three strands, city-dyed, retired to a case when the tower was re-corded in leather.  The wax said four strands, the fables said four, and the rope in her hands had three, because the fen strand had been cut out and the ends spliced.  Mira had spent the summer learning ropewalk work from a retired rigger in the fen town, and she carried a fourth strand she had twisted herself from fen rush, dyed with marsh iron.  Her plan was not force but order: to open the wall by reconfiguring the braids of a centuries-old ceremonial rope.  A four-strand braid sits fatter than a three; seat it in the deepest groove and the strike train hauls a different barrel.  At the equinox, with the weights down and the escapement still, she braided city, city, fen, city, so the fen strand rode outermost.  Then she merged rope and machine, threading the old cord back into the works it had been retired from, while Anselm wound the strike barrel eleven turns.  The fatter braid gripped the fourth groove, the one no plan admitted, and somewhere inside the wall a counterweight began to climb.  The door rose a hand's width, breathing out cold air and the smell of old paper.  Inside lay the concord as first signed, four seals, the fen name whole, and beside it the scraping knife, left like a confession.  Mira did not gloat; she photographed the knife where it lay, slid the concord out through the gap, and numbered its folios.  The fables went into the catalogue beside the treaty, cross-referenced, no longer nonsense: testimony kept by people who had been told they were not there.  When the eleven minutes ended and the clock resumed, the wall was solid again, exactly as the plans insisted.  The confusion had not been an error in the record; it had been a policy.  Clarity, Mira wrote in her report, is a machine you must re-braid before it will lift anything.  She left the fen strand in the works, and the tower has struck four bells at noon ever since, which the university is still learning how to explain.
