The airship Perpetua had not flown in nine years, but she still caught the last of the daylight in her skin.  Her envelope was patched linen, her gondola brass and teak, and the museum that owned her wanted her scrapped by spring.  Nell, apprentice to a cartographer who had died owing her three lessons, was the only person aboard.  She liked it that way, mostly, and she waved at everybody.  From the mooring gallery she called down to the fair crews, traded gossip with a passing skiff, and learned the names of all six children who came up to see the old gas cells.  Then they went home to their suppers, and the ship was hers again, and the silence had a friendly shape to it. 

Her master's last commission lay unfinished on the chart table: a blank quadrant of the salt flats labeled, in his shaky hand, slow light.  He used to say the flats were finished; she had never once believed him.  Nell wanted the next great discovery on paper, small and certain, because a discovery is an argument the museum cannot scrap.  The only clue her master had left was the interpreter's phrasebook from the pilgrim years, its leather cracked, one page dog-eared so often it had gone soft as cloth.  Seven languages asked the same question there: where does the light wait?  In the eighth, a mountain tongue nobody spoke on the flats anymore, someone had penciled the answer: underfoot, where the feet were.  She copied the phrase into her log every week, faithfully, the way you water a seed you cannot name. 

On the evening the starlings came back, she finally looked down.  Autumn murmurations boiled over the flats at dusk, ten thousand birds folding and unfolding like something breathing.  The old varnish on the observation deck began to glow.  Every survey hand knew the trick: dope and linseed hold an hour of sun after the sun has gone, stranded luminance, light left behind like a guest who missed the boat.  Usually it faded evenly, a dull green wash, useless.  Tonight it came up in squares.  The hopscotch board.  Pilgrim children had scratched it into the deck sixty years ago, and the fair children still hopped it every afternoon, so the dust lay everywhere except in eight polished squares.  Where feet had worn the varnish thin, the trapped light escaped first. 

Nell knelt with her log and noted the order.  Four, then one, then seven, then two.  Not the hopping order; the wearing order, the places small feet had landed hardest, year after year, in a game whose rhyme nobody remembered.  She noted the intervals too, timing each square against the ship's clock, and the intervals were not random.  They were distances.  Eight squares, eight legs of a walking route, the worn depths giving the lengths, the sequence giving the turns.  The pilgrims had taught their children the road as a game, so that a thing worth knowing would survive being forgotten.  Nell plotted it on the blank quadrant, hands shaking, and the line ran out into salt where no survey had ever bothered to go. 

Outside, the starlings turned all at once and poured toward that bearing, and beneath them the flats glimmered faintly, an hour late, giving back a sun that had already set.  Salt holds light the way varnish does, and the birds came for the warmth rising off it.  A marsh of stranded luminance, forty miles wide, waiting in the dark for somebody to write it down.  She had the discovery, and nobody to tell until morning.  So she hailed the empty gallery, told the gas cells, and laughed at herself, and the laugh sounded like company. 

Then, because a chart is only a message to a stranger, she went back to the board.  She chalked a ninth square at the end of the eight and wrote the bearing inside it, small, where a hopping child would put a foot.  In the phrasebook she added a ninth language, her own, and folded the corner down beside the old one.  Whoever wore that square thin would be wearing the road, and the light would come up under them, and they would know where to look.  Nell signed the quadrant, waved goodnight to the last skiff, and sat alone in the friendly glow until the starlings settled.
