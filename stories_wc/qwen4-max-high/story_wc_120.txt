Vera wrote fortunes for a living, three hundred a night, and not one of them promised anybody a future.  Her desk stood on the top floor of a glass spire beside a frozen lake, an old hotel the bakery had bought for its ovens and its silence.  All winter the ice held the sky so perfectly that the horizon vanished.  Cloud above, cloud below; a mirrored horizon, with the spire hanging in the middle of it like a needle in a bubble.  Vera worked inside that doubling and lost her hours in it. 

She was not homesick for a place.  The house she grew up in was a parking lot now, and she felt nothing about that.  She was homesick for a November when she was nine, for the radiator's two knocks and her mother humming above it, and she could no more travel there than to the moon.  What she had instead was a segment of nautilus shell, three chambers of it, a pale comma her father had carried home from a ship and dropped on the kitchen tile.  The break had taken the rest of the spiral, but the fragment still knew the ratio by which a life widens. 

Vera had studied conducting once, before the money stopped.  She did not want an orchestra.  She wanted to conduct unheard symphonies, music that never gathered into one room, and the cookies were how she had learned to do it.  Every fortune was a cue.  Hum while the kettle climbs.  Count four before you open the door.  Say the name you have not said this year.  Ten thousand strangers a week obeyed her without knowing they had been given a downbeat, in diners and parking lots and hospital cafeterias, each holding one thin bar of the piece.  No one would ever hear the whole of it.  That was the point, and that was the grief.  She could not go back to that November, but she could make it happen again in pieces, in strangers' kitchens, at hours she would never know. 

Tonight she was finishing the last movement, and she had until dawn.  The trucks left at first light; whatever she wrote after that would sit in a drawer for a year.  There was a harder deadline underneath it.  Exhaustion came for her at a known hour, and it took the hearing first: the inner music thinned, then flattened into ordinary noise, and she could not find the notes at all.  She had to finish before exhaustion hit. 

So she worked the way she always worked, by mapping childhood memories.  She laid the shell segment on graph paper and traced its curve outward, continuing the spiral the break had stolen, chamber by chamber, year by year.  Into each chamber she set one memory and took from it a duration and a pitch: the two radiator knocks, a low pair; the screen door's spring, a rising third; her mother's hum, held.  The shell decided the tempo, because a nautilus widens at a fixed rate, and so does a child.  The map was how she got home to a November no calendar would take her to. 

By four she had reached the chamber she had been avoiding.  Nine years old, the lake behind the house, the first freeze, her father saying listen, and the ice making a sound like a string being tuned under a floor.  That was the note the whole symphony had been walking toward, and it had been sitting in the frozen lake outside her window all winter. 

Her hands had begun to shake.  She wrote the last fortune fast: Listen to the largest quiet thing near you.  Then she carried the tray down the spire's stairs and set it in the hopper, and the folding machines closed her downbeat into ten thousand pale mouths. 

Outside, the sun came over the ridge.  Light struck the ice and the mirrored horizon broke; the lower sky drained away, and the world put its horizon back where it belonged, one clean line.  The trucks pulled out. 

Vera stood at the glass with the shell segment in her fist and raised her other hand.  Somewhere ahead of the trucks a woman would crack a cookie and hum while her kettle climbed; a man would count four; a boy would say a name.  She held the upbeat, and the hush went up like a held breath, and the whole unheard thing waited, one inch above the world, for her hand to fall.
