Nim's hands shook so badly that the relics in her pack chimed like small bells.  She had been a courier for eleven days, and she was still certain that every ridge held someone waiting to tell her she was a fraud.  Above the ridgeway the aurora came up green and slow, folding like a curtain in a draft.  Sabeth would have stopped to watch it.  Sabeth had walked this route for forty years and then, one March, had not come back from the pass, leaving Nim a mule, a route book, and a pendant.  Inside its glass lay a dragonfly wing, older than any road, veined like a map of a country nobody had named.  The trick of it, Sabeth said, was that it vibrated sympathetically: whatever it came near, it answered.  A struck bell, a heartbeat, a person leaning in to listen.  And while it answered, the moment held.  A doorstep goodbye stretched into an hour of soup and stories; a handoff at a gate became a whole afternoon.  That was the work, Sabeth insisted, not the carrying.  Anyone could carry.  The job was to coax wonder out of the routine, because a road walked without attention was just gravel, and a life of gravel would grind a person down to grit.  Nim believed her, and also believed she had been left the wrong inheritance: the pendant needed a steady hand, and hers trembled.  Tonight's relic was a paper twist of seeds.  Terrace villages below the ridgeway had eaten their seed grain through the long winter, and the archive at the head of the valley had opened its oldest jar.  Dormant things, the archivist warned, sometimes need persuading.  Sabeth's route book said the same thing in a hand Nim could not read without hearing her voice: warm them with attention.  The first door in the village opened on a woman too tired to be curious.  Nim felt the pendant go quiet against her collarbone, and her hands shook harder, and she nearly fled.  Then a girl came around the corner of the house, maybe nine, holding a chipped bowl.  She looked at the wing and asked what it was.  Nim said: a dragonfly that died before there were people to see it.  The girl leaned in.  The pendant began to hum, so faintly that Nim felt it in her teeth before she heard it, and the aurora stopped folding and simply hung.  Nim's shaking did not stop; it turned out the pendant did not want stillness, only sympathy, and a trembling hand is a kind of listening.  She talked.  She told the girl about Sabeth, who could make a toll gate last until the stars moved, and who had gone over the pass and left a hole shaped exactly like her.  The girl asked questions, and each question lengthened the minute they stood in.  In that stretched hour Nim knelt and pressed the seeds into the cold soil beside the doorstep, and the wing sang against the ground, matching the small stubborn frequency of the things inside the husks.  By the time the woman came out with a lamp, the seeds had germinated, white roots threading down and pale hooks lifting the dirt.  One hour, borrowed out of an evening that should have held four minutes.  Nim walked the ridgeway for fifty years after that.  The details went first, the way details do: the mule's name, the chipped bowl, the exact green of that night.  Then Sabeth's name went, and Nim's after it, until the couriers who inherited the route book only knew that a shaking woman had once carried a wing along the ridge and made a village bloom in a single evening.  They tell it now at every handoff, and they get it wrong, and they tell it anyway.  The pendant still hangs in the archive, and it still answers, though nobody remembers whom it answered first.  What survived the fade from memory into myth was not the woman but the habit: stop at the door, ask the question, let the minute stretch.  Wonder, it turns out, is a seed too, and it germinates in whoever is paying attention.
