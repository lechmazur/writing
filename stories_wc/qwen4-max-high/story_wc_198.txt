The fog came up the headland every morning at four, and Odile came with it, saxophone under her arm like a sleeping bird.  People in town called her the morning fog saxophonist, which was accurate; she had never once played in sunlight.  Below her stood the prism atrium, three hundred panes of glass angled into a single wedge, and in the mist it stopped being a building and became a radiant grotto, light pooling in it the way water pools in a cave. 

Her grandmother had built it as a listening room.  During the war Ilse had sat inside it with headphones and a pencil, taking down a station that sang instead of spoke.  Nine bars of melody, repeated for eleven nights, then never again.  The cryptographers called it a forecast; Ilse called it a prophecy, because it named a date that had not happened yet, and a town, and the town was this one.  She never decoded the last bar.  She wrote the whole thing out as an etude for saxophone, hid it in a book of scales, and taught her granddaughter to play everything except the ending.  "The code is a mouth," Ilse whispered, the last morning she could speak at all.  "Play around it and it stays shut." 

The will was stranger than the etude.  The atrium and the headland were left in trust, and the trust held one asset the lawyers could not name, so they typed "the hush."  It vested in Odile at dewfall on the equinox, on condition that she keep it: one hour of playing in the grotto, and not one note of the ninth bar.  "A vested hush," the clerk said, laughing, and then not laughing.  That morning was this morning. 

Odile stepped inside as dewdrops formed on the glass, each pane sweating one bead at a time, and the beads took the early light and broke it into quietly vivid bands, rose and iodine and a green she had only ever seen in her grandmother's kitchen.  The mist made the grotto enormous and small at once, a cave you could hold.  She wet the reed.  She began at bar one, and the fog carried the tone out through the vents to the town below, where people would wake with a tune in their heads and never know why.  Bars one through eight were safe; they were only a question.  The ninth bar was the answer, and an answer, Ilse used to say, is a door that opens from the other side. 

Odile wanted it.  She wanted the date, the ending, the sound of the old woman's voice arriving out of the code the way she used to arrive out of the fog with her collar up and her hands cold.  Silence alone would not do it; an empty bar is an invitation, and the fog was patient and had a good ear.  Instead she played the negative space.  She played the scale the bar was built from, every degree but the ones it needed; she played the rhythm with the hits lifted out; she played the breath, the valve noise, the small knock of the key, so that the shape of the missing bar stood in the air like a hole cut in paper, exact, legible, and silent.  Anyone listening would hear a prophecy the way you hear a word with the vowels taken out.  The glass hummed along, hungry, filling in overtones.  Twice the atrium tried to sing the bar for her, the panes ringing where the dew ran, and twice she drowned it under a low B-flat that meant nothing at all. 

The hour turned.  The date the code had named was this one, and it had waited sixty years for a mouth.  Odile gave it a hole instead.  Dew gathered and slid and gathered, the light climbed the wedge of glass, and the day went on being ordinary, which was the whole of the inheritance.  The hush vested.  She owned a silence now, and the glass, and the fog that came up the headland at four, and she understood at last that her grandmother had not left her a secret but a job. 

Odile packed the horn, and before she closed the case she leaned into the shape she had left in the air and whispered her grandmother's name, breath only, no tone, the way you speak to someone in a room where they used to sleep.  Outside, the fog thinned, and the town came up out of it, roof by roof, still there.  Tomorrow at four she would come back and keep it again.
