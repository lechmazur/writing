The thunder had been walking the mountains all afternoon and never came nearer.  Voss kept it there.  His seeders combed the storms apart above the pass, so the basin stayed white, and the lithium men loved him.  He had given the town a clinic, a school, and this drought.  He saw no difference in the giving, and I took his money anyway.  He never asked what any of it cost anyone else.  My archive of extinct voices lives on the interest of dry lakes.  I have eleven thousand hours of dead birds and no place left that will answer them. 

That morning he handed me a foam case.  Inside lay an egg the size of a plum, gray where the blue had gone out of it.  Sarn grebe, last clutch, 1971, the year his father's diversion took the lake.  He had bought it at auction, along with the drawer and the museum's debt.  "I have the recording," he said.  "I want the echo." 

A voice is only a voice.  An echo is a place agreeing that the voice happened.  Salt swallows sound; the flat gave back nothing, and he had made the flat.  So I spent the summer reading cracks.  Where the old rivers died, the mud had split into polygons, wide where the silt lay deep, hairline where it lay thin.  From a drone, those cracks drew a map: every drowned channel, every terrace, the exact lip of the vanished shore.  I found the cove where the grebes had sung, and the cliff that used to hand their calls back doubled.  Half a mile out on the salt, I scratched an X. 

Now we stood at it while the thunder waited at the range like a dog on a chain.  The seeders cycle every forty minutes; the lull between volleys lasts twenty-two.  He had bought that lull from his own company at a price he never told me.  On the invoice, the twenty-two minutes were listed as a calibration test.  "Open it," he told the radio.  Upgradient, men lifted the canal gates, and the water came out brown and then clear, spreading over the salt in a sheet no deeper than my laces.  An inch of lake, rented by the minute.  The sky lay down on the ground.  It smelled like the inside of a shell, mineral and old. 

I set the speaker on a float.  Voss knelt in his good trousers and held the egg to his ear, the way children hold shells.  He had been nine when he lifted it out of a nest going cold in the reeds, and he had never once said sorry, to me or to anyone.  He did not want the lake back.  He wanted proof that the lake had answered. 

I played the file.  One male, recorded in 1969 by a man with a reel machine, singing the call that has a gap in it where the female should come in.  The water took the call and laid it flat and handed it back.  Four seconds later the cliff returned it again, thinner.  For a moment there were two birds where there had been none, and the gap in the song was filled by the world itself. 

Voss made a sound I will not describe.  Then he opened his hand.  The egg went into the inch of water, filled, and settled into the salt like a stone into a grave.  He let it go the way he let everything go, easily, having already taken what he wanted. 

The pumps came back at minute twenty-two.  The thunder stepped closer and was combed apart again.  By morning the flat was white, the crack map still there, the men back at their brine wells.  He wired the archive enough to record a hundred more dead species, and asked for his copy in a lossless format.  I sent it.  Somewhere in the file, under the male's call, the water is still agreeing.  He will dry every lake he owns, and he will keep that echo forever, and both things are true, and neither one cancels.
