The ember nexus opened at twilight, when the city's spent fires came down the flues to die together.  It was a shallow bowl of brick in the hillside, and everything burned there without flame.  Ilric had worked in it for nine years, and had made nothing.  The guild called him hopeless, and the guild was right about gold.  He had never made a grain of it.  He felt the failure the way other men feel weather, in his teeth and his fingertips, and he never once complained.  His master had called him cold.  He was not cold; he was a man who had learned to hold everything still inside himself, the way frost holds a flower. 

In his satchel he carried what was left of his father's crozier.  The bishop's staff had come down through six generations of men who all knew exactly what they were for.  Ilric was to be the seventh.  At his investiture he had snapped the crook from the shaft across his knee, and walked out of the cathedral with the pieces, and no one had stopped him, because no one could imagine it.  Nine years later he was still trying to make the wood live.  Not gold, not the elixir, not the Work: only to vivify a dead branch that had been cut and carved and told what shape to be. 

Every attempt burned it a little more.  The shaft had gone entirely in the first trial, and he had been left with the crook and a lesson.  That was the arithmetic of the nexus: nothing new came out of anything that had not first been taken apart.  So he had learned to destroy carefully.  His ledger held four hundred and eleven trials, and after each one he halved the heat.  Char, then scorch, then a browning no deeper than a bruise.  He was walking the fire down toward the smallest ruin that could still make something, step by step, error by error. 

Tonight the crook lay in its iron mold in the coals, at a heat he could just bear to look at.  Twilight came up the hill and the embers went from orange to rose to the color of an old scar.  In his pocket lay one copper penny, stamped with a bishop's face, the last coin his father ever gave him.  He took it out now, meaning to spend it on one more night of coal.  His fingers opened. 

The penny fell, turning, and while it fell he watched the crook.  It took the coin less than a second to fall, and in that second the nine years rearranged themselves.  He had been heating it in the old curl, in the shepherd's hook, holding the wood to the shape his father's hands had wanted.  Four hundred and eleven times he had asked dead wood to become a crozier again.  That was not life.  That was obedience with a pulse. 

The penny struck the coals.  He did not hear it land, because he was already pulling the crook out of the mold he had built for it.  He broke the mold on the brick.  Two years of work, the one perfect thing he had ever made, and its pieces went into the fire and began to glow.  Then he laid the bare crook in the embers, held to no shape at all, and gave it the smallest heat he had.  Nothing burned.  The wood drank the heat and did not blacken, and along its underside a seam opened, and something green came out of the dead grain like a breath held nine years.  A shoot, then a stem, then a bud the size of a fingernail, tightening itself against the cold air above the coals.  It did not open.  He understood that it would not open tonight, and perhaps not for a long while; it was alive, and being alive meant it would decide that for itself. 

Ilric sat back on his heels with his hands full of ash and looked at the closed bloom holding still above the heat, and did not weep, and felt everything.  There was no gold in the bowl, and no elixir, and in the morning the guild would call him hopeless again, and he would let them.  Somewhere below the hill the cathedral bells rang vespers for a bishop who had never been made.  He left the penny in the coals and went down the dark road with empty hands.  Behind him the bud kept its own counsel, unopened, unfinished, free.
