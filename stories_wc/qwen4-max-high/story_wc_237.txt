The tea platform was a slab of floating rock no wider than a fishing boat, and the air on it was so soaked with unspent rain that every sound arrived wrapped in wool.  Neri knelt at the low table with her sleeves pushed back, which she never did.  Her forearms were mapped with storm-glass, the pale ridges left when a rune froze under her hand and shattered into the skin.  That was what ink did here: it crystallized when stressed, when the writer's pulse climbed, and a frightened rune became a beautiful, useless shard.  Three years a rune scholar, and Neri had made nothing but glass.  Master Odru poured the first tea, and the pour made no sound at all.  He had trimmed the tip of her crow quill that morning, blunt as a spoon, so that she could no longer draw the tiny clenched defensive runes she hid behind.  "One broad stroke," he said.  "A scar is a sentence you keep interrupting."  Below them the storm walls turned, two grey herds grazing toward each other, and between them ran the narrow lane of hush the tea masters had built the platform to find.  A breath between storms: not peace, only the pause where a thing could finally be said.  She had come to write the night she was eleven, when she tried to cut a ward for her mother's boat and the ink froze and opened her arms instead.  Everyone said the boat was lost to weather; Neri had spent nine years believing it was lost to her handwriting.  Odru laid the paper flat and set the inkstone at its head.  Her hand began its old tremor, and the first stroke went white and granular, sugar instead of meaning.  She stopped.  "I can't be calm about it," she said.  "No," Odru said.  "Then be eleven."  He meant the superstitions, and she knew it, because he had asked her once what she used to do when the sky came.  She had told him, ashamed: hold your breath between the flash and the thunder so the storm cannot count you.  Keep a crow's feather in your sleeve because crows know which way the wind will turn.  Never let the tea see your face.  Neri had shed all of that at the academy, the way you shed a milk tooth, proudly, and had been afraid ever since.  Odru slid a clean sheet under her hand and said nothing, which was the kindest thing he owned.  Now she tilted her face from the bowl so the tea could not see it, and took the blunt crow quill in her fist like a child holding a stick.  The storm walls closed to a corridor; the hush went so saturated that the wet wool of the air pressed on her ears.  Lightning stood up somewhere in the grey, a white root, and Neri held her breath.  Everything after happened inside that held breath.  The thunder had not arrived, so she was uncounted, and being uncounted she was not afraid, and being unafraid her hand went slow and wide and honest across the page.  The trimmed tip could not make small frightened lines; it laid one fat stroke for the boat, one for the water, one for the girl on the shore who was not to blame.  The ink stayed black.  It stayed black, and it moved, and the runes did what runes are supposed to do: they said the thing.  Under her sleeves the old glass let go.  She felt it shed, scale by scale, sliding out of the ridges and ticking onto the stone, and the skin beneath was pink and ordinary and hers.  Then the thunder came and counted her, and she let her breath out into the wool of the air.  Odru did not praise her.  He turned the page toward himself and read, and the platform drifted a hand's width, and the storms went past each other like strangers who had agreed not to speak.  "It is not a ward," he said at last.  "No," Neri said, gathering the shed glass into her palm, all those failed beautiful words.  "It's a story."  She tipped the shards over the edge and watched them fall into the grey, and then she drank her tea, which had gone cold and tasted of rain and of being eleven and unafraid.
