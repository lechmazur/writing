The shadow left the tower the night the queen died, and it had not stopped moving since.  Mira followed it across three provinces with a ledger under her arm.  People called it the nomadic penumbra, a half-dark that grazed.  Inside it, the missing were not ghosts but gestures: a hand tucking hair, a wrist turning a cup, shoulders shrugging off rain.  No faces, no voices, only the small motions people make when they think no one is watching.  Mira meant to be their historian.  Not of the interregnum, not of the dukes circling the empty throne, but of thumbs smoothing tablecloths and the pause before a knock.  She had earned the right strangely.  At nineteen she was pulled alive from the Grain Hall collapse, two days after the shouting stopped.  Dust had taken her voice, so the city hired her for its evacuation drills.  A crisis actor, they called her: she lay on stretchers and wept without sound while officials timed their litters.  She was the only fake victim who had ever been a real one, and she wept accurately, which unnerved everyone. 

Now the penumbra had drifted back to the capital, into the whispering gallery beneath the unfinished dome.  There a breath at one end arrives whole at the other, and the marble keeps every syllable it is given.  The Keeper met her at the curve of the wall.  Under her arm, beside the ledger, she carried the chessboard dug from the Grain Hall rubble, every pawn missing.  Kings, bishops, knights, rooks: a war of officers with nothing left to spend.  She set it on the ledge each visit, hoping the half-dark would fill the empty squares.  It never did; the penumbra gave back only motions, never matter.  Among them, tonight, she found her father's hand hovering over a square it could no longer touch.  She wrote it down: hesitation, two seconds, weight on the left foot. 

The Keeper of the anchor was benevolently ruthless, and the bereaved came to him anyway.  Each night he tethered the shadow so the grieving could visit, and each dawn he unbound it, because a shadow held still kept the dead repeating one motion forever.  He had unbound it in seven cities, and seven cities had cursed him for it.  Mira had come to ask him to stop.  She could not ask aloud; the dust had left her nothing the marble could carry.  So she used the method the gallery's deaf masons had invented: she walked words.  Her soles hissed on the marble dust, and the dome gathered the strokes of each letter and delivered them, whole, to the far ear.  She paced STAY.  Four letters, eleven turns, and the Keeper heard them arrive inside his own skull.  He answered aloud, gently.  "If I bind it here, that hand hovers over that square until the dome falls."  "It will never set the pawn down." 

Mira looked at the board, at the officers standing in their bright, useless rows.  In the rubble she had freed her wrist from a hand that was still warm, and she had lived.  She had spent nine years calling that a betrayal.  Now she watched the hand over the chessboard tremble, wanting to move, unable to finish.  Her ledger held two hundred gestures, every one written in the past tense.  She stood, and walked again.  UNBIND.  Six letters, and she took them slowly, the way her father used to cross a room. 

The Keeper heard, and bowed, and cut the last cord.  The half-dark slid out of the gallery like a tide going home.  For one moment the hand completed its move, set down something that was not there, and the empty square lit as if a pawn had just been placed on it.  Then the light went with them, and the gallery was only a room where whispers traveled.  Mira wrote: he played e4, and he was afraid of it, and he did it anyway. 

Outside, the dukes were still arguing over a crown, and the shadow was already crossing the river toward the next town that had lost somebody.  She would follow it in the morning, ledger and pawnless board, writing down what people did with their hands while the throne stayed empty.  Historians of the interregnum would name the dukes and their claims; she would name the pause before the knock.  The marble kept the shape of her walking, and whispered it back to no one until dawn.
