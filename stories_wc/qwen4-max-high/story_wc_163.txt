The reservoir had fallen forty feet by August, and the drowned city came up out of it like a held breath.  Maret walked the cracked mud to the forgotten research facility that had studied the city for thirty years and then studied its silence for thirty more.  She was a landscape astrologer: she cast charts for places, not people, and the dam authority had hired her to name the auspicious hour when the sluices would open and the valley would go under again.  Sunrise, they told her; nothing after sunrise.  Ilya met her at the door with a lamp, a man who had never stopped keeping the log.  "Water depth, temperature, birds," he said.  "Somebody has to write it down." 

Inside, the corridor smelled of iron and cold silt, and the lamp laid a bright seam along the wall where the flood had once stood.  Maret had spent her life reading such seams, the hinge where one thing became another, and she had never once stood inside a place she charted.  Under the commission lay her real errand: to learn the rhythm of belonging, how a house keeps a family, how a valley keeps a town.  In the observation gallery, four inches of water lay over the tile, and beyond the glass the city stood in the lamplight: doorframes, a mill wheel, the orchard's black stumps. 

Ilya opened the log to its first page and took out a heron's feather, half of it burnt to a curl.  "The night before the water came, everyone burned what they could not carry," he said.  "My mother pulled this out of the fire and told me the town was not gone while somebody kept a piece of it."  Maret looked at the black edge, at the exact seam where char stopped and white quill began, and felt something in her chest turn over.  Then she asked for it, softly, and laid it on the flooded floor.  A feather holds air in its barbs; it will not sink, and it will not argue with a current.  It swung toward the stairwell, hesitated, swung back, and swung again, keeping a count she could feel in her wrist. 

"The gates are already breathing," she said.  "The lake comes in and goes out through the crack, like something asleep."  Ilya crouched, and for the first time in years he watched the water instead of measuring it.  It was softly ingenious, that trick of the feather, and Ilya laughed once, a sound like a door unsticking. 

They climbed to the catwalk above the gallery to wait for the hour she had been paid to choose.  The mudflats stretched out under a sky with no moon, and the dark was so complete that the seam between water and air had disappeared.  "Hold still," Maret said.  "Breathe out with me, slow, and look low."  They let one breath go together into the cold, and the orchard answered.  Fireflies came up out of the stumps and the reeds, the first of the summer, rising along the exact streets where the lamps used to be.  It captivated her, and she was a woman who had made a career of not being held by anything.  Below her a town lit itself in the pattern of a sky; above her the real stars came out over the ridge, and she could not tell which chart she had been hired to read.  She drew it, streets and all, the first chart of her life with people inside it. 

"There," Ilya said, pointing with two fingers.  "That was our kitchen."  He said it on the exhale, and she matched him, and the light in the reeds pulsed once, as if the valley had a heartbeat that could be joined.  This was the rhythm, then: not a wall around a place, but a breath taken at the same time as someone else.  Afterward she would call it a luminous respite: four borrowed hours in which the valley was permitted to be air and light. 

At the seam of dawn the sluices opened, and the water came back up the streets with a sound like a long inhale.  She went down and set the feather on the water rising through the gallery floor, so the town could keep its own piece.  It turned once and went toward the streets.  On the road above, in the log, under depth and temperature and birds, Maret wrote the hour she had chosen: not a drowning, a return.  Then she wrote next August beside it, and her name, and Ilya held the lamp steady while she did.
