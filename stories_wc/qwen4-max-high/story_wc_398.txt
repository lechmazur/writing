The last bus pulled out of the vacant station at ten to ten, and its tail lights went down the October valley like something being swallowed.  From row twelve, Ivo watched two people stay standing on the platform.  He was the only sentinel left on the hill; the vineyard had been sold in June, the pickers were gone, and he had one week to hand over the keys.  He lifted his lantern and swung it twice, the old signal for come up, there is warmth here. 

They climbed to the amber pavilion, the harvest shelter whose panes had taken the color of the leaves pressed against them, so that everyone inside looked lit from within.  Vesna had come up from the city to marry Andrej on Saturday.  They had cancelled it on the platform below, quietly, seven years folded into one sentence, and then the bus had gone without them.  He had known Vesna since she was a picker's daughter running these rows.  They sat at opposite ends of the bench and told him nothing, which was how he learned everything.  He poured cider and set the cups down where each would have to reach past the other, and said nothing about that. 

Then he took the thing from his coat pocket.  That afternoon, cleaning out the stove he would never fire again, he had reached behind the firebox and rediscovered the edge of a love letter.  A corner of paper, brown along its burned curve, the rest forty years gone.  Marta's handwriting survived on it: I loved you in the only.  He had burned the rest at twenty-two in this stove, and for forty years he had finished the sentence the way an angry man finishes things.  "In the only way that let me leave," he used to hear. 

Vesna read it aloud, and Andrej looked at the floor, and the stove ticked.  Ivo did not tell them to stay together.  He had no interest in patching anything; he wanted, with an ache he could not have named, for two people to know what it had been like to be the other one.  So he did what he had done all his life, which was to talk about vines. 

"Come see row twelve," he said, gently, the way you move a moth off a window instead of swatting it.  Outside, the leaves hung amber in his lantern light.  He showed them a graft he had made the spring Marta left: a noble scion, a vigorous rootstock, both flawless, and the union between them a brown seam that had never healed.  "Neither one is sick," he said.  "Neither one is lying.  Some pairs are simply destined to mismatch, and the wood tells you honestly, and you can spend forty years calling it a wound instead of a fact." 

Andrej said, "So we wasted seven years."  "I kept this vine," Ivo said.  "It taught me the difference between failing and not fitting." 

Then he asked them, softly, to try one thing: not to say what they had wanted, but to say what the other had been afraid of.  Vesna said Andrej had been afraid of becoming his father, who stayed in a marriage like a man serving a sentence.  Andrej, after a long time, said Vesna had been afraid that if she stopped translating herself into his language she would disappear.  Each of them had guessed right, and being guessed right undid them both.  They did not embrace.  They sat closer on the wet step and cried in a way that had nothing to do with getting back together. 

Ivo put the burned edge into Vesna's hand, and she opened her fingers toward Andrej so he could read it too, and the two of them held the small brown curve between them like a leaf they were both responsible for.  And Ivo, watching, finished the sentence differently at last: in the only way I had, which was not the way you needed.  Forty years of blame went out of him like heat out of a stove. 

At half past five they walked down to the vacant station for the first bus, carrying the fragment, arguing gently about whose pocket it belonged in.  Ivo stayed, the last sentinel of a sold vineyard, and went along row twelve in the dark, touching the seam that had never taken, grateful for what it had taught the three of them.
