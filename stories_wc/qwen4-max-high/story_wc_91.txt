Berenice gave away her fortune in eleven years, and then her house, and then the last silver spoon, until all she owned was a crate of jars.  Inside each jar lived lightning bugs, gathered from places where one thing had become another.  A mill turned into apartments.  A hospice garden.  She labeled them in pencil, because she wanted to learn the texture of transformation, and she believed light was its residue.  Now she had come to the black sand beach below the volcano, where lava quenched into glass and the waves ground the glass into sand.  The island called this week the slow dance.  For nine nights the moon crawled toward the comet, and on the ninth it would cover the comet's head, and the fishers would read the mountain's mood by that dim light.  Berenice slept in the boat shed and ate what the market dropped, and still she gave things away: her bread, her sandals, the dry corner of her mat.  She could not stop arranging people.  It was her one remaining talent, the way she orchestrated coincidences.  She moved a bicycle so its owner would take the long path and meet the nurse who needed him.  She told the ferry captain the wrong hour, twice, until he landed at dusk beside the woman who had waited thirty years for an apology.  Her last arrangement was the largest.  On the ninth night she wanted old Teodora, who kept the island's prophecies, to stand at the point with Sef, the young geologist, so that the verses and the instruments might finally speak.  She left notes on both their doors, each promising the other would be there.  Then the mountain changed its mind about waiting.  Ash fell at midafternoon, thick as gray snow, and buried the coast road.  Sirens, then the crier, then the bells: everyone to the point, where the boats were.  But the road was gone, and the dark came early under the ash, and the village stood in the square with lamps that would not stay lit in the wind.  Berenice took the detour.  She went down to the black sand, the long way around the shoulder of the volcano, and she opened her crate.  One jar to each hand, and there were forty hands, and then more.  Bugs cannot be blown out.  So the village walked the shore in a line of small green lamps, forty jars swinging, and the sand began to whisper.  It does that, fresh black sand, when the tide pulls back: a million grains settling, a hush that whispers.  Teodora, somewhere behind her in the line, began to laugh and to cry at once.  The verse she had recited to tourists for sixty years said that in the slow dance, when the sand whispered, one who owned nothing would carry captured stars the long way, and the fire would pass the people by.  Berenice had never heard it.  She had only wanted two stubborn people in the same place, and a road had closed.  At the point, Sef was waiting with his instruments and a face full of ash, exactly where her note had put him, and the ferry captain was there too, wrong hour and all, with three boats and room for everyone.  Sef had read the tremors that morning and told the captain to load; the prophecy and the seismograph had agreed without ever meeting.  They crossed the bay while the lava came down the far shoulder and hit the sea.  Above them the moon slid over the comet's head, unhurried, the slow dance finishing on schedule.  The sound the new stone made was a hush, a vast whispering, and Berenice leaned over the gunwale to hear it.  She had one jar left, the first one, from the mill.  She had carried it through eleven years of giving.  Now she understood that she had been trying to hold the moment of change still long enough to touch it, and that the moment was not a substance but a passage, a hush you went through with other people.  She unscrewed the lid.  The bugs went up over the black water, blinking, and the child beside her said the sea had stars in it now.  Berenice owned nothing at all, and the whole bay was lit.
