The dead call at twilight, when the glass of the atrium sweats and the mist comes up through the floor grates like something remembering how to breathe.  Vesna worked that hour, because that hour was when the lines went warm.  Her title on the badge said contact tracer, and under it, in smaller letters, for hearts.  Grief is catching; that is the whole science of it.  A dead woman speaks to the people who loved her, and if they answer, they go too, and then they call their own.  So the Bureau sent tracers to map the affection, name by name, and close the lines before anyone picked up.  Vesna had taken the job for the ones who could not tell a voice from a kindness: the deaf, the very young, the lonely old who kept the receiver by the bed. 

Modern receivers filter the dead out as noise.  Vesna used her mother's hearing aid, a beige analog thing from decades back, its cord brittle, its little battery door held with tape.  Her mother had answered once, and the aid was what came back.  Held to the mouth of a speaking pipe, it caught the faint pulses beneath the hum, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. 

Tonight's file was Ada Brekke, three days dead, one photograph.  The last photograph: a booth strip, four frames, Ada laughing with a hearing aid pressed to a boy's ear.  On the back, in pencil: teaching him rain.  Vesna stood in the mist and felt the floor tilt a little, the way it always did when a chain came alive under her hands.  Ada's own aid sat in the evidence drawer downstairs, still faintly pulsing.  Two aids from the same old lot, tuned to each other long ago, so a grandmother and a grandson could hear the same television.  The twin was still out there, in the tenement wing above the atrium, on a child who could not hear well and would answer anything that sounded like her. 

Protocol said close the trunk line and log the contact as unreachable until morning.  Morning was a whole night of calling away.  Vesna was not brave; she was only unable to leave a question standing.  She wanted to know which stairwell, which door, whether the boy slept with the thing in his ear.  So she went up, holding the aid to each landing's pipe, following the pulses the way you follow a smell of burning.  Fourth floor, a door with a paper star. 

The boy was nine and sitting on the floor with his back to the radiator, and he had the aid in, and he was saying hello.  Every hello thinned him.  Vesna knelt and did the only thing her job actually was.  She took his hands and she assured him.  Assurance is not comfort; it is a closed circuit, and it has to be true, or the line stays open.  She lifted the aid from his ear and slid the battery out, and the little whine died, and then she told him the truth.  Ada was dead.  The voice had been the shape her love left in the wire, and it was gone now, and it could not call him again, and he was not going to die tonight. 

He asked if that meant he would never hear her again.  Vesna did not lie, because a lie leaves the line open.  She said yes, and then she gave him the last photograph, all four frames of it, his grandmother laughing, the aid at his ear.  "That is what she taught you," Vesna said.  "Rain."  "Listen for that instead." 

The hush afterward was the reeling kind, the kind that comes when a hum you have lived inside stops and leaves your ears ringing with your own blood.  Down in the atrium the mist stood in the twilight like a crowd that had forgotten why it came.  Vesna sat on the fourth landing with her mother's aid in her palm and waited for the floor to stop tilting.  The report would call it an intervention; someone upstairs would use the word heroism, and she would find that embarrassing.  She had not gone up to save anyone.  She had gone up because she could not stand not knowing which door.  Forty chains a month, and the saving was only what fell off the curiosity, the way filings fall off a key.  Below, the tenement began to make its ordinary sounds: a kettle, a television, a woman calling a child in to supper, and no one answering who should not.  Vesna wrote contacts: one, assured, and went down to close the drawer.
