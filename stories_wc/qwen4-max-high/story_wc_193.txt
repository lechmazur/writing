The mushroom farm grew inside a beached submarine, and Ines liked that its light needed darkness to be seen.  Blue came up through the seams of the growing racks like something stitched into the black.  The mushrooms fed on congealed abstractions: expired warranties, old grudges, the word soon.  They broke a concept down into carbon and light, and three nights a year, when concepts became physical, the whole hull glowed bright enough to read by. 

Ines had forty-one names to read.  Two years ago she had sold a ladder called Ascendancy, and she had sold it to her mother, her cousins, her choir.  The ladder fell, the way ladders do, and she survived it, which was the cruelest part.  Her shame had been thickening ever since into a grey slug in her coat pocket that weighed more every month.  Her mother's name was first on the list, in her own handwriting, from a night she had promised the moon over a kitchen table. 

The farm's real money was the press, an old ballast tank that could squeeze that carbon and that weight into diamonds.  Ines wanted forty-one stones and forty-one envelopes.  Only on Concretion nights did the pressure run deep enough, and the press had a viewport with a missing bolt.  On ordinary nights that was a drip the foreman wrapped in tape.  On Concretion nights the pressure stopped being physics and became an argument, and arguments find the weakest seam.  Nobody had taken the shift in three years. 

Her phone had been glitching since the first frost, texts arriving from Ineses in adjacent worlds.  Half the letters came through as symbols no keyboard owned.  One wrote: d0nt take the shift, i t00k it, i am a sm#ll sound in the wall now.  Another wrote: i kept the m#ney, i b0ught a house, mom speaks to me on holidays.  Every branch had an opinion, and every opinion was a version of the same promise: I will make it right.  A promise kept and broken at once is a quantum promise, and on Concretion night it condenses. 

Hers came down into her palm at midnight: a brass submarine porthole bolt, threaded, cold, wet with a sea that was not there.  Of course it was a bolt.  A bolt is the smallest thing that says the window will hold. 

She volunteered before the foreman finished asking, grinning like a girl who had already stolen something.  Ines had always been the honest kind of trouble, cheating at cards only to slip the winnings back into the loser's coat.  Tonight's trick was small and beautiful: she wrote forty-one names on the assay's ownership line instead of her own.  Whatever the press made would belong to them by law, and the foreman, who never read anything, initialed it. 

She climbed into the tank with the grey slug in one hand and the bolt in the other.  The pressure came up like a crowd remembering her name.  It found the empty hole in the viewport and began to argue.  Ines threaded the promise in and turned it until her wrist burned.  Through the little window she could see the mushrooms burning blue, patient as stars in a ceiling.  The bolt held because nobody had yet decided whether it was kept or broken. 

Her phone lit the dark with one unread message, the one that would tell her how it ended.  Reading it would collapse the promise into one thing or the other, and a broken thing cannot hold a window.  So she did the mischievous, decent thing and deleted it unread, stealing the answer from every world at once.  She had sold certainty for two years; refusing it felt like restitution. 

The tank squeezed for eleven minutes.  When the hatch cracked, blue light ran along the seams of the racks, and in the tray lay a scatter of small cloudy stones, and she counted them twice: forty-one.  Not jewels, but the assay paid by weight, and shame is the densest thing a person makes.  Her pocket was empty for the first time in two years.  She would mail them tomorrow with her name printed on every slip, which was harder than the pressure had been. 

The bolt stayed in the port, unobserved, holding.  Ines walked out through the glowing rows, and the dark stitched itself shut behind her, and she let it keep the answer.
