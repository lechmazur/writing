After rain, the crystal forecourt whispered.  Water found the old fissures in the pillars, and the stone let go of sounds it had kept for forty years.  Mira kept the night desk there, a quiet registrar in a city that had stopped needing words.  Clarity had come in her thirtieth year: a lattice under the skin that let everyone's internal weather shine plainly through the throat.  Speech had gone the way of handwriting, and the concert hall behind her had gone quiet with it.  Her job was to write down what the lights meant.  That was the trouble: the glow was ambiguously clear, bright as glass and just as unreadable, so someone had to name it, and the someone was her.  People came to the forecourt to be recorded and left certain she had shrunk them.  Lately they left tickets instead of complaints.  Stub ends from the hall's last season, slipped under her desk lamp, each one carrying a doodle.  A spiral with a bar across it.  An eye, scribbled out.  A key with no teeth.  She read them the way she read everything now, alone, and decided they were threats.  On the ninth night she did what she had never done: she put her ear to the wet crystal and eavesdropped.  Rain made the stone a listening throat; it carried two men at the far gate, muttering the way old men still muttered.  One said the ledger should be burned, because a woman who names other people's fear must be hiding her own.  The other said the old projectionist, Ivo, had been leaving her tickets all week, and she had not answered one.  Mira sat back into the hush and felt her throat go the color of a struck bell.  They were right, which was the unbearable part.  For six years she had written her own nightly line as calm, and for six years she had been afraid.  One false word, and the whole ledger had rotted around it.  She had spent six years wanting the mistrust to thaw, and had offered nothing but accuracy.  Accuracy was not transparency.  She could keep decoding the doodles alone and stay mistrusted, or she could open.  The hall's projector had been dead since the silence came, but its lens was still good, thick as a fist, ground to throw a small thing huge.  She unscrewed it, carried it out into the dripping forecourt, and set it before her lamp.  Then she held her ledger page in the beam and let the crystal wall take it, four meters high.  Every entry she had ever named, including her own six years of calm, and beneath it, in her shaking hand, the correction: afraid.  She turned her collar down so the lattice at her throat could shine into the lens unfiltered.  The wall filled with her weather, and it was not calm, and everyone who came to be recorded that night saw it.  Nobody left.  The hush had a grain to it, like a held chord.  Ivo came through the gate with a ticket in his fingers.  He did not speak; he stepped into the beam and held the stub up so the lens could throw its doodle across the wall beside her confession.  She wrote her readings under it, where he could refuse them.  The spiral with a bar across it, magnified, was not a crossed-out thing at all.  It was a spiral with a bridge: a way in.  The eye scribbled out meant looking away, out of respect.  The key with no teeth was a door he could not open for her.  Still ambiguously clear, still guesswork, but shared now, enlarged, and honest about being guesswork.  Mira wrote the night's line for him and held it in the light so he could check her.  He nodded, and the correction she had feared became the first thing anyone had ever agreed with.  By dawn the queue at the desk was longer than it had been in years, people waiting to read their own entries and argue.  Arguing was allowed now; the ledger had a margin for it.  The rain stopped, the pillars dried, and the whispers went back into the stone.  Mira kept the lens on her desk, where anyone could look through it, and wrote her line as it was: afraid, and less alone.
