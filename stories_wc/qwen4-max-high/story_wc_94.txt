Above the cloud deck the observatory waited out the storm with its dome shut, an eye refusing to look.  Vera called the waiting a vigil, and the lull between storm cells the only honest hour, the hour when masks slip.  Below her, lightning lit the clouds from inside, a page held over a lamp.  She had come up the mountain to rarefy.  Eleven years ago the Bureau had struck forty-one lines from her second book, and afterward she learned to thin a poem to its load-bearing words, the way air thins at altitude and lets you see farther on less.  Now she developed glass plates for Anselm, the astronomer, who photographed the dark rift in the Milky Way and paid her in wages, paraffin, and silence.  In her apron she carried a darkroom timer, brass, its hand sweeping sixty seconds, its bell a small struck star.  She used it to time development, and to time the pauses.  In June the supply truck had brought her a sealed envelope wedged in an old bus timetable: route 14, capital to paper mill, discontinued before she was born.  The address was written in the hand of the man who had censored her.  She had not opened it.  She kept it wedged at page fourteen, departures, the way you keep a wound clean, by not touching it.  Anselm wanted to know why she stayed when the road reopened.  "I am making a map," she said.  He assumed she meant the sky.  She meant scars: the white seam on her wrist from the mill, the burn on Anselm's palm, the censor's tremor, the black bars across her poems.  She had met him once, at a hearing, and his hand shook as he signed.  She wanted their cartography, not where it hurt but where the hurting had opened a way through.  At two the storm's mask slipped.  The wind dropped so suddenly the silence rang, the cloud thinned to gauze, and the rift showed above it: not a hole, Anselm insisted, but dust, a river of unlit matter lying between us and the light.  Anselm cranked the shutter open and bent to the eyepiece; three minutes of sky was all the night would give.  Vera set the timer for three minutes, the length of a development, the length of the pause, and opened the envelope.  There was no letter inside.  There were forty-one strips of paper, razored from her proofs, each carrying one murdered line.  He had kept them all, eleven years of excisions, and mailed them to the only address he could find for her, a mountain above the weather.  On the timetable's margin, in his shaking hand: I was told to remove the words, not to destroy them.  Something in her went furiously tranquil, the way a tray looks still while the chemistry inside it races.  She laid the strips along the rift on last week's print, black bar over black dust, and saw that a censor and a galaxy do the same work.  They put darkness in the way, and the darkness is a thing, and a thing can be charted.  Then she took a needle to last week's plate and scratched the missing words into its black emulsion, letter by letter, forty-one wounds.  She held the plate up to the open dome.  The stars came through the scratches.  Not many: a sparse, thinned, high-altitude light, rarefied to exactly what could be borne.  That was the map, not the scars but the sky they let in.  Wonder came back into her, unpermitted and exact, the way it had come before the Bureau, before she learned which words were weather and which were contraband.  The bell rang, the pause closed, and cloud rose again to cover the rift.  Anselm found her with the plate against the slit and forty-one strips spread across the timetable like departures for a line that no longer ran.  "Good exposure?" he asked.  "Three minutes," she said, and did not say eleven years.  She had waited eleven years for an apology, and what arrived instead was the words themselves, which was better.  She read them aloud into the returning storm, in the order the dust allowed.  Lightning took the poem and lit it, page by page, from underneath.
