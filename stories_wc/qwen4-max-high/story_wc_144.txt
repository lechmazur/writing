Mist came off the harbor each evening and laid itself over the rail depot like a hand over a mouth.  Inside the old signal box, Sova kept her repair shop: three benches, a swinging lamp, and the stone catch basins the railmen had cut to cool their axles.  The basins had gone still years ago, and still water, she had learned, tells the truth about light.  She had ground lenses for twenty years before a fever took her sight, and she had overseen the depot's repairs for nine years since.  Her apprentices were her eyes, and her craft was turning what they told her into what they should do.  In her ledger she wrote down what each thing was for, rather than what was wrong with it.  At seven o'clock a boy came in dripping, holding a shoebox with air holes punched in the lid.  Inside lay an artificial hummingbird drone, green as a bottle, its wings folded like closed fans.  "Channel guide," the boy said.  "The Perihelion lifts anchor at slack water, seven-fifteen, maiden voyage, and she won't cross the shoals without the bird."  "My mother pilots her."  "It went into the slip this morning and stopped singing."  "Then we have time enough," she said, which was not a fact but a gift.  Sova laid two fingers on the drone's breast and felt the small stutter of its heart.  Salt had gotten into its throat, and salt she could rinse.  But a guide stutters when it cannot see, and the crack would be in the wing lens, finer than a hair, past the reach of her thumb.  "Toma, lamp behind me."  "Wen, hold the lens an inch above the middle basin, face down, and tell me the light on the bottom."  She listened to them work: the lamp chain, a stool's scrape, the tick of water settling.  "Rings," Wen said.  "Bright net, all even."  "And under the wing?"  "The net bends, and there's a doubled line running from the leading edge back to the third rib."  Sova turned the words the way she turned a bolt, feeling for the thread.  A doubled line meant the crack ran deep enough to split light in two; the third rib meant it stopped where the wing was strongest.  "Then we patch across it, never along it," she said.  "Mica film, a hand's width, second rib to fourth, so the wing carries the strain sideways."  Toma cut the film; Wen laid it; Sova's thumbs did the rest, spreading resin warm as honey, counting the pressure aloud so the boys would learn the number of it.  The lamp's heat would cure the patch in four minutes.  Under the lamp the resin set, and Wen, unprompted, held the wing over the basin again.  "It's still there," he said, and his voice went thin.  "The doubled line."  Five minutes to slack water; the boy's breathing filled the shop.  Sova did not hurry; hurrying was a way of going blind twice.  "Tilt the lens," she said.  "Does the line slide, or does it sit?"  A pause, the small slop of water, then: "It slides."  "Then it is the edge of our patch, where mica meets glass, not a wound," she said.  "A scar bends light too."  "Learn the difference and you can oversee this room with your eyes shut."  She rinsed the salt from its throat with distilled water, dried it against her wrist, and woke it.  The wings opened; the note came up out of the benches, out of the floor, out of the boys' chests, a hum so fine it seemed to be thinking.  It went out the open door in a green line, and the boy ran after it, shoebox forgotten.  Sova stood in the doorway with the mist cooling her face and listened to the harbor take the sound.  Far out, iron dragged over iron, link by link: an anchor coming up off the bottom for the first time.  Then a ship's bell, and the long sigh of a hull beginning to move where no hull had ever moved before.  Wen came to the doorway beside her, and because she had taught him, he offered the report unasked.  "The bird's light is on the water ahead of her bow, one line, steady, not doubled."  "Even, then," Sova said.  "Write it in the ledger: channel guide, patched across the crack, returned to the pilot who trusts it."  She went back inside to wait for the next thing that could not say where it hurt.
