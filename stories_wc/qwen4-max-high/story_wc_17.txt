After the climate shifts, the city's lower corridors went under for nine months of the year, and the guild learned to map by ear.  Salt killed the street trees; the ivy came instead, and it went indoors.  Mira was the last corridor echo cartographer in Vantage, which meant she was also the last person obligated to finish her mother's atlas.  The work was simple to describe and impossible to teach: strike a wall, listen to the return, draw the hollow behind it.  Mira had been born sensitively perceptive, the guild's polite phrase for a child who flinched at footsteps three floors down.  Her mother had been the same, and it had eaten her.  The guild paid her a stipend that was really a leash, and every apprentice she trained asked, first, whether she was as good as her mother.  What Mira wanted was not the atlas.  She wanted to know what walls dream about.  Every cartographer knew the slack hour, when the tide stalled and the temperature inside a building matched the air outside, and the plaster began to give back sounds nobody had made that day.  Her mother had called it dreaming, in the one letter she left, and then had refused to explain.  The explanation came instead through half-burned diaries found in a fireplace, in the family house on Candle Row, where Mira went to sell the stove.  Her mother had fed the notebooks to the fire in her last winter, and the flue had choked on them, and the damp had put the fire out.  Whole pages survived, edges brown, ink still legible.  They said the walls of the drowned arcade repeated her mother's own tapping back to her, night after night, in her exact rhythm, including the mistakes.  They said she had stopped being able to tell which of them was doing the work.  The last readable line was a warning that sounded like an invitation: do not answer it.  Tucked into the spine of the least burned notebook was a film negative strip, six frames, the only survey her mother had trusted to something water could not ruin.  Mira held it against the window and the world inverted: black sky, white ivy, and an ivy-veiled passage running behind the arcade's east wall where every official map, her mother's included, showed solid stone.  In the fourth frame there was half a shadow that might have been a woman leaning toward the wall, ear pressed to the plaster.  Mira went at slack hour, with her hammer and her mother's strip curled in her pocket like a sleeping insect.  The ivy had grown through the arcade's ribs and held the ceiling up out of pure habit; water stood knee-deep and warm as blood.  She found the seam by touch, then struck it once.  The echo came back wrong, too long, a corridor where the atlas promised nothing, and she drew it, hand shaking, because drawing was the only prayer she had.  Then she waited.  At the turn of the tide the wall began to tap.  Three quick, one slow, a hesitation, three quick again: her mother's stutter on the fourth beat, the flaw the guild had copied for thirty years without knowing it was a woman's tired wrist.  Mira understood then what walls dream about.  They dream about being listened to.  They keep the one person who ever pressed an ear to them and asked what was inside, and they repeat her, faithfully, forever, because it is the only conversation they know.  Her mother had not been haunted.  She had been quoted.  The burden was not the unfinished atlas; it was the fear that she was only her mother's echo, tapping the same flawed beat because it had been put in her hands before she could choose.  Do not answer it, her mother had written, because answering means agreeing to be repeated.  Mira lifted the hammer and struck something new: two slow, a pause long enough to be a question, then a run of five.  The passage took it, faltered, gave back her mother's rhythm, faltered again, and offered hers.  She worked the seam open a hand's width, and through the ivy caught a glimpse of the passage beyond: a step up, dry floored, going somewhere no atlas had ever bothered to want.  She wrote it down, both rhythms, hers and her mother's, and labeled them clearly, so that whoever came next would know which was inheritance and which was choice.  Then she stepped into the passage and let the wall learn her.
