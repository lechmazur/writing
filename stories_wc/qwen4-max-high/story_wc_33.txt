The cobalt arcade held its breath at twilight, forty shops of blue glass gone dark except the tobacconist's, and Emil Vray walked its length with his cap in his hand.  One mail drop a day, at dusk, by agreement of two governments that each called the strip theirs and neither called it a country.  Vray was the postman of the disputed territory, licensed by nobody and tolerated by everybody.  He bowed to the sergeants at both ends, learned their children's names, and carried what they had forbidden.  Respect was the toll; rebellion was the freight.  Nobody spoke above a whisper here; the glass carried sound, and sound carried across the seam. 

Under his coat, wrapped in a scarf, a bottle knocked softly against his ribs.  Cobalt glass, thumb-thick, and inside it a small grey weather turning over and over: a thunderstorm, bottled on the ridge by a glassblower who had died in the spring.  Her last letter was in his sack, addressed to a house that had been rubble since March.  He had not stamped it dead.  He did not stamp anything dead. 

The border here was not wire but drones, and Vray had mapped it the way he mapped everything: by linking absences.  A lamp that never lit in the fourth arch.  A dog that had stopped barking at the north gate.  Eleven names gone from his round in one week.  And a hum overhead that failed for eighty seconds every evening above the shuttered kiosk, always at the same minute.  The lamp had been unscrewed; the dog had been given away because dogs bark at rotors; the silence was weather that arrived on schedule.  Absences, laid end to end, made a corridor. 

The smugglers flew at the drop, when both sergeants had their backs to the arches; the tobacconist's boy ran a quadcopter with a fishing line and a hook, carrying cigarettes and insulin.  They grounded the patrol with weather: uncork a bottled storm, and for a minute every rotor on the seam sat down in the rain.  Vray had paid three months of stamps for the bottle and the boy's silence, and had never carried anything for himself. 

At the kiosk, on an upturned mail crate, a girl was doing sums by the light of the tobacconist's window.  Ana had been nine when her mother crossed the seam for work and promised to send for her by the first thaw.  Three thaws had come.  Vray had fostered her since the second one, quietly, without papers, the way he fostered the letters nobody would carry: feeding them, keeping them warm, refusing to let them be declared gone.  He had asked everyone on his round where broken promises went to heal, and the answers had been useless: the church, the river, the capital, the grave. 

Ana held a letter she had rewritten forty-one times.  It did not ask her mother to come back; it said she had grown two inches and could hold her breath for a minute and was not angry, which was the hardest sentence in it.  Crossing mail was the one thing the two governments agreed on, and the sergeants would take it at the box. 

The exchange shutter rose for its one minute of the day.  Vray laid out the sanctioned stack, bowed, said good evening, Sergeant, and meant it.  Then he walked to the fourth arch, where the lamp never lit, and uncorked the storm.  It came out small and furious, a black fist of cloud the size of a wardrobe, and the arcade filled with rain and a crack of thunder that shook the blue glass in its lead.  Above the kiosk the hum stopped.  Eighty seconds of hush, brittle as the tiles.  The boy's quadcopter climbed through the rain with Ana's letter on the hook and went over the seam. 

Vray did not hide the empty bottle, and the sergeant, soaked, looked at it and wrote nothing down.  That was the whole rebellion: to be seen, and to be let alone.  The cloud thinned to nothing; the drones came back one by one, blind and obedient. 

Ana asked whether the letter would get there.  Vray said he did not know, and that he would carry the next one, and the one after, until the seam wore out or he did.  He had wanted a place on a map where broken promises healed.  It was not the church or the river.  It was a crate under a cobalt arcade, a girl doing sums, a promise still being written down by someone who refused to call it dead.
