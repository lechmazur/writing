The hush came down from the ice field above the soundless sanctuary, and it came the way glaciers come, an inch a year, grinding every sound to flour.  Oren had written under it for thirty-one years.  He was the clock scribe, which meant he did not ring hours but recorded them: a stroke of ink for each one the valley failed to hear.  Speech was permitted and nobody used it.  Oren preferred the ledger to any face; ink never asked him to explain himself. 

In the margins of the eleventh year he had begun to draw a bell.  Not a diagram, at first, only a curve, the way a man worried a loose tooth.  Then a crown, a waist, a lip; then the alloy, seven parts copper to three of tin, his father's ratio, smelling of wet sand and hot molds.  He meant to cast one bell and hang it in the forecourt, so the hush would have something to break against.  He tuned it on paper to his mother's laugh, a low note with a crack in the middle of it. 

The foundry in the valley closed in his forty-third year.  He told himself the furnace had gone cold, which was true, and that a man of his temperament could not have argued with founders anyway, which was truer.  He folded the drawings into the archive and wrote hours.  His hand grew careful over the years, the pen barely denting the page, as if a louder stroke might wake something. 

Then, in the last week of autumn, it rained all night on the sanctuary's forecourt.  The court was paved with slabs of pale crystal quarried from under the ice, and the old scribes had chosen it for a reason nobody had written down.  Oren went out in the hush of a hidden sunrise, when the light climbed the ridge but the sun itself stayed behind it, so the valley brightened without any source it could name.  He carried the drawings under his coat.  The wet stone smelled of his father's yard: rain on hot sand, the green mineral breath of a mold just opened. 

And the forecourt was whispering.  Not metaphor; the crystal held what fell on it and gave it back slowly, hours later, so that last night's rain was arriving now in a thousand small syllables.  The hush could grind a shout to nothing, but it could not catch anything that arrived so slowly.  He stood in the middle of it and heard, threaded through the whisper, a low note with a crack in the middle.  The court had been ringing his bell for four hundred years, patiently, whenever the weather let it.  He had only never come out after rain.  Rain was the one hour he had always handed to a novice, and he had never once asked why. 

Something in him wanted to argue: that a whisper was not a bell, that a thing struck by nobody could not be called made, that his eleven years of margins deserved a furnace and a rope and a sound the whole valley would stop for.  He let the argument stand a while, the way you let a dog bark itself out. 

Then he knelt and laid the drawings on the wet crystal, face up.  The rain still in the seams climbed into the paper.  The crown went first, then the waist, then his father's ratio, the ink loosening and spreading until the bell was only a gray weather on the page.  It was excruciatingly gentle, that unmaking; nothing tore, nothing was taken by force, and he had to bear every second of it with his eyes open.  That was the part he had not planned for: not the loss, but the kindness of it.  The whisper kept on underneath, unhurried, glacier-slow, saying the note he had spent eleven years trying to deserve. 

When the paper was blank he folded it, because a man who has kept a ledger for thirty-one years folds things.  He put it back in his coat.  Inside, the hour turned.  Oren sat at his desk and made his stroke, and then, in the margin where a bell had lived, he wrote four words he would never say aloud.  It rang without me.  The hush took the words the way it took everything, slowly, and kept them.
