The last wish was granted on a Tuesday, and by Friday the cove had nothing left to ask for.  Marek Vell had granted it himself: a fisherman's request that his boat never rot, sealed into an amber bottle whose neck fused shut the instant the words left his mouth.  That was the law of the work: a spent wish closes forever.  He deserted the Wishing House that night, leaving the queue of petitioners on the stairs, and did not come back for eleven years.  Without wishes the town had no reason to exist, because it had never learned to make anything else.  The lantern-makers left, the rope-walks rotted, and paper-lantern cove became a shelf of empty houses above a black beach. 

He came back at forty-six with a bad knee, a satchel of chalk, and the bottle.  The bottle had no opening; he had tried a diamond saw once, in a rented room, and the glass had not even scratched.  You could only look through it, and the world on the other side came out amber and slightly bent. 

The first to return was Sela, who had been a girl at the last wishing and now had a daughter and a debt.  She asked him to relight the lanterns.  He said no.  Light with no work behind it would be a lie, he told her, and a lie would keep them waiting another eleven years.  She called him cruel, and he let her, because he had stopped believing in kindness that cost nothing. 

His alchemy had never lived in the furnace.  It lived in the small lit room behind the eyes, where a person can hold perhaps seven things at once and no more.  Wishing had been easy because the bottle held the pattern for you.  With the bottle sealed, the only crucible left was working memory, and it was tiny, and it forgot.  So he taught them to hold. 

Sela stood on the cracked quay with chalk in her fist and held four things: her daughter's cough, the tide tables, the smell of the old rope-walk, the number of empty houses.  She dropped one, caught it, dropped another, and swore at him for making her do arithmetic in the wind.  On the ninth morning she conceived it whole: a rope-walk run on tide-hours, cordage sold to the fleet at Harrow, her daughter in the dry loft where the hemp dust could not reach her.  Nothing had been granted.  Something had been conceived, which is a different verb. 

Others came, because a working rope-walk needs a smith, and a smith needs bread, and bread needs a miller who has been waiting eleven years for a reason.  Marek made each of them stand on the quay and hold their seven things until the pattern stayed.  Some wept and went away.  He did not soften it.  Comfort was what the wishes had sold, the promise that wanting was itself a kind of labor. 

He had not deserted to punish them.  He had deserted because he wanted to reimagine an entire civilization: not a town that asked, but a town that held, and made, and remembered on purpose.  That was too large for one mind's little room, so it had to be distributed: seven things per person, a quay full of people, and chalk. 

In the second spring they hung the paper lanterns again.  Marek objected out of habit.  Sela told him the light was not a wish now; it was so the rope-walk could run a second shift.  He stood a long time with that, holding it, and did not drop it.  Then he wrote on the nearest lantern the four things he could keep: the knee, the chalk, the cove, the bottle.  He set the amber bottle in the empty socket of the harbor lamp, where its sealed glass threw a bent gold light across the water.  Nothing could be poured out of it.  Everything could be seen through it.  That, he thought, was the whole of the new civilization, and it fit in the small lit room.
