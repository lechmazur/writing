The geese paused over Vessel Square, a thousand wings holding one hesitation above the dry basins, then went south without landing.  In the city of forgotten fountains nobody looked up anymore; the Quiet Ordinance had taught them to keep their eyes down and their voices under a whisper.  Ilsa looked up, being paid to inspect lamps, and being constitutionally unable to believe what she was told.  They called her the luminous skeptic: a faint blue light lived under her skin, left from a childhood mouthful of recall water, and she insisted it was only phosphorus.  She doubted the water, the ordinance, and the promise that anything of her mother still lay sealed in the mains.  She doubted, and she worked. 

Her mother had been a glazier, and glaziers leave crumbs.  Offcuts no bigger than barley grains, pressed into the pavement joints along the buried pipe: a private notation forty fountains long that the sweepers never noticed and the geese would never eat.  Ilsa followed those breadcrumbs on her knees, by felt-shaded lantern, down to the low quarter where the master sluice slept under a stone lid.  In her coat she carried her mother's last piece, a stained glass box the size of a loaf: blue, amber, one pane of plain white, the lid leaded shut.  Through the white pane she could see the brass key, sealed in by her mother so that nobody could reach it in a temper. 

The city said the memories in the pipes had gone stale and dangerous, that a man who drank would remember strangers' dead and lie down in the road.  Ilsa suspected grief had simply stopped the trams once for nine days, and the trams had won.  The wardens still walked their rounds in felt-soled shoes, listening for the sound of anyone remembering out loud.  Either way something was trapped down there, and she meant to liberate it, not because she believed it would heal anyone but because a locked thing was an insult. 

First she tried force, quietly, felt around the pry bar, and a hairline crack ran through the amber like a warning.  She stopped and did what her mind was good at, which was imagining only what could actually be true.  She could see lead creeping under its own weight, the way old windows sag, a metal that flows a millimeter in a season; she could see the sluice spindle furred with lime and the exact plane where a hard wrench would snap it.  Her mother had called this sidereal patience: waiting on star time, not clock time, because the stars keep a different appointment.  Vega cleared the campanile four minutes earlier each night, and that drift, not the calendar, measured a thing slowly letting go. 

So she stood the box on end, letting gravity draw the lead out of the seam, and carried vinegar and a mallet wrapped in three layers of felt down to the sluice.  One drop, one tap, one night.  The ordinance forbade noise above a murmur, so she made a virtue of it, working in a hush so complete it amounted to defiance.  Forty-one nights, ninety, a hundred and sixty, each begun four minutes earlier than the last.  She told the spindle, in a low voice, that she did not believe in it and would outwait it anyway. 

When the geese came back north and paused again over the square, the lead had crept a finger's width clear of the seam.  The lid lifted without a sound, and the key came out cold and green at the edges.  That night the spindle unstuck, not with a crack but a sigh: a quarter turn, then another, and the stone lid rose on water that had held its breath eleven years. 

It climbed the pipe and came out of forty forgotten mouths, and the city came down in its nightclothes and knelt at the basins, whispering.  Two wardens knelt with the rest, their felt shoes dark at the toes.  Ilsa drank from the lion's mouth in Vessel Square and got her mother's hands: the felt-wrapped mallet, the crumbs of glass, a voice saying, wait, it is moving, you cannot see it move.  Her skin lit the water blue, and she still called it phosphorus, and she drank again.  Over the roofs the last stragglers went north, and nobody in the square spoke louder than the water.  Ilsa carried the empty box home and stood it on end out of habit, to see what gravity would do next.
