The arch at the north edge of Kettle Reach was older than the town, and under it the aurora came down low enough to touch.  Green light pooled in the keystone like water that had forgotten how to fall.  Inside the meetinghouse hung a calendar nobody filled in, twelve empty boxes for a year that refused to be named.  People measured time instead by what fell through the arch: meteors, and the fine grey residue they left behind. 

Vera called it remembered stardust, because it kept whatever it landed on.  A pinch on a windowsill would replay the argument that had happened beneath it, in the voices of people long dead.  Vera was the town's dreamer, moonstruck since the fever year, and she heard what nobody said.  Swallowed sentences came to her whole: the apology a man practiced at his wife's door and never delivered, the song a girl hummed only inside her teeth. 

Then the dust grew greedy.  It stopped merely keeping the unsaid and began taking it, and the town went quiet in a way that frightened everyone but Vera, who could still hear the noise inside the silence.  Cobblers set down their knives.  Her mother, who had painted the meetinghouse ceiling, stood before a blank canvas until her hands shook, then washed her brushes and did not say why.  Vera heard the why plainly: "I am afraid the next one will be the last good thing in me."  The dust had it now, packed hard into the mortar of the arch. 

Her father had left her his astrolabe, a brass thing worn soft at the edges by three generations of thumbs.  She found the trick by accident, on a night when she was too tired to be careful.  A star fell through the arch, and she followed it with the alidade, scratching its path onto the plate.  Where the line closed, the dust let go.  A traced fall gave back what it crossed: a stored quarrel, a lullaby, the whole warm weight of something a person had never managed to say. 

So she worked.  Across the unwritten calendar she stood under the arch each night, sleepless, tracing every fall, returning the town's stolen making to its owners.  The cobbler cut leather again.  A boy sang in the street and did not know where the tune had come from.  Each time she laughed, a gladness with a shadow inside it, because she gave everything back and kept nothing.  She had not slept in so long that the aurora had begun to look like the inside of her own skull. 

Her mother's canvas stayed blank.  The fear in the mortar was the hardest knot, and every path she traced bent away from it, as if the sky itself would not go there.  Then, on a night with no number, a star fell slowly, straight down through the arch, and stopped in the air above her.  The astrolabe grew warm.  She heard, in her own voice, the sentence she had never let anyone hear, not even herself: "I want to make something, and I am afraid that I am only a window."  The dust had taken that too, years ago, and had built the whole quiet town on top of it. 

She lifted the alidade and could not move her arm.  To trace this fall she would have to stand outside herself, and she was too tired to be anywhere but inside.  So Vera did the one thing she had never done.  She lay down in the snow beneath the arch, set the brass instrument on her chest, and rested. 

The star came down the rest of the way without her hand to guide it, and drew its own line across the plate.  She felt the knot open.  Everything the dust had hoarded went up through her and out, unpainted pictures and unsung verses, her mother's fear and her own, and none of it hurt; it was like being emptied of light.  She slept while it happened, which was the mercy. 

In the morning her mother was painting, badly, happily, weeping.  Vera did not go to the meetinghouse to write a date in the empty box.  She took the astrolabe, still warm, and scratched a line on the plate that no star had made, and it was hers.
