The cobalt observatory kept its cold the way other houses kept silver.  Autumn had stripped the orchard below, and the blue tiles threw that emptiness back at anyone who spoke in the dome.  You did not hear your voice returned; you heard the shape of the hole where it should have been.  The astronomers called it the echo of nothing, and they had stopped speaking above a whisper. 

Maret stitched galaxies in the meridian room, where nobody minded a fool.  Her charts were linen, not glass, and the colonel had approved them himself: pretty work, women's work, harmless.  Glass plates could be revised.  Three plates had already left the archive, and certain stars had left with them. 

Maret hummed, dropped her needle, asked the colonel whether stars were sewn on with knots or with glue.  He laughed and told his aide, in front of her, that the granary gates would be the whole business, and that the clock would say so.  A fool is a room people furnish freely. 

But she could not accuse anyone with a sentence like that.  So she did what her needle did.  A stitch cannot leap the cloth; it can only enter the next hole, the adjacent one.  She worked outward from what she had heard into the possibilities that touched it.  Coup at the bell.  Coup at the grain convoy.  Coup when the levy was signed.  Three panels, side by side, each one a neighbor of the last, each one waiting for a fact to kill it. 

The laundress killed the first: the bell had been taken down for recasting on Tuesday.  The stable boy killed the third: the levy was already signed, and nothing had happened.  She unpicked those two panels with her thumbnail, the way she unpicked any bad seam.  That left the convoy, and the stopped clock that would swear the astronomers had been watching the wrong sky at the wrong hour.  Then the granaries would open, and the astronomers would be shot for signaling the enemy. 

She needed the hour to be true somewhere else.  In the crypt below the dome stood the eternal candle, lit at the founding, fed wax by each astronomer in turn, never once allowed to die.  Its shaft was ringed in brass, hour by hour, and it had told the observatory's time for two hundred years before anyone bought a clock. 

Maret went down with her taper, as she did every evening, because blue lamplight lies about thread and only a true flame shows cobalt from black.  She lit the taper, and then, mumbling about her poor eyes and her muddled errands, she went through the whole house setting every watch and every kitchen clock to the burning rings.  The cook scolded her.  The aide laughed at her.  Eleven clocks agreed with a candle, and no one thought it worth stopping. 

Then she went back up and did the thing she had come to the observatory for, thirty years ago, when they let her stay because she was quiet and odd.  She listened for the stories nobody had thought to hear.  The laundress, the stable boy, the cook, the girl who carried milk down the hill: each had a minute and a word, and Maret sewed them into the chart as stars.  Brightness for how sure they were, position for the hour, and the old constellations drawn over them so the colonel would see only decoration.  Anyone who later read the linen against the candle's rings would find eleven witnesses saying the same hour, and a clock that lied alone. 

The convoy would come at dawn; the colonel had said so twice, laughing, to a woman who could not count her own stitches.  Maret folded the chart into the milk girl's basket, under the cloth, and gave her the taper in a lantern to see by.  "Keep it burning," she said, "it is only an old woman's whim." 

The girl went down through the wet grass with the basket, and above her the astronomers stood at their instruments and said nothing at all.  The hall clock had already been stopped at nine, quietly, by a man who thought no one kept other time. 

Below, in the crypt, Maret sat by the eternal candle and threaded a needle in its light.  She had one more possibility to reach, and she could only get there one hole at a time.  Somewhere down the hill a girl was carrying eleven unheard stories and a flame the coup had forgotten to blow out.  Maret listened to the silence above her, and heard it echo, and kept stitching toward the morning.
