When the eclipse ended, the sun came back over the ridges and found Sela's wrists.  She pulled her sleeves down and kept working.  For three minutes of darkness she had crossed the whole encampment unveiled, and the freedom still hummed in her like a struck bowl.  Now the burning came back, and with it the old arithmetic: shade, distance, minutes.  The camp lay in grooves the wind had cut through soft stone, tents strung between fluted walls, every corridor a throat that whistled. 

Her brother had kept the camp's ledger, and he lay beside the well with his throat opened and the ledger gone.  Sela did not weep and did not shout; anger keeps better cold.  The water claims were written in disappearing ink, which sunlight eats, so that no raider could steal a right by stealing a page.  Once a season, in the only dark the day ever offered, the keeper brought the book out and re-inked every claim while the camp watched.  In the dark the thief had knocked the bottle over, and the spill lay on the stone in a black fan, still wet, still legible.  It was already going. 

She sent the hummingbird up.  Her brother had built it from a survey drone, no bigger than a palm, wings beating eighty times a second, a violet lamp beneath its chin.  He had built it to answer the question she had carried for nine years: why does time slow in a crisis?  Her daughter had fallen from the cistern wall, and the falling had taken a long bright minute in Sela's memory and less than two seconds in the world.  She had run and arrived late, and she wanted to know where those minutes had gone.  The drone saw four thousand frames a second; it had been hovering over the well when the knife came out, and she had not yet dared to look. 

Under the lamp the fading letters stood up out of the stone, blue and furious.  Others saw a mess; Sela, who tracked strays and thieves for a fee of water, saw spoor.  She was wild on purpose, sleeping outside the walls, eating what the wind offered, refusing every softness that dulls a nose.  Wildness was a discipline, and it was why she could read a spill.  Ink thins where a hand has dragged through it, and thin ink vanishes first.  She read the order of its going: a comma gone gray, a streak gone ghost, a word leaving in the time it took to breathe.  The vanishing ran east, finger by finger, and east was where the man had gone.  The last word to leave the stone was a name: Doran, who sold the camp its rope.  He had forged claims for three seasons, and her brother had found the seam in them. 

Sela went east along the wind-cut corridor, veil down, the hummingbird whining ahead of her with its little violet sun.  She found Doran in a dead-end groove, holding the ledger open to the returning light so the pages would blank themselves.  He saw her white hair and her pale eyes and stopped, because everyone knew what she was and what she had lost. 

She thumbed the drone's memory onto her wrist glass and watched her brother die at four thousand frames a second.  The knife came out of Doran's sleeve at the speed of any ordinary knife.  Nothing stretched.  What had stretched at the cistern was her attention, widening until it held everything and could do nothing with it.  The machine could give her all the time she wanted now, afterward, and none of it could be spent. 

Doran said her brother's name, asking for mercy inside it.  Sela took the ledger, spread her veil over the other pages, and held his claim alone in the sun.  His forged water went gray, then ghost, then nothing.  She handed the book back and told him to walk east.  In a camp that lives by its ledger, a man with no page was never there at all.  He went into the whistling corridors and disappeared, and by evening the camp had no word for him.  The council would have the drone's memory, which no sunlight could eat. 

Sela sat in the shade with the hummingbird asleep in her palm, her anger cold and useful, and watched the stone where the ink had been.  The pattern was gone; the reading of it was hers, and she would keep it.
