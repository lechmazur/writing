After the blackout, the dusk came in thick and gold, like syrup poured over the terraces.  From the toll road I could see the lightning collection tower above the orchards, its copper crown still drinking the dry storms of late summer and keeping what it caught in glass jars.  Everything below it was dark, and everything below it was blooming, and nobody had time to notice both. 

The drone hives had failed in the third week.  They had homed on the tower's beacon hum instead of the sun, so when the grid died they drifted in perfect rows above the apricots and dropped like burnt sugar.  Now we pollinated by hand, twelve thousand blossoms a day, brush and breath and a crick in the neck.  Each evening I climbed the toll road to buy an hour of light for my mother's sewing. 

Mira kept the gate, and Mira could not lie.  A ground-strike had gone through her at nineteen, in at the boot and out at the crown, and left her unable to say a thing that was not so.  The tower company called it candor and paid her a hazard bonus. 

On her table sat a lacquer box, black, with gold apricot blossoms drifting across the lid.  She never opened it while anyone watched.  The toll was two coppers, or one answer.  She would slide a matchbox across the table, and inside, on the paper liner, a riddle in her small upright hand.  Answer it and the gate opened free. 

I asked her once why she bothered, when the company would take coppers and the city had no patience left.  "Because a riddle is the only door I have," she said.  "I cannot say what is not true, so I cannot invent anything for you."  "A question is honest and still keeps its secret."  "If I stopped asking, this road would be nothing but a road." 

All that month the riddles circled one thing.  "What wears gold, spends dust, and never once needed a wire?"  I guessed a moth, a rumor, the sun.  She shook her head each time, and each time her thumb went to the lacquer lid, and she said nothing, which for her was a kind of holding. 

Then the engineers came up the road with their clipboard and their questions.  They wanted to know whether any natural stock survived, so they could template the next hive from it.  I was buying my hour of light, standing right there.  Mira slid a matchbox across to the foreman.  "Answer it and I will tell you everything I know," she said, and that was true, and it was also a wall.  The foreman read it, laughed, and wrote on his clipboard that nothing living remained.  They went down the hill to build another beacon. 

I stayed.  The dusk had gone the color of nectar, and the orchards smelled of warm sugar and scorched wire.  I thought about gold that was not money, and dust that was not dirt, and a thing that had never needed the tower at all.  "A bee," I said.  "A real one." 

Mira's face did something I had never seen it do.  She opened the lacquer box.  Inside, on cotton, a queen stood in a cage of woven grass, and around her a knot of workers moved like a slow brown fist. 

"They fumigated the last wild hive the spring the drones arrived," she said.  "I carried her out in my shirt, and I have fed her sugar water since the lights went out."  "I have been waiting for somebody to guess." 

"Why not just say?" 

"Because they would take her and print ten thousand copies," she said.  "And because a fact handed over is spent, but a thing you guess yourself stays alive in you." 

She put the box in my hands, and it was warm, and lighter than a purse of coins.  "The high terrace was never sprayed," she said.  "Open her there." 

The gate swung, and I went down through the terraces with the box against my ribs, the way she had carried it.  At the high terrace I opened the cage among the apricots, and the workers went up into the branches with no light at all to steer by, hunting sugar in the dark. 

I still climb the toll road every evening, and Mira still slides the matchbox across, and I still know the answer at once.  But I have learned to stand a while in that thick gold light with my mouth shut, holding the wonder, before I say it out loud.
