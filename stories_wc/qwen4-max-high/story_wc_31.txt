On the night of the lantern release, Emil worked the late shift, lighting the pier lamps behind the crowd.  His son Teo waited on the last bollard with a paper lantern in his lap.  Teo had been deaf since birth, and in the spring he had asked what color his name was when his father said it.  Emil had answered with facts, because facts were where he hid.  A name was only air, he said; air had no color; sound was pressure and nothing more.  Every word was true, and none of it was honest. 

In that harbor, vows used to burn.  A promise, once made, kindled a small flame in the maker's cupped hands, and it burned as long as the promise was kept.  The town wrote its vows in the ledger of the library aboard the Hotel Aster, a moored liner, until the night she went down with three hundred pages still glowing.  After that people wrote promises on lanterns and let them go, which cost nothing and could not be checked. 

Emil's mother had been the Aster's night porter, and all he had of her was a brass skeleton key tag stamped ASTER 000, the key lost with the ship.  She had told him the library shelved its vows by cabin number, the way a hotel shelves its guests. 

The release was always timed to the year's lowest ebb, so the lanterns would carry out past the bar.  Tonight the water drew back farther than Emil had ever seen, and the drowned ship's hatch stood above it like a knuckle.  He had been waiting for this tide since the spring.  He told Teo to count the lanterns and not move, took the spare lamp from its hook, and went down the ladder. 

Inside, the library was a black room of shelves, and the sea moved in it slowly, breathing.  An air pocket held under the deck beams, twenty years old, tasting of iron and wet paper.  You cannot search a dark room by aiming at it.  His mother had taught him to wander purposefully, the way a lamplighter walks his round: a fixed route, an unhurried pace, the eyes left free to adjust until the room gives up what it holds.  He walked the aisles in a slow spiral, lamp low, reading the brass numbers on the shelf ends.  Two hundred, one hundred, ninety, the numbers falling as he came inward, the water shushing around his boots. 

Shelf 000 was a drawer beneath the lectern where the great ledger lay chained.  The tag had never been a keepsake; it was an address.  In the drawer lay his mother's own page, and it was still burning.  A flame the size of a thumbnail, blue as the inside of a wave, alive under twenty years of harbor.  He had spent his life certain her vow was "I will come back," and certain the dark had proved her a liar.  The page said otherwise: "I will keep a light for whoever comes last."  He was the last shift, the one who came after everybody; she had kept it through him, and the keeping was the color.  Sound had no color in the air; it took its color from what a person meant to do about it. 

Above him the sky was filling.  He climbed out into the hush the town fell into at the release, three hundred faces tilted up, nobody speaking.  The lanterns went away over the water like meteors climbing, and the quiet was the quiet after one falls.  Teo stood where he had been told to stand, his lantern unlit, watching his father come up out of the sea. 

Emil could have told him the truth: that the ledger was ruined, that the tide would cover the hatch within the hour, that a name was only air.  Instead he knelt, set the lamp on the boards, and began to signify with hands that knew only the harbor's crude words for fish and weather and rope.  He spelled the boy's name, then made the sign for will, then promised, slowly, to learn every word Teo had.  It was not a fact; there was nothing true about it yet.  Light came up between his fingers, green as a lamp seen through harbor water.  The crowd kept its eyes on the sky.  Teo looked a long moment, then signed the word for green, and the small sound he made was that color too.  Emil lit the paper lantern from his own hands and they let it go, and kept the rest.
