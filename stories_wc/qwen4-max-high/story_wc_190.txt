The terrace drifted a hand's width every hour, and Marta Vey, its gondola operator, measured its mood in decibels.  She had run the gondola between the prism terraces for nineteen years, and her mind worked like the air she rode: fluidly logical, taking the shape of the channel it found.  Below her cabin the valley floor held the old rail line, and every evening the freight train whistled at the crossing.  She had learned to do her real work in the quiet that follows a train whistle's echo.  Only then, when the sound had drained out of the cliffs, could she trust the needle on her sound meter.  The prism sang under stress.  A terrace holding steady hummed at thirty decibels, a sound like a held breath; a terrace about to shear climbed past sixty, and the glass began to shed.  Monitoring noise levels was not a rule the company had given her; it was the only language the glass spoke.  Hung beside the meter was a storm glass vial, camphor and salt sealed in a tube no longer than her thumb.  The whistle's vibration catalyzed the sleeping solution; in the hush after the echo, crystals bloomed along the vial's wall, and their shape told her what the pressure was doing.  Feathers meant shear.  Tonight the feathers came fast and sharp, like frost on a nail.  The needle sat near sixty and would not settle.  Her last passenger would not stop humming.  Ivo, eleven, had come up the cable that morning wearing his mother's coat, and he had hummed ever since.  His hum sat on top of the terrace's song, and she could no longer hear which of them was climbing.  She could order him quiet.  She had tried that with her brother, years ago, in the hour before a terrace let go, and he had gone into the silence terrified and alone.  Silence had taken him, and ever since she had wanted to teach it to comfort instead.  She knelt so the boy's eyes were level with hers.  "You can keep that noise," she said.  "But I need your ears for one minute, and then you may have it back."  She put the vial in his palm and closed his fingers around it.  "The train will whistle again soon."  "After the echo comes back off the cliffs, there is a quiet."  "It is not empty."  "It is the only thing up here strong enough to hold us, and it needs somebody to listen to it."  Ivo's hum faltered.  The whistle came, thin and far below, and the cliffs handed it back, and then the hush opened over them like the inside of a bell.  Vaulting, cold, enormous.  The boy's hand tightened on the glass.  "It's snowing inside," he whispered.  Feathers, thickening.  Without his hum the reading stood clean at sixty-two, and the terrace's own voice was audible at last, a wire drawn tight.  She had her answer, and the answer had a shape: launch now, in the hush, while the seam still held.  She swung the gondola out and clipped the boy's belt to the bench.  "Keep listening," she told him.  "Tell me if the quiet changes."  He nodded, and the hum stayed swallowed, because he had been given work.  The cable took them off the terrace into the drifting dark.  Halfway across, the prism let go behind them with a sound like a chandelier learning to fall.  Light went everywhere, sheets of it, green and violet sliding over the valley.  Ivo did not cover his ears.  He held the vial up and watched the last crystals settle, and he said his mother's name into the quiet, and the quiet did not drop it.  Marta kept her hands on the brake and her eyes on the needle, thirty decibels now, a held breath.  They were still in transit, and would be until morning, suspended between one shelf of light and the next.  That was all right.  Being in transit was where the boy lived now, between a mother and no mother, and she could not put him down.  She had spent nineteen years moving people through the air, and tonight, for the first time since her brother, she had moved something else as well.  The boy leaned against her arm, listening, and the silence went on holding them both, and it had learned how.
