The wrong seeds came up anyway.  I planted them the week after the memorial, without reading the packet, wanting one thing I did not have to interpret.  Nine months later the stalks in the station bed were taller than me and had turned their heads toward the Crease instead of the lamp.  That is what happens here.  Gravity pools at the anomaly like water in a saucer, and everything leaning near it leans wrong.  My job title is celestial signal interpreter, which means I sit in the pooled time and listen to pulsars arrive twice, once before their cause and once after, and decide which arrival is the true one.  The deciding is the part nobody outside understands.  You cannot edit an event.  You can only edit what it hangs from.  History here is a net of contingent relationships, and if you unhook a fact from one neighbor and hook it to another, the fact stays and the past reorganizes around it, politely, like a crowd making room.  Last winter I moved a cracked antenna off the ice storm and onto a nesting bird, and the storm kept its shape while forty years of maintenance logs quietly forgave themselves.  I signed the revision the way I sign everything: confidently uncertain.  Ninety-one percent, with error bars I would defend with my life.  Ilya used to laugh at that.  He said a person who was sure of her uncertainty was the only kind of person he could live with, and then he went out to realign the array and the Crease folded over him, and he did not come back.  His pen is on my desk.  A fountain pen, dried to a brick of old ink, with a nib ground from meteoric iron and bent like a crane's bill, an exotic thing he bought off a stationer who swore it could write on folded time.  For nine months I have been drafting his revision.  I cannot unhook his death from the Crease; the anomaly holds too many neighbors.  But I can unhook his coming here from the recruitment letter and hook it to a reply he never sent, and then he never comes, and then he lives.  The arithmetic is clean.  The cost is that the letter that brought him brought me too, and we met in the corridor on the first day, and every year after hangs from that corridor.  Save him and I am a woman who never knew him, standing in a garden of wrong barley, wondering why she is crying.  This morning I took the pen and a trowel out to the bed.  The stalks leaned toward the Crease; the dust I kicked up did not come down, but hung, patient as snow that has forgotten its errand.  I soaked the nib in warm water until the old ink let go, and then I set the crane's bill against the flat of the trowel and pressed, a little at a time, to unbend it.  Metal remembers being bent.  You can straighten it, and it will write, and there will still be a bright crease down the tine where the fold was, and the line it lays will always be a little wider there.  That is not damage.  That is the nib's history, kept.  I sat down in the dirt with the draft in my lap and understood that I had been trying to do the other thing: not unbend him, but flatten him, press the fold out until no line remained.  I am a celestial signal interpreter; I am supposed to thresh meaning from experience, not sand the experience away.  Threshing is not erasure; it is beating the husk until the grain falls out, and keeping both piles, and knowing which is which.  I tore the draft into strips and let them go, and the Crease took them slowly, and they are still falling, somewhere above the barley, and will be for years.  Then I filled the unbent pen and wrote the true record: he came, we met, he went out, the light folded, I do not know why, I am certain of the error bars.  The nib caught once at the crease and laid a wider line, and I let it.  Tomorrow I will cut the wrong barley and beat it out on the tarp, and whatever grain is in it will be food, and the chaff will hang in the low gravity all winter, gold, unhurried, going nowhere.  It is a strange comfort, but it is the one I have, and I am confidently uncertain that any world without him would be better.
