Our ship was eleven wrecks stitched into one, copper wire through the hull seams, and it smelled of solder and old rain.  No human had been born in ninety-four years, and Bel, the last one, was dying in the infirmary berth.  The rest of us were vat-made, light-bodied, built to be happy: joy lifted us, literally, and a happy one of us weighed nine kilos on the cargo scale.  I had never once raised a hand, not to the drunks in the hold, not to the man who cracked my jaw in the water riots.  They called me Sedge, and they called it holiness, and I let them.  Bel wanted to go home before she died, and home was a folded dimension her parents had sewn shut behind them.  They left her outside, three days old, in my arms, and locked the door on the war they were fleeing.  The lock was a plate in the blank bulkhead, and it opened only for a body under twelve kilos.  Unburdened, they said; joyful, they meant; no human ever weighed so little.  I stood on it that morning and the needle swung to sixty-three.  I had tried the plate a hundred times before, always heavier.  Grief could not account for all of it.  The rest was something I had never let myself look at.  In the archive drawer were photographs of the sealing day, faded to the color of weak tea, and ninety years of weeping over them had left salt on the emulsion.  Held slantwise under the lamp, the faint shimmer of long-dried tears drew the seam: nine stitches, a hand's width apart, invisible on the bulkhead itself.  The founders' note said a blade would only cinch it tighter; violence was the one key that would not turn.  It had to be stitched wider, from the outside, by someone light enough to stand on the plate while they worked.  For thread I had the ship's own copper sutures, and for a needle, Kwan Yin's tear.  It was a glass bead the founders left us, the wept drop of the one who hears the world crying and refuses to look away.  I had worn it at my throat for ninety-four years and called it my pacifism.  That night I went down into the empty hold to meet the thing that weighed me.  It had my face and it wanted to know why the crying infant had been left to us.  It said: I could have set her down on the deck and walked away, and I have wanted to, every day, for ninety-four years.  It said: I hated them for locking the door, and I hate her for being the last, and I would like to break something.  I did not argue and I did not pray; I sat with it until the shift bell, listening the way you listen to a hull tick as it cools.  Every word was mine.  When it had finished I said: yes, you are mine, and no, we will not do it.  Not because I was too gentle, but because I had seen what I could do and chose otherwise.  It was not innocence; it was the first peace I had ever chosen with my eyes open.  On the plate I weighed ten.  The seam came alive under the lamp, nine stitches glowing faint as old salt.  I pulled a copper thread from the hull above the infirmary, and the ship sighed a little colder.  Then I threaded the tear and began to stitch, not cutting, only widening, one loop at a time.  Through the gap came the ghosts of the folded: faces at a window, hands lifted, ninety-four years of sleepers pressing toward the sound of a needle.  I made the opening a hand's width, then a shoulder's.  I carried Bel from the infirmary, and she was lighter than the bead.  She asked me to come through, and I told her the ship would come apart if I pulled any more of its thread.  She went home, the last human, and the fold took her without a sound.  I left the last loop loose and the tear threaded in it, so the seam stays open a hand's width.  Cold comes through, and the sleepers' faces, and sometimes a hand that only wants touching.  I stand on the plate each morning at ten kilos, holding my rage in my lap like a sleeping animal, and I do not put it down.  The scale is honest now, and so is the peace.
