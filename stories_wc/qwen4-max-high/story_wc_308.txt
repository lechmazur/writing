The canyon kept every sound it had ever been given, and gave none of them back.  Towers of bone stood in it like a drowned city, the ribs of beasts that had come down to drink when there was water, stacked and fused by centuries of layered dust.  Maren had skimmed stones across that dust for forty years, reading the canyon by the tones it returned.  A harmonic stone skimmer did not throw; she tuned.  Flat stone, low angle, a spin that made the note hold.  Now she worked from a ledge, because her legs had been crushed under a falling tower in the spring, and because the thing that knocked the tower down was still walking the canyon.  Not a beast.  A pulse left wandering: one struck note, loosed by a skimmer named Idra two hundred years ago, that had never found its answering echo and so had never been allowed to die.  It went around and around the bone towers, and where it passed the dust jumped and the old bones shed their skins.  Maren's rite was simple and older than the towers: you answered a dead skimmer's note, and let them rest.  Idra had taught the woman who taught Maren's grandmother, and nobody had ever gone in to answer her.  To answer this one she had to know where it would be, and she could not walk to find out.  So she set herself to learn to navigate without moving.  All summer she skimmed and listened, and all summer the echoes lied, because here every answer arrives wearing the clothes of an older answer.  She had outlasted worse than confusion.  The birds were honester.  Grey swifts nested in the eye sockets of the towers, and they would not fly through the pulse.  They bent around it, a hole traveling through the flock, and Maren learned to read that hole the way her grandmother had read weather: through the patterns in a flock of birds in flight.  Nine days of watching gave her its circuit: a slow amble up the west wall, a wobble at the narrows, a long stall at the third tower where the sound had to climb.  She scratched the map into a rib.  Then she went looking with her hands through the dust that had drifted against her ledge.  Dust here was a ledger; each layer a century, each century a color, ash and pollen and the slow rain of bone.  In the ninth layer she found a stone arrowhead, chipped from grey chert, its notch cut for a cord.  Maren had been taught to handle old things the way you handle sleepers: gently, and without pretending they were yours.  She turned it over and appraised it, as she had appraised ten thousand relics.  Not a price.  A place in the order of things: ninth layer, nine hundred years, a hunter who had stood where the water used to be and thrown at something worth the throwing.  Flat, balanced, a good skimming stone, and older than every name the canyon remembered.  It seemed right that the answer to Idra should be something that had already outlasted her.  On the last morning of summer the swifts went up, and the hole in them came around the south bend, and Maren knew where the wandering pulse would be in eleven breaths without moving so much as her head.  She laid the arrowhead flat against her thumb, found the note Idra had left behind, and skimmed.  The stone went out over the dust, skipping, singing, seven touches, eight, and the eighth was where the pulse was.  Two notes met and made a third thing, a chord, and the chord went home into the bone the way water goes into sand.  For the first time in two hundred years the canyon of endless echoes was quiet enough to hear the swifts' wings.  Maren wrote Idra's name on the rib beside her map, and the hunter's layer, and the date, because someone would come after her with worse legs and better questions.  Then she began, slowly, to drag herself toward the narrows, along a road she had walked all summer without leaving her ledge.
