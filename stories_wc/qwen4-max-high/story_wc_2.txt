Sela Quill repaired clocks that no one would ever come back for.  After the fire took Harrow, the survivors brought her their stopped ones anyway, and she wrote each repair into her ledger, minute by minute, because minutes had turned out to be the thing that killed people.  Beneath the ashes of the harbormaster's house she found the admiral's lost compass rose, a brass card with thirty-two points and a thirty-third scratched in by hand, unlabeled.  The card was warped, useless for navigation, and she kept it in her apron pocket the way other people keep a letter.  Then the well went dry.  The town said the fire had killed the spring, and Sela almost believed it, until she lined up the seven clocks on her bench.  They had stopped at 3:04, 3:16, 3:28, 3:40.  Twelve minutes, again and again, which is not how fire behaves.  Coincidence had become pattern, and pattern was the only language Sela trusted.  She had built the ledger to keep uncertainty out, and the town had burned anyway.  She put her ear to the dry well and heard, faint and regular, something breathing.  The water had not died; it was being held.  The way down began through the swirling leaves in a neglected greenhouse behind the burned chandlery.  No one had watered the vines since spring, and the dead leaves never settled; they rose in a slow column against the cracked glass, pulled by a draft from below.  Sela followed the leaves the way she would follow a needle, and they led her to a fissure under the potting bench.  She went down with a lantern, a pendulum rod, and the admiral's rose.  Below the town, below the ash and the cellar stone, the river fell out of a black ceiling into a pool she could not see the far side of.  A subterranean waterfall, and every twelve minutes its roar thinned to a sigh, and the sigh pulled air down through the wells, and ash into seven clock cases.  The throat of the fall was choked with charred beams and a gray paste of ash; the water was backing up behind it, patient and enormous.  Beneath the ashes, the river was still alive, and it was going to take the town's foundations with it when it finally let go.  Sela opened her ledger to the page where she had planned this descent, and found she had planned nothing that fit.  She had drawn the cavern as a tidy box; the cavern was a throat, and it was swallowing.  Out of habit she held the rose up to the lantern, hoping the thirty-third point would mean something, and it stayed blank.  The admiral had lost his ship on a coast he had charted perfectly; the story went that he drew the extra ray afterward, for the bearing no one can name in advance.  Sela had always read that as a warning; standing in the roar, she read it as an invitation.  So she improvised.  She set the pendulum rod against the keystone beam, seated the brass rose as a shim where the rod bit into wet char, and watched the leaves that fell down the shaft from the greenhouse.  They climbed while the water thundered and dropped when it sighed.  The leaves were her escapement now, a twelve-minute beat she could not measure any other way.  She waited two cycles, uncertainly certain, hands on the rod, sure of the rhythm and sure of nothing else.  The leaves lifted.  She threw her weight, the brass card bit, the beam rolled, and the ash went out of the throat in one gray cough that pulled her lantern into the dark.  The fall found its voice, and the wells above her began to fill.  She climbed out through the greenhouse at dawn, soaked, without the rose, which was somewhere under the water now, holding a bearing she had finally named.  Harrow got its water back that morning, and its clocks, eventually, though Sela left one on the bench stopped at 3:16.  In her ledger she still plans the hours, but she leaves the last line of every page empty, thirty-third and unlabeled, room for the thing she cannot schedule.  She had wanted a plan that could not be surprised; what she has now is a plan that can be, and hands that know what to do when it is.
