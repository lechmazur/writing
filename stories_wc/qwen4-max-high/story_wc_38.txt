The corridor knew me before I knew it.  Nine meters down, the drowned laboratory held one pocket of air, and I came up into it out of the black water.  Iodine and warm pennies, the smell of a childhood nobody had ever told me I spent here.  The tiles sloped toward a drain I could have found blindfolded. 

On a ceiling rib above me, a spider dropped its first anchor line and began to build.  My gauge was cracked, so I took the spider for my clock.  Whatever I could do here, I could do in the time it takes a spider to spin a web. 

Behind the hatch glass a girl sat with her knees up, eleven maybe, watching me the way you watch weather.  The tag on her wrist said nine.  Mine had said nine too, in a room I could not remember and could not stop remembering.  The outer handle was sheared away; the lock lived on her side, six rings of letters, one word.  The Bureau had added its own refinement: one wrong word and the chamber would take the lake. 

Emil built those locks.  Emil, who taught me ciphers in a cell with six bone dice, letters cut into their faces, so we could play hangman with the names we were forbidden to say.  We hanged men who had already been hanged, one wrong letter at a time.  The Bureau drowned him with the program three days ago. 

The line in my helmet crackled, the officer up in the boat, patient as a hook.  "Give me the word," he said, "and I winch you both up."  He wanted the key because the key opened other doors, other labs, other nines.  I did not answer, and I did not hurry.  Defiance is mostly that: keeping your own tempo while another man counts. 

I had Emil's journal in oilskin against my chest, four years of entries that looked like bad poetry and were not.  Every line scanned, and the first letter of every stressed word spelled what he needed spelled.  I break codes the way other people hear music, and I have never been able to explain that to a man with a clipboard.  Emil taught me the reading as a dance, because a child cannot hold a rule but can hold a step.  Slow, slow, quick, quick, slow: the hangman's step, which my mother danced with me on a kitchen floor that had never been a kitchen. 

The spider had its frame up and was laying the spokes.  Sound does not cross four centimeters of steel, and the tone tests had taken the girl's hearing; I saw her watch my mouth and give up on it.  So I stood in the shallow water and danced the key at her.  Slow, slow, quick, quick, slow, my palm slapping the glass on every beat that mattered.  I held up the hangman's dice so she would know whose hands were teaching. 

Her chin lifted.  Her feet answered before her mind did, the same step, the same small lift of the wrist, because Emil had taught us both in this room, eleven years apart.  The officer said my name twice, the way you rattle a door.  I pressed the journal page to the glass and danced it twice more while her finger went along the line, stopping where I stopped. 

The spider was on its spiral now, the last slow coil.  Six letters.  Her hands went to the rings.  The word was AFRAID.  Emil had built every door in the program to open on the one word they beat out of us, so that the thing we were punished for saying became the only thing that could turn a lock.  Weakness, encrypted, is a key nobody dares guess. 

The hatch swung and the girl came into my arms, all elbows, smelling of iodine.  I put the dice in her pocket and the journal in her shirt, because a key is only worth keeping if it is handed on.  "Still working," I told the boat.  "She is only learning a dance." 

Above us the spider had finished, sitting at the hub of a wheel it had spun out of its own softness, and the whole thin thing held.  We went up slowly, the way you must, and she counted the steps against my chest.
