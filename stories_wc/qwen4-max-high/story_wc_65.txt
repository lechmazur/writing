Halla mapped ignorance for a living.  Other cartographers drew coasts and toll roads; she drew the blank places where surveys stopped and guessing began, and shaded them by how badly a wrong guess would hurt.  The valley called her the ignorance cartographer and paid her in barley. 

She worked in the obsidian spire above Lake Kerne, a tower of black glass the old kings had raised over a hot spring, so its lower floors steamed while its windows wore frost.  Below her the lake lay frozen to the far shore, three weeks early.  On the desk lay the Ledger of the Coming Year and a kingfisher quill, its nib cut from the shaft of a blue feather her grandmother had taken from a bird that no longer came.  The birds had stopped coming as the ice took the lake earlier each spring; the feather was the last of them. 

The charter was plain: whatever the cartographer inked on the first page before midwinter became the valley's plan, and the valley would build that and nothing else.  One page, one line, one year; the law called this writing the future, and the law meant it literally.  This winter the granary held four hundred measures of barley and the sowing would need three hundred and eighty.  Twenty measures of slack were the entire room she had to plant something new inside. 

So she had spent sixty days running the winter forward on the counting frames, feeding them forty-one years of ice dates, spring flows, and yields, and letting the arithmetic guess a thousand springs.  Her imagination ran hot, but she kept it tethered: every flight had to land on a number somebody had actually measured.  She had imagined glass houses over the hot spring, and the frames said the glass would eat eighteen measures and fail.  She had imagined a fish weir under the ice, and the frames said the water would go foul by February.  A wider purse would have bought glass and wasted it; twenty measures forced her to invent something small enough to survive being wrong.  Then she had imagined a stone throat carrying the spring's warmth out beneath the lakeshore fields, and the frames gave her thaw eleven days early, a second sowing, and a margin.  But the throat ran under the northeast shore, where her surveys were thinnest, and on her own map that ground was shaded black: bedrock unknown, water table unknown, ignorance nobody had ever paid to fill. 

Steward Bram climbed the stairs at dusk with his own page, a fair copy of last year's rows, safe and exactly as hungry as the valley had always been.  "Write the old rows," he said.  "You cannot spend barley on ground you have not measured."  It was true, and it was why she had spent her last four measures hiring the well-digger's sons to sound the northeast shore with iron rods.  She unrolled the sheet: nine soundings, bedrock at two spans, gravel above it, the black shading gone pale.  "Still a guess," she said, "but a guess that knows its own width."  Bram read the soundings twice, then read the three measures she had set aside to abandon the throat if the second sowing failed.  "You have budgeted for your own ignorance," he said.  "That is the map I make."  He laid his safe page on the brazier and watched it curl into a black wing. 

The model had prevailed by three measures and nine iron soundings, and she knew how narrow that was.  She dipped the kingfisher nib, and ink climbed the slit the way water finds a channel.  She did not write yet.  She thought of next winter's cartographer climbing these stairs to shade a new blank place, and of the year after, when the frames would eat the first harvest's numbers and widen the throat or close it.  A future was not a sentence you finished; it was a sentence that kept being corrected, and twenty measures of barley were enough to begin one.  Below the window the lake held its white silence, and under that silence the spring ran warm, patient, and unused.  The nib hovered a breath above the blank page, blue as the bird that would come back when the water opened.
