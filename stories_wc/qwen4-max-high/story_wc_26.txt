The library blooms above the clouds, and I am paid to watch it darken.  Dreams became shareable forty-one years ago, and we learned the price by paying it.  Give away enough of them and your shadow comes loose at the heels.  Unbound, it drifts toward whatever light is left.  So the archive keeps dusk noticers on the terrace, and I am the sharp-eyed one.  I can see the seam where a shadow leaves its feet, a hairline of brightness, gone in a breath.  Up here the shelves are living vines, and every dream we store opens as a pale flower above the reading desks.  At this altitude dusk lasts almost an hour, which is why they built the library here, above the weather, where the light goes slowly.  My sister Ada paid for the first vine.  She sold ninety dreams to a broker in the valley, one a week, until nothing of hers grew at night.  She wanted a place where nobody would have to sell what they dreamed.  The wanting was the last thing to go, and it went with her shadow, out across the cloud field, one evening in the founding year.  Now she sits below the weather and is gently, terribly content.  When I visit, she asks about the weather up here and is glad to hear it, and nothing in her leans forward.  She gave me a river stone from the bend where she first said it out loud, smooth, grey, warm from her pocket.  I keep it in my coat while I work.  The terrace camera has filmed every dusk since the founding, silently, because sound carries dreams the way water carries seed.  Last month I began comparing silent footage from decades apart, the founding year against this one, frame laid over frame.  I was looking for a girl-shaped shadow.  I found her in the old reels, small, arm out, reaching for something at the edge of the terrace.  Then I ran the new reels and could not find her anywhere, and I understood that I had been watching her all along.  The enormous shadow that falls across the clouds at dusk is not the library's.  The building's own shadow is a thin, honest thing that points east and behaves.  The other one has arches, and a roof of leaves, and doorways that open where no door stands.  Unbound shadows do not decay; they metamorphose into the shape of what their owner wanted.  Ada's girl-shadow had grown, over forty-one silent years, into the library she never got to keep.  I sat with the two films running side by side until the flowers closed.  Regret is a bright thing when you finally see what it is made of.  I could not give Ada back her wanting.  There is no procedure for it; the brokers only sell one direction.  I asked them anyway, twice, and the second time they were kind about it, which was worse.  But a river stone remembers weight, and an unbound shadow will still come to the hand it knew.  This evening I walked out to the terrace edge, softly deliberate, the way you move near something that has been startled for forty years.  The great shadow lay over the clouds like a country.  I set the stone down in the last of the light, and I waited.  It came the way weather comes.  The arches folded, the leaf-roof drew in, the doorways closed like eyes, and for eleven seconds there was a girl on the terrace with her arm out, holding a stone.  Not Ada; the shape of the hope Ada had, exact, unbent, still reaching.  Then the light went, and it unfolded back into the library, and the flowers opened for the night readers.  The camera caught all of it, silently, the way it catches everything.  Tomorrow I will file the two reels together, the founding dusk and this one, so that anyone who compares them can see it plain: hope keeps its shape after the person cannot.  I will label it with her name, and the word for what she wanted, and I will keep the stone in my coat, warm, and go on noticing.
