Noon on the obsidian ridge, and the black glass threw the sun back at the town like an accusation.  I watched it from the clock tower, where I had lived for eleven months, winding.  Our bell did not tell hours so much as permission: when it rang, the sluice gates shut, and the salt flats became sea.  I wound it every seventh day, forty-one turns, and logged the temperature and the air's pressure in a hand small enough to shame a clerk.  Last spring four rakers were still out on the glass when it rang, precisely, honestly, on time.  The tide table was ninety seconds stale; I had measured the drift myself, reported it once, politely, and then let it go.  Diligence is cheap until it costs you your standing in a small town.  Afterward they brought me the broken rakes, because here a drowned worker's rake is snapped so that no one else harvests his patch.  Marta Vey brought her husband's handle and did not throw it at me.  She laid it on my winding bench and said, read it when you can bear to.  Under the loupe I use for escapements, the ash grain opened like a road.  Along it she had carved marks so small I first took them for splits in the wood: one for every day since the water.  Not curses.  A tally of grief being spent a hair's breadth at a time: swept, ate, mended, slept.  Three hundred marks, and nowhere on that handle the word forgive.  I understood then that absolution here was not a door but a staircase, and that I had been waiting for a door.  So I went to the pendulum.  The bob is a brass lens filled with lead, heavy as a sleeping child, and it swings above a pit that goes down to the ridge's cold root.  I caught it at the top of its arc.  There is a pause there, at the apex, when the weight is neither falling nor rising and the whole town's time hangs undecided in my palm.  In that pause the heat came up through the floor and I saw the flats again, the glare, the four bent backs, the water taking the light before it took them.  Ninety seconds; I could not give them back all at once.  Slam the bell ninety seconds late tomorrow and the downshore boats, who set their watches by us, would go onto the shoals in the dark.  Balance is shared, or it is not balance.  But beneath the bob there is a threaded cup for rating weights, and a grain of salt in that cup lowers the center of oscillation by a measurable nothing.  I released the bob.  It fell, and the escapement bit, and the tower resumed giving the town its seconds.  I did the arithmetic three times: one second of delay gained each month, ninety months, seven and a half years of grains.  Then I emerged.  Down the hundred and four stairs, out into a glare that made the obsidian ring like a struck pan, along the ridge to the harbor board with my tables under one arm and the broken handle under the other.  People came out of doorways to watch me pass; eleven months is long enough to become a rumor.  I read my figures aloud twice and refused, this time, to be polite about them.  They voted the correction lawful, published, posted at every gate, so that every watch on the ridge would lose the same slow second together.  Marta stood at the back with her arms folded, and afterward she came to the tower and watched me weigh the first grain of salt on the assay balance.  Salt from her husband's patch; I had swept a jar of it from the flats in April and could not throw it away.  I dropped it into the cup, and with my finest graver I cut a mark on the handle beneath her last one: a line, and beside it, one.  She read it under the loupe, holding the wood steady with both hands.  Not forgive.  One.  That is all any of us can be given: the next small mark, and the next, until the handle is full or the debt is.  Above us the bob climbed, hesitated in its bright pause, and fell again, a grain heavier, keeping the town's slow honest time.
