Vesna had run her mother's nursery for thirty-one years and still called it her mother's.  She kept ledgers of plant behavior: which leaves folded before rain, which stems leaned toward a footstep, which roots would cross a pot to find wet clay.  Studying them was the only prayer she knew.  Storm season came in late that August, three squalls in nine days, and the third one took the trellis down.  She found it at dawn, mossy and shining after rain, its lattice folded over the linden sapling like a hand closed on a moth.  The linden was hers, or had been.  Fourteen years ago she had dug it from the roadside as a whip of a seedling and tied it to the trellis to make it climb straight.  Lindens do not climb.  It had spent fourteen years trying to be a tree in the shape of a fence.  Every spring it pushed out juvenile leaves, small and pale, the kind a seedling makes, and every spring she cut the leaders back and retied the tape.  Her apprentice, Teo, watched her do it with his jaw set.  Teo was circuitously sincere: he never said a thing was wrong, he said what it resembled.  "A stake holds a tree the way a fist holds a bird," he said once, and went to water the ferns.  She had written the sentence in the ledger, under observations, where she could pretend it was about plants.  Now the moss was cold under her palms and the tape had gone green with algae, and the sapling stood bent in the wreck, holding.  She knelt to free it and stopped.  If she cut the ties, the thing she had made would end, and she did not know what she was if she was not the one who made it.  That was the metamorphosis she had been refusing for fourteen years, and it was hers to make, not the tree's.  She went inside and opened the ledgers to the pages she had been avoiding.  Six years of staked saplings against six years of unstaked ones: the staked trees had thinner trunks, longer internodes, and snapped in the first August wind.  Wind was not damage.  Wind was the instruction a tree needed to hear before it would build itself wood instead of height.  She had recorded it, dated it, initialed it, and then gone out and tightened the tape.  Teo came with a pry bar and did not offer to help until she asked.  She asked.  They reconstructed the trellis across the afternoon, and she made him do it wrong on purpose: posts set wide, crosspieces loose, a frame the wind could walk through.  Not a cage, a trellis for the beans that actually wanted one.  Then she cut every tie on the linden.  The sapling sprang up nine inches and stood there shivering, crooked, ridiculous, alive.  She did not stake it.  The method was the hardest work she had ever done, because it never finished: she rebuilt by releasing control, and then had to release it again each morning, while her hands itched.  In September the linden made its first adult leaves.  She picked one and held it up to the wet light: heart-shaped, and lopsided at the base, one lobe running further down the stem than the other, the way every linden leaf in the world is made.  No linden leaf pretends to be symmetrical.  She pressed it into the ledger, on the page where Teo's fist and bird were written, and under it she wrote what she had never written plainly.  I was the stake.  Teo read it over her shoulder and said the beans looked thirsty, which was his way of saying she had done a good thing.  She had stopped calling the nursery her mother's, though she still said it sometimes, out of habit, the way a tree keeps a scar in its wood.  The storms were not over; there would be another by Friday, and another after that, all through the season.  She went out after the next rain to the mossy trellis and stood with her hands at her sides, watching the linden lean and recover, lean and recover, thickening where the strain was, becoming what it had been waiting to become.  She let it.  Then she went in and wrote it down, so that tomorrow she would remember to let it again.
