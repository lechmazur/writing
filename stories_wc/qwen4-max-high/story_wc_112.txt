The station manufactured its own weather, and Pell manufactured its sky.  He was the sky mechanic, obliging in the way machines are warm: tea at 0400, a yes before the question finished, kindness that ran on schedule whether or not anyone needed it.  I needed him to stop the sky, so of course I asked. 

The xenobiology station sat under a dome on a moon where the air was thin enough to freeze thought, and the fliers we studied navigated by magnetism, not light.  At twilight the moon's field lines knotted above us into a magnetic nexus, and inside that knot the fliers roosted, hanging in the hush between one wingbeat and the next.  That hush lasted four seconds.  I needed ninety. 

My hypothesis was simple and unfundable: that for the fliers, time moves sideways.  Not a road behind them but a room around them, every moment standing adjacent to every other.  Their wing membranes stored memory through holographic principles: interference fringes laid down in the tissue, so that any fragment, however small, held the whole animal's life.  Read a shard while the flock beats, and their wings churn the field; you get sequence, a smear.  Read one in the hush, wings at rest, and you should get everything at once, spread laterally, the way a hologram gives the whole window from a splinter of glass.  The hush meant the nexus, and the nexus meant Pell's lever. 

He listened to the whole pitch without interrupting, which was his particular courtesy.  Then he said, "That'll cost you the heat," and went to do it.  "They hold through storms," he said.  "They can hold through us." 

The lever stalled the dome's field coils at the exact angle of convergence, holding twilight open like a door with a boot in it.  The power had to come from somewhere, so it came from the radiators.  Frost climbed the inside of the windows in ferns.  Pell brought me tea anyway, and a second jacket, and did not mention that his hands were shaking from cold or from something else.  Outside, four hundred fliers hung motionless in a sky that would not finish darkening.  I had asked for ninety seconds; he gave me eleven minutes, because I never said stop and he would not say it for me. 

The shard came from a flier that had died in the netting last winter, a triangle of membrane no bigger than a stamp.  I lit it with the reference beam and turned the plate until the fringes agreed.  Under the reader it went from smear to structure.  Not a life story but a life field: hatching beside the first storm beside the mate beside the netting beside the cold, all of it side by side, nothing behind anything.  Time moves sideways; I had my proof, and it was quiet as a light coming on in an empty room.  Nothing in me announced itself; it was a subdued awakening, the sort that arrives seated. 

Pell was looking at the sky, not at my screen.  "Ana died in this light," he said.  "Eleven years, and I still keep the sky here when nobody is watching."  He said it the way he said everything, on schedule, as if the words had been waiting in a queue.  That was when I understood his obligingness: a man who says yes to everyone never has to admit what he wants for himself.  In six years I had never asked him a question that was not a request.  He had given me eleven minutes of stopped twilight because he wanted eleven minutes of stopped twilight. 

If time moves sideways, she was not behind him.  She was beside him, the way the hatching is beside the netting, and no amount of frozen sky would make her further away or nearer.  I told him that, badly, in the wrong words, with my breath fogging.  He listened without interrupting, which was his courtesy, and then he did something he had never done in eleven years.  He asked me for something.  "Let it go dark," he said.  "And stay for the tea." 

I pushed the lever back.  The nexus loosened, twilight drained west, and four hundred fliers woke the way they do everything: a shiver along the membrane, a single beat, no announcement.  The radiators ticked, and warmth came back into the room like something forgiven.  Pell poured, mechanically warm, his hands still shaking, and I stayed.
