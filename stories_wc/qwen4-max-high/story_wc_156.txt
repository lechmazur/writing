Marisol Vega brought dead murals back to shouting color, and she did it loudly.  In the sound wave garden, where hedges were clipped into waveforms and three granite dishes faced one another across tuned gravel, loudness was a tool.  She hummed scales at fresh plaster to find its resonance, numbered her pigment jars in the order of jokes she had forgotten, and tapped every repaired inch with a coin.  Methodically playful, her old supervisor had called her, which was the kindest thing anyone had said about her work.  The coin was a hobo nickel, dug out of the garden's footing on her first morning: a buffalo nickel some wandering hand had recarved, the profile shaved into a man with a hat and a bindle, five cents turned into a stranger.  The mural she was saving ran along the curved wall behind the middle dish, and it showed Odile Ferrer, the acoustician who had planted this place, holding out her hand.  Odile had hired her once, at twenty-three, and Marisol had been certain about everything ever since.  The hand was empty.  Water had taken whatever Odile once offered, leaving a pale blur the exact shape of a question.  The archive photograph was clear enough: a coin, round, ordinary, certain.  But the nickel in Marisol's pocket had come out of Odile's own mortar, and a woman who built a garden out of echoes did not plant a plain nickel in her foundation.  At dusk her apprentice Teo climbed the scaffold and asked, "So what goes in her hand?"  Everything that happened next happened in the pause before she answered.  She leaned back, and the middle dish gathered the garden's noise and poured it into her ear, and she went under.  She woke to a finished wall.  In the painted hand sat a plain nickel, photograph-perfect, and Marisol felt the clean click of a job done right.  Then she hummed her testing scale, and the wall gave nothing back.  The hedges stood clipped and mute; the gravel took her footsteps and kept them; the dishes were only stone.  Certainty, it turned out, was a soundproof room.  She woke again, still on the scaffold, Teo's question still hanging in the air like a struck note.  The hand was still blank, and she understood that the first waking had been a room inside this one.  She spoke into the dish to steady herself: "It's a coin."  The garden took her voice around the hedge-waveforms, off the granite, back along the curved wall, and returned it bewitched: not her tone, not her vowels, but an old woman's dry amusement, saying the same three words as if they were the funniest thing she had ever heard.  Reflected sound, Odile had written in the garden's only surviving notebook, is the one mirror that cannot be posed for.  Marisol laughed, and the laugh came back as somebody else's, and she woke a third time inside the laugh.  Now the footing trench was open and the plaster was wet, and Odile, alive, knelt with the nickel in her palm, thumbing the little carved hat.  "I found a face in five cents," she said, and dropped it into the mortar, and asked the young restorer watching her whether she would rather have the answer or the guessing.  Marisol had answered "the answer" then, and had spent thirty years being right.  She woke out of that room, and out of the next, and out of the mute finished wall, the way a diver comes up through colder and colder water.  Teo was still waiting; the echo of her own three words was still dying in the hedge.  She took out the nickel and set it on the scaffold rail, where the wanderer's bindle caught the last light.  She loaded her brush with the ochre she had ground for a hat, for a bindle, for a face nobody had authorized.  Certainty had a photograph; curiosity had a coin somebody had bothered to change.  Thirty years of being right had cost her the guessing, and she wanted it back.  "Teo," she said, ending the pause, "what if she's offering us the man instead of the money?"  The dish took his shrug, and her question, and handed them back altered, and the two of them stood listening, unanswerable and awake, while she painted.
