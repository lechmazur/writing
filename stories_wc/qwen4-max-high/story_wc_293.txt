After the hitchhiker vanished, I stayed at the scenic pullout on the mountain's shoulder and played, because it was the only place on the pass where cars slowed enough to hear a fiddle.  Below me the gorge held what the fire had left of our town, and above me the new bridge stood in scaffold wrap, printed with ten thousand faces.  They were the amnestied.  When the records hall burned, the state scanned what survived, and most of us came back as blocks.  The legislature called it pixelated amnesty: if your face resolved to fewer than eight pixels across the eyes, the state could not swear you were you, so your debts, your warrants, your sentences dissolved.  Mercy by low resolution.  My sister Ada had a warrant.  She also had a face I could have drawn blind, and now the state had made her a gray square, and the word beside her square was never.  Never identified.  Never found. 

The hitchhiker had climbed out of a pickup at dusk with silver nitrate stains on her fingers and a plate box under her arm.  A fiddler who eats from a hat learns to be dynamically observant, reading a stranger's weather and retuning to it.  I noticed her boots had no dust, her thumb had no callus, and her eyes went to the wrap instead of the road.  Her face was scar-work, shiny and new, and I did not know it.  Then she said my name, and the name of the tune I was playing, which Ada wrote for the old bridge and never wrote down.  She put a flake of wet-plate collodion glass in my palm.  On it, sharp as a wire, was a quarter of a face: one eyebrow, the corner of one eye, three lashes.  It was Ada's eye, from before, caught in a shop window's reflection.  Glass remembers better than servers.  She had been photographing the town on glass the morning it burned.  The fire took her face and left the plate.  I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, bright and cracked, because I was holding proof and pardon in the same hand and they canceled each other.  Identify Ada and the amnesty fails; the warrant wakes.  Keep her blurred and she stays a square, a never. 

"Don't hand it up," she said.  "Watch the wrap."  Then a truck came over the rise with its lights on, and in the time it took me to shield my eyes she was gone.  The drizzle had wet the whole pullout except two boot-shaped patches of dry gravel. 

I have recounted that minute to troopers, dispatchers, and the drunks at the overlook, who call me the bridge-song fiddler, and every telling loses her a little more.  So I stopped telling and started watching.  The gorge breathes at dusk, one long exhale every eleven seconds; I timed it on my bow.  The wrap took the breath and spoke.  Panels lifted, slapped, settled, and I saw that some had been cut away with a knife, so the wind whistled through the gaps at pitches.  Someone had tuned the scaffolding.  I mapped the flutters on the back of a toll receipt: high, low, low, high, a rest, then the climbing phrase.  It was the bridge of Ada's bridge song, the part she never played for anyone but me.  Never, said the state.  Never written down, I had told them, and here it was, whistling out of construction scrim at four thousand feet. 

I could have carried the flake down to the records office and bought my sister a name and a cell.  Instead I folded it into the receipt and tucked it under my chin rest, where the wood keeps things.  Then I lifted the fiddle and answered the wrap, note for note, the way we used to answer each other across the water when the old bridge still stood and our mother still counted us in.  My hands shook.  The wind held its eleven seconds.  When it came again a new panel opened, a note I had not heard since I was nineteen, and I understood that the pardon could stay coarse while my sister stayed sharp, that I could keep both, and sculpt something possible from the ash of never. 

I am still at the pullout.  Every dusk I play the crossing, and every dusk the bridge plays back, and drivers stop to photograph the beautiful gray faces and hear nothing at all.  I do not believe it, and I answer anyway.
