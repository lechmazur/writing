The arbor stood on nine quartz pillars, raw and unpolished, and in the river mist they looked like frozen milk.  Ilse had built it herself, plumb and level, each post firmly grounded in tamped gravel.  She had spent eleven years reading fracture surfaces for an insurance lab, and she did not believe in anything she could not measure twice.  That was why people called her a mystic, and why they came.  She listened the way other people looked.  A widow would say her husband's name once, and Ilse would hear the half second of hesitation before it, the small breath taken to lift the weight.  Then she would put her palm on the door handle of the dead man's car and tell the widow what he had left in it.  Courage, usually.  Patience, once, in a gearshift worn smooth by a hand that had waited at a lot of red lights.  These were lies.  She knew the metal held nothing but oil and skin salts, and she knew the widows drove home steadier, so she kept telling them.  A truthful lie, she called it in her ledger, and underlined the first word.  The ledger was her real work: forty-one months of tests, begun years before Neve died, meant to prove that objects absorb emotion.  She logged temperature, humidity, and the exact words her clients used, hunting for any residue that outlasted the feeling.  Every morning before the clients, she went to the back of the arbor, where Neve's car sat under the dead vine.  Nine months ago the fog had come off the river and her daughter had met a logging truck on the bend.  Ilse had identified the glass herself, from photographs, before she let anyone tow it home.  Tempered side, laminated windscreen, radial star at the driver's shoulder, no defect, no fault, nothing to blame.  The hush had shifted after the funeral.  For weeks the house had held a silence so complete she could hear her own blood, and then, in June, the silence moved.  It left the kitchen and settled here, under the quartz, around the car.  She wrote the date down, because a shifted hush was data.  Frost formed on the driver's window most mornings, and through it she communed with her daughter.  She used Neve's glass prism, the one that had hung from the rearview mirror, holding it to the grey light until the mist broke into its parts.  In that thin spectrum the ferns on the glass stood up in relief, and she read them the way she had once read a fracture: origin, direction, intent.  One branch always ran left and hooked, like the tail of the N in Neve's handwriting.  Nine mornings out of ten, that hook.  She logged it as absorption: the glass keeping the shape of the hand that had once wiped it clear.  On the eleventh of October her assumptions shattered, and it took her four seconds.  She wiped the window clean with her sleeve, breathed on it, and watched the ferns come back exactly, hook and all.  Not memory.  A hairline scratch, three inches long, left by the tow chain, seeding the ice along its line every single time.  Her own breath was the water.  Her own attention was the handwriting.  She sat down on the wet gravel in her good coat and let the mist come through the pillars.  The prism hung from her fingers and threw a small, ruined rainbow onto her boot.  Nothing had been stored.  The glass had kept nothing, because glass keeps nothing; that is why we make windows of it and not graves.  Then the hush moved again.  She felt it go out from under the arbor, past the quartz, up the gravel path toward the house, and she understood that it had never been in the car.  It had been in her, and she had carried it out here every morning and set it down.  She opened the ledger and crossed out forty-one months.  Under it she wrote: objects do not absorb emotion; people leave it where they can find it again.  At nine she had a client, a man with his wife's gloves in his pocket, and she put his hand on the door handle and told her lie, the truthful one, about what the metal had kept.  He wept and drove away steady.  That night she hung the prism in the kitchen window, where the refrigerator hummed, and said good night to her daughter, knowing it was a lie, knowing it was true.
