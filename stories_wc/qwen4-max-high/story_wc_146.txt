The bell tuner came to Vesper Hollow in what would have been the last week before the collapse.  She carried no tools but a tuning fork of black ebony, and she never said who had sent for her.  I was the only one left to meet her.  I stayed because my mother's name is on the bell.  Eleven houses, eleven doors open to the weather, grass grown up through the hinges.  The hush of that place was not emptiness; it was wild, the way a held breath is wild. 

She went straight to the ancient archway at the head of the lane, the gate the town had been built through and around.  In a niche above it hung the town bell, green with weather, silent since the last funeral.  She struck the ebony fork on her heel and pressed its stem to the keystone.  Wood gives no bright ring; it knocks dull and then takes the pitch of whatever it touches, which is why she used it.  She listened with her teeth. 

"Flat," she said.  "Two generations flat." 

I asked what was flat. 

"The agreement," she said, and would not say more. 

The mason who raised the arch had also laid the cobblestones, and he had tuned the streets like strings under it.  Stone alone was never meant to carry that weight.  A town walking in measure was meant to carry it, thousands of feet a day feeding the low note that keeps a load spread honest through every joint.  When the mill closed and the families went down to the valley, the walking stopped, the note sagged, and the keystone began its slow slide. 

"So you will rebuild the arch," I said. 

"I will rebuild the walking," she said.  "Stone follows." 

She was infinitely patient, and it was not gentleness; it was a craft.  Every dawn she stood under the arch, struck the black fork, and waited for the town to answer.  On the fourth dawn she put the fork in my hand and told me to carry it down to the valley and strike it on doorframes. 

"Wood remembers," she said, which was the whole of her argument. 

I knocked on thirty doors, held up a black fork, and said the gate was singing flat and needed feet.  They thought loneliness had turned me strange.  But old Ferris came, because his grandfather had laid those cobbles, and then his cousins, and then the woman who used to keep the bakery, and then children who had never seen the place. 

She set us in lines and gave us a meter, heel and toe, slow as a resting heart.  We walked from the well to the arch and back, twenty-two of us, and the wild hush took a pulse.  By the third day the cobbles answered under our shoes with a sound you felt in your knees.  By the fifth, the dust stopped sifting from the keystone.  Each dawn she measured with the ebony fork and said only, "Less flat."  Never finished, never praised, only less flat, and she seemed content with that arithmetic. 

People began sleeping in their old houses to be nearer the walking.  The bakery opened so the walkers would have bread at the turn by the well.  Ferris reset the loose stones he crossed each morning, and the children invented skipping games inside the meter, which she allowed. 

"A town needs some noise in its chord," she said. 

I asked her why she tuned bells at all, if what she cared about was feet. 

"A bell is not for being loud," she said.  "It is so everyone agrees what hour they are standing in."  "I only work before the collapse."  "Rubble has no note left to correct." 

In October she climbed to the niche and filed the bell's lip until it matched the fork, and then she let it speak.  Under the arch our feet came down on the beat, all of us, and the stone held.  She left the next morning without goodbye, and left the ebony fork on the well ledge, which I understood to mean the work was ours now.  I strike it every dawn against the keystone and listen with my teeth.  Vesper Hollow is not saved; it is kept, one measured walk at a time, and the hush here is still wild, but it is shared.
