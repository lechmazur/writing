Mira fell asleep at her workbench with a fresco fragment in her palm, and the daydream folded her in.  Eleven minutes, perhaps, between her tea going warm and cold.  In them she walked the Orchard District, drowned three centuries deep in fog.  Quince trees stood like broken ribs; the fog beaded on them and dripped, patient as accountants. 

She was famous outside for murals that shouted: cobalt saints, tangerine whales.  Lately every one of them had come out of her polite and dead.  She had come here to find where her joy had gone, the way you retrace a street looking for a dropped key.  The fragment was plaster no bigger than a sardine tin: a painted hand offering a painted pear, ochre and rose.  Someone had loved that wall enough to make it, and the fog had kept the love and taken the wall. 

Mira did not grind pigment anymore; she painted with light.  Her satchel held lenses, mirrors, a phosphor lamp, and gels cut from old festival banners.  Light was honest and quick, but it needed something to land on, and fog only smeared it into weather.  That was her whole trouble, really: she had colors and no surface that would hold them. 

A woman sat under the dead quinces with a lantern shuttering in her lap, tapping.  "You're the loud painter," she said, not unkindly.  "I'm the quiet one lately," Mira said.  The woman was a node, that was her word for it, in the Lattice, the district's knowledge network.  Paper rotted here and ink ran, so the fog folk had strung their memory between households: each kept one thing and answered for it.  A recipe, a hue, the name of a pear nobody had tasted in nine generations.  Lantern taps carried questions along the wires; answers came back like water finding level. 

Mira held up the fragment and asked the network what the hand had been holding.  The taps went out; the fog ticked; a boy arrived with a tin the Lattice had guarded against just such a question.  Inside, coiled, was film: a traveling photographer had shot the last harvest festival, eighty years back, and died in the fog before he could develop it.  Eighty years of latent pears, waiting in silver.  "Exposed," Mira said.  "Not lost."  "Nothing here is lost," the woman said.  "Only unasked." 

The network gave up the developer reluctantly, household by household: quince-leaf tannin from one, ash lye from another, a temperature, a count of seconds kept by a man who kept only counts.  Mira worked in a ruined cider house, laughing at herself for taking orders from a fog.  The strip came up out of the bath like a shy animal: dancers, a ladder, a woman tossing a pear, a child open-mouthed with joy at nothing at all. 

And Mira understood the joke the district had been holding for her.  She had spent years demanding a wall that would hold joy forever; joy was not a wall, it was an exposure.  So she hung the wet negative in the lantern's throat, cut a gel to the ochre of the fragment's pear, and aimed the beam into the fog.  Fog was not a ruined wall but a wall of ten million small mirrors, and it took the picture gladly.  The display bloomed between the dead trees: dancers thirty feet tall, the pear arcing in ochre, a child's mouth wide on a laugh nobody could hear.  Doors opened all along the ruin; the fog folk came out and stood in their own remembered weather. 

"It'll fade when the lamp dies," the woman warned.  "Good," Mira said, and meant it, and was a little sad, and laughed anyway.  "Then we light it again tomorrow."  The joy was never in the keeping; it was in the relighting, the network of hands passing one picture along. 

Her tea had gone cold; the daydream unfolded and let her out.  The fragment was still in her palm, ochre and rose, warm from her hand.  She would need a hundred neighbors and one old tin, and she began, absurdly, to grin.  Outside her window the real fog stood waiting, patient as accountants, and Mira went to find her lamp.
