Odile brokered lanterns, which meant she sold other people's light and kept none of her own.  The conservatory's letter had offered forty lamps, and she had come to price them.  The Verrault Greenhouse grew only extinct plants.  Every species inside had gone quiet in the wild, and the glass held the last of them the way a decanter holds a last inch of wine.  Shadows fell in facets across the benches, sharp as cut crystal.  Anselm Reyes met her at the door with a warning.  "Don't smell anything," he said.  "I never do," Odile said.  She was aromatically cautious the way other people were careful on ice: she wafted, she never inhaled, she kept her face averted from open bottles.  Nine years ago a stranger's perfume had dropped her to her knees in a train station, and she still did not know why.  That was the real reason she had taken the job.  "I want to know why a smell can do that," she told him.  "Why tears, when a photograph does nothing."  Reyes nodded like a man who had been asked before.  "Every other sense stops at a relay station and gets translated," he said.  "Smell goes straight into memory and grief, no customs officer, no warning."  "The dead arrive before you know they've come."  Odile touched the crystal perfume vial in her coat pocket, cold as a plumb line.  It had been Ilse's, and it had been empty for nine years, and she carried it anyway.  The lamps were being sold because the greenhouse was transforming.  At midnight the grow-spectrum would go dark and the ultraviolet arrays would come up, and every remaining plant would be imaged, mapped, and rendered into data.  Then the heat would be cut, and the collection would be compost, and the archive would be the plants.  "You're keeping the record and losing the thing," Odile said.  "We're keeping what light can hold," Reyes said.  He led her to the last bench, where a single moth-flower stood in a clay pot, its petals the color of weak tea.  "Scented at dusk," he said, "and it is the only one left anywhere."  Odile leaned in and stopped a hand's width away, wafting once, the way she had trained herself.  Nothing.  Then a thin, green, honeyed thread reached her, and her eyes filled before she had decided anything at all.  "That's it," she said.  "That's what she wore."  Reyes looked at the vial she had drawn out without meaning to.  "Perfumers used it," he said quietly.  "Fixative, and the heart note."  "There were four houses that had the recipe, and then the flower went extinct, and then the perfume stopped."  So the stranger in the station had worn the last of a species, and Odile had wept for a flower and a sister at once.  Midnight came.  The warm lamps died, and the ultraviolet rose, and the greenhouse turned into a cave of blue glass.  Under the new light the moth-flower's petals showed what human eyes had never been meant to see: dark runways and landing marks, a pattern laid out for moths, glowing like a lit airfield.  Odile held the vial beneath the array.  The empty crystal was not empty: residue fluoresced along its inner walls, a pale ghost of the liquid, and on the stopper two fingerprints burned soft green where Ilse's thumb and finger had turned it, nine years and a lifetime ago.  The same green as the flower's runways.  Proof, in light, that her sister had touched something that no longer existed, and that the smell had gone straight past every guard she had built.  As the transformation completed, the cameras began their slow sweep, taking the flower apart into numbers.  Odile unstoppered the vial, put her nose to the crystal, and breathed in properly for the first time in nine years.  Almost nothing came, a whisper of honey and green, and she grieved anyway, standing in the blue dark with her shoulders shaking and no relay station to spare her.  Reyes waited, and did not tell her to stop.  Afterward she wrote him a check for the forty lamps and asked for one more thing.  He gave her a lantern from the old array, cold now, its glass still smelling faintly of the room.  She would not sell this one.  Some light you keep, she thought, because of what it once let you see.
