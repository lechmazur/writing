The cliff ladder had ninety-one rungs, and Odile's knees had stopped agreeing with all of them.  So she watched the stars from below, the way an earthbound watcher must, in the one place the sky came down to meet her.  The grotto coiled back into the headland like a sleeping serpent, and most mornings mist filled its throat.  At slack tide the pool at the far bend went utterly still, and the chimney in the roof let one round eye of sky down onto it.  There she had learned the constellations upside down, ankle-deep, breathing salt. 

The village above had kept no flame for eleven years, not since the net-lofts burned and took her father and three others with them.  Children grew up learning the shore by sound.  Odile wanted to give them the sky back, and to do it without fire.  That was the whole of her ambition, and it had taken her a decade to find a method. 

Sea glass.  The tide gave it up green and brown and rarest cobalt, already softened by forty years of tumbling.  She spread her haul on the flat rock and weighed each piece on the little brass balance she had inherited, pairing like against like.  Then she polished.  Wet sand first, then finer sand, then tripoli on a leather strop, the same patient gem polishing her father had used on the lamp's lenses.  Frost went to silk; silk went to water. 

As memories resurface when the hands are busy and the mind is not, she found herself back on the beach at midnight, eleven years ago, holding a bucket she never threw.  She had stood frozen while the lofts went up, and afterward the village had called her stillness wisdom, which was a kindness she could not accept.  She had run instead to the grotto and lain in the shallows until the pool calmed and gave her back the stars, unbothered, whole.  That was the first pearl of stillness she ever held: a quiet so complete it could carry light without consuming anything.  Now she meant to make one you could hang in a window. 

The last bead was cobalt, no bigger than a pea, and she worked it round and round until it stopped being a shard and became a drop.  Stillness was the whole trick; only a quiet surface could take the light in and hand it on without keeping any.  Held to the mist, it was reflectively vibrant, throwing back the gray morning as a soft blue flare, and it burned nothing at all. 

By the end of the week she had strung forty beads on waxed thread, each counterweighted with its twin so the whole mobile hung level and easy.  She had mapped it from the pool: the Serpent, the Hare, the long slow curve of the Fisher's Wife.  She hung it in the empty net-loft, where the roof had never been rebuilt, open to the sky. 

The evening zephyr came off the water and touched it, and the thing turned.  Moonlight struck the beads and left them again changed, and the loft filled with slow drifting points of green and gold and blue, blooming across the blackened beams.  People came up the lane holding their breath, the way you approach something that might vanish.  A child put her hand into a patch of blue and laughed to find it cold.  No smoke, no wick, no fear; only borrowed light, given back. 

Odile stood at the back with her hands still raw from the strop, and let herself weigh this night against the other one.  It did not balance, and it never would, but the scale tipped a little toward the light.  Someone asked what the lights were called, and Odile found she knew every name.  She said them out loud, and the children repeated them wrongly and beautifully, and the zephyr kept turning the beads so the names moved with the light.  Tomorrow she would go down to the grotto again, earthbound, ninety-one rungs from the sky, and gather what the tide had softened.  There was more than enough light lying around, she had decided; it only needed someone willing to sit still and polish it.
