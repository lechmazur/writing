After the campfire's last spark died, Ines raked sand over the ashes with her boot.  Only then did Keeper Marden stand, knees cracking, and lift his lantern toward the tower.  The town had paid her to sit with him through that night, and the night was nearly spent.  Ines was a conscience for hire.  Councils rented her when a decision came too easily; she wore the gray cloak of doubt so that no one else had to.  Under its hem a person could say the unsayable and be forgiven by lunch.  Her gift was being sincerely insincere: she could argue any side with a warmth that fooled even her own pulse, then set the conviction down like a borrowed coat.  The council's certainty was tidy: the Perpetua had broken on the teeth of the point, four men drowned, and Marden's lamp had been dark at the wrong hour.  At dawn they would vote to automate the light and to hang the blame on him, and they wanted it recorded that doubt had been consulted.  The stair wound up through salt smell and drafts; the tower breathed like something alive.  On the gallery, brass tubes hung from a rail, and the breeze off the water made them speak.  Ines stopped with her hand on the doorframe.  Marden's wife had cut those tubes, and as a girl Ines had learned their grammar: which chimes a man chose to hang was a sentence, and the wind only read it aloud.  She counted the gaps before the sounding ones, three gone, four left, and the four said: the fog was in my glass.  Glass meant the lamp's lens; glass also meant the eye, and he had let the wind say both.  She had known that grammar since she was nine, and had not expected him to use it on her.  Inside, he unhooked a small cloth from the lamp housing and unfolded a shard of the old reflector.  The silvering had gone out of it, so the curve held nothing: a hollow mirror shard, a bowl of lamplight with no face in it.  "Look through," he said.  She held it up and saw the lamp room through the place where her reflection should have been, and the room did not simplify.  There was the wick he had trimmed faithfully for thirty years, and there was the ledger where the council had halved his oil in spring.  There were the two letters he had written asking for help and never sent, their envelopes gone soft from handling.  "I could have said this to the fire," he told her.  "You said it to the wind," Ines answered, "because the wind is not on the council."  He almost smiled, and she felt the cloak's weight change on her shoulders.  Her contract required a finding: one sentence, weighed, delivered at the vote.  She could feel the old trick arriving, the warm sincerity that would let her call him careless and mean it beautifully for eleven minutes.  Then it would let her call the council thrifty and mean that too.  That was the whole trade: certainty rented by the hour, and nothing in the town ever had to hold two things at once.  She had come to sell them a doubt they could return; what she wanted now was harder, a doubt they would keep.  Outside, the sky went the color of the inside of a shell.  Ines reached up and unhooked the cloak's brass clasp.  Without it she was only a woman from this town who had learned to leave, and her hands were unsteady, and Marden saw them, and did not look away, which was its own kind of shelter.  She hung the gray cloak on the rail beside the chimes.  Then she unhooked the little tube that meant my and hung the cracked one that meant our.  The breeze climbed the stair and read it to everyone coming up: the fog was in our glass.  Her finding, when she wrote it on the ledger's blank page, was two sentences long, and both were true, and neither was comfortable.  Marden pressed the hollow shard into her palm and closed her fingers over it.  "For the vote," he said.  She went down bare-shouldered into the wind, carrying nothing that reflected her, and above her the chimes kept saying it, patiently, until the town arrived to hear.
