Sela moved along the dock like water finding its level, and the crates she carried seemed to weigh nothing at all.  The other stevedores called her graceful the way they called the weather cruel: something you argued with and never changed.  Nobody at Pell Harbor asked why a woman built for swimming spent eleven years hauling other people's cargo.  Under her ribs were two soft slits that the dock surgeon called a deformity and her mother had called breathing.  They only knew she was from Innisfern, the island that was not always there.  Every eleventh year the tide drew back farther than ordinary, and then you could see the island's bones.  The sublittoral fringe: kelp laid flat like combed hair, urchins socketed in stone, and a stair going down into water that never left. 

Bram had shelled that stair eleven years ago, from a gun deck, under orders.  Now he was harbormaster, and he kept his heart behind his teeth.  She had stayed eleven years to hate him, and somewhere in there the hating had turned into the only tenderness either of them could stand. 

The Quiet had come at the war's end, the way weather comes: no treaty, no reason, only the fact that a hand raised to harm another simply opened.  Powder burned, knives cut bread, and that was all.  Since violence was impossible, people fought with the truth instead, which drew more blood. 

That evening Sela set the scrimshaw on Bram's ledger.  Whalebone gone yellow as an old tooth, scrimshawed by the pirate who had loved her great-grandmother: a whale with an island on its back, and under it a row of pricked notes, the shape of a song.  Whalebone held long enough will unlock a guarded heart; that was what the bone was for, that was why pirates carved their confessions into it.  Bram picked it up because he was too proud to refuse it. 

He said, "I saw the stair go under and I logged it as a clean hit."  He said, "I have wanted to be forgiven for eleven years and I have never once asked." 

Sela's fist came up.  It opened on the way, the way every fist did now, and arrived as her palm against his cheek, and she held it there hard enough to hurt them both without harming either.  "Then ask," she said, "and don't you dare make it small." 

"Forgive me," Bram said. 

"No," she said, tenderly, still holding his face.  "Not yet.  Ask me again at the low tide." 

Dawn brought the eleventh-year ebb, and the harbor emptied like a tipped bowl.  Sela walked out over the fringe in her boots, past the flattened kelp and the urchins, to the stair.  Bram followed with the bone in one hand and her safety line in the other, because for eleven years he had been the man who held the other end. 

"You'll drown," he said.  "You've breathed air since you were nine." 

"I've been drowning since I was nine," she said.  "This is the other thing."  She had spent eleven years being useful and closed, and she was done with both. 

Then the whale called from the deep.  It came up through the rock and through the soles of her boots, a note too low for hearing, and the whalebone in Bram's hand rang like a struck bell.  The pricked notes on the scrimshaw were that call, written down by a pirate who had needed to come home. 

Rock broke the surface at the foot of the stair.  Innisfern came up streaming, black and barnacled, eleven years of dark sliding off its shoulders, and the island was there and would not be there by noon. 

Bram's fingers closed on the line. 

"Ask me now," she said. 

"Forgive me," he said, and his guarded face came apart. 

"I do," she said.  "Now let go of me." 

He could not, so she detached the line herself, unclipping it from her belt with the same grace she used on crates, and she put the loop into his hands so that he would be holding something.  "Keep the bone," she said.  "It only works if you keep saying it." 

Then she went down the stair into the cold, and her ribs opened, and the water went into her like the first honest word she had spoken in eleven years.  The island began, patiently, to disappear, taking her down with it, and Bram stood in the fringe with an empty loop and a whale's confession, saying it, saying it, to the sea.
