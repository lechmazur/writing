The first time I knocked on Mr. Halloway's door, I asked for sugar and lied about why.  I had already condemned his building in my head.  That is what the city pays me for: to walk through old places and find where they stop holding themselves up.  The sugar was an excuse to stand inside a thing I had already sentenced. 

He gave me a cupful and asked what I did for a living, and when I told him, he laughed like a man hearing a joke he had waited years for.  "Then you read buildings," he said, "badly."  He had taught dead languages for forty years: Latin, Gothic, a little Akkadian to seminarians who forgot it by summer.  He said a wall was only a slower sentence. 

The next Thursday I borrowed sugar again, and the Thursday after that.  By August the sugar was a fiction we both maintained, and the tea was real, and the lessons had begun.  He taught me to interrogate a stairwell: to tap the treads and listen for the hollow answer of a patch, to read the mortar's handwriting between bricks.  His hands were patiently nimble, arthritic and quick at once, turning pages, pressing wax, tracing a crack without touching it.  The wax came from an antique desk seal he kept beside the teapot, brass, heavy as a plum, its face cut with a mark like a bird's foot inside a circle.  He said his grandfather had cut it, a mason who signed his repairs the way scribes signed corrections, small and proud, in the margin. 

I had seen that bird's foot before.  On my grandmother's lintel across town, in a house I watched come down in an afternoon.  That is why I wanted the language: not to save anything, at first, but to stop standing in front of the last words of a house and hearing only noise. 

My report was due, and I kept finding reasons to walk the stairs again.  Everyone in the building knew the review was coming, and everyone had agreed not to know.  I had told them my visit was routine, and they had told me the cracks were old, and neither of us had asked the other to prove it.  Those were the agreed upon lies Halloway named for me, gently, over cups, the way you name a relative's illness. 

"Buildings lie constantly," he said.  "Brick pretends to be stone, a cornice pretends to carry what it never touches, and the trick is knowing which lies are load-bearing."  "And which are only courtesy," he added, and I thought of the sugar. 

The night of the meteor shower, the tenants carried chairs up to the roof garden.  Tomatoes in buckets, a fig tree in a barrel, beans climbing the water tower's legs, and the parapet Halloway had been teaching me to read all summer.  He could not manage the stairs alone anymore, so I climbed beside him, one step at a time, his hand light and sure on my shoulder. 

While the sky streaked and everyone made their wishes out loud, I took a flashlight to the parapet and interrogated it properly for the first time.  The date stone said 1889, but the mortar around it was young, and the stone itself was dressed for a different wall.  A transplant, a courtesy, a lie.  Under the coping, where nobody would ever look, I found the bird's foot in the circle, cut small and proud.  The same hand that had signed my grandmother's lintel had signed this roof. 

I sat back on my heels among the beans and felt the building turn familiar, the way a stranger's face turns familiar when they say your name.  Halloway lowered himself onto an upturned crate and waited, because he already knew what I had found. 

"You knew," I said. 

"I knew my grandfather's hand," he said.  "He rebuilt this roof after the fire, and I wanted to know whether you could read him without me pointing." 

Above us the meteors kept coming, small corrections in an old text.  The parapet was sound; the lie was only courtesy, a date carried over from the building that had stood here before the fire. 

I wrote the report the next morning: repair the cornice, repoint the south wall, retain.  It cost the city more than a demolition, and I argued it in a language I had learned at a tea table. 

On Thursdays I still knock and ask for sugar.  He still hands it over, and we both agree to call it sugar, and the seal sits between us like a third guest, its bird's foot pressed into a fresh red pool, cooling.
