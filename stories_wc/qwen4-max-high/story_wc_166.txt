The nostalgia ban never mentioned the tide.  It forbade longing, and the tide was longing given a body, so the dancers stopped.  Thessa had been the coast's most skilled tide dancer.  She had learned the art by observing gaps: the hesitation before the seventh wave, the breath a swell takes at the turn, the small silence where water forgets its own weight.  A dancer who watched the waves drowned; a dancer who watched the spaces between them could stand on the sea for eleven heartbeats.  She used the same method to leave.  The amnesty offered elective exile: sign away the city, walk out past the hooded lamps, keep your hands.  Everyone called it surrender.  Thessa read the clause again and found the gap in it, the way she found the gap in a wave.  The law could take her harbor, her teacher's ashes, her name from the rolls.  It had no clause for skin.  She signed, and did not look back; looking back was the crime, and she intended a better one. 

The exiles kept a twilight garden under stars, three terraces above the tideline where the city's hooded light could not climb.  Black fig trees, salt-burned hedges, a basin of standing water that held the whole sky without comment.  Here the stars were allowed.  Thessa worked at dusk, when the tide below ran obsidian and everything graceful in the garden was also sharp.  In her cuff she carried one dust speck ground from her teacher's moonstone cabochon.  The assessors crushed the stone on the courthouse steps to make a point about relics; she knelt and palmed the smallest ruin.  Wetted with salt, moonstone dust spontaneously anchored to living skin; no needle, no binder, no tool the law could confiscate.  That was why the old dancers had used it, and why the assessors had bothered to crush it. 

The girl Bel had been born after the nostalgia ban and had never seen anyone stand on water.  She came to the garden for the figs and stayed for the silence between Thessa's instructions.  Now she held out her forearm, and the skin was mortal, and that was the entire point.  Paper burns, stone is seized, archives are audited.  Thessa wanted to pen a cosmic verse on mortal skin: the moon's pull, the eleven heartbeats, the turning map of stars that tells a body when the water will let it stand.  A verse that would rot with the girl in sixty years was a verse no ministry could ever confiscate. 

She wetted the speck with seawater and her own spit, laid it at the wrist, and waited for the gap between Bel's pulses.  Then she wrote.  The speck caught, then crept along the salt of the girl's skin, silver-black, a hair's width, and it did not blur.  She guided it: the whole sky small, the tide's hesitation beneath it, and the girl's arm never trembled, which told Thessa she had chosen correctly.  At the elbow the verse ended, and so did the teaching.  Bel read her own arm by starlight, moved her weight, and for two heartbeats the basin's water took her feet.  Two, not eleven.  Thessa said nothing about it, and the nothing was a gift. 

The last instruction was the hard one.  The ban was not entirely wrong: a verse hoarded becomes a relic, and a relic makes a museum of a person.  So the verse had to fledge.  It had to leave the arm that carried it, enter another, and keep leaving, molting, dying, never sitting still long enough to be worshipped.  Thessa's teacher had pushed her out of the dance house at fifteen with a stone in her palm and no goodbye.  She had understood it as cruelty for thirty years.  Now, in the black garden, she understood it as a launch. 

Before dawn she walked Bel to the gap in the seaward wall, the one the exiles never repaired.  Below, the hooded lamps of the city, and the patrol's lantern swinging, and the interval between its passes.  Bel knew the interval; she had learned to watch it.  Thessa's signature barred her from that gate; the girl had signed nothing.  "Go inside and teach it," Thessa said, "and teach it again before they find the arm."  The girl went down through the gap and was gone into the dark below.  Thessa did not watch her go, and did not watch the place where she had been.  She went down to the tideline alone, found the hesitation before the seventh wave, and stood on the sea eleven heartbeats with nothing written on her.  Then she stepped down, empty, and let the water close.
