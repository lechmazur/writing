Odo swept dreams out of the labyrinth's dead ends the way other men swept leaves.  Thirty years of it: greased hinges, replaced lamps, and the soft gray drifts that sleepers shed in the blind alleys under the observatory.  Dreams clogged drains like wet lint, and he bucketed them out because nobody else would.  His knuckles split every winter from the lye, and closed every clear night when the moon came through the tower's cracked roof.  Down there, that was simply how moonlight worked, and he had stopped remarking on it. 

The astronomer Ilsa Venn had built the labyrinth to measure sorrow.  She held that grief was as weighable as salt, and she chose her unit carefully: one pin, dropped and heard in a silent corridor.  Mourners walked the maze, slept in its alcoves, and left their dreams behind; her sextant took the angles between those scattered dreams, and the angles gave a number.  Then she dropped the sextant down the tower stair, and the following week she died, and the numbers stopped.  Odo kept the broken instrument in his kit, wrapped in flannel, beside the oil can. 

He wanted what Venn had wanted, though he could not have said it in her words.  A lime tree grew in the labyrinth's center court, older than the tower, its roots buckling the corridors he patched.  His wife Sela had waited under it for him every Thursday for nineteen years, and he wanted to know what the tree had seen.  Not remembered: witnessed, held, kept in wood.  Venn's notes said the roots took an impression of everything that stood above them, and that a map of scattered dreams could read it back.  So for four winters he had plotted his bucketfuls on graph paper, one mark per dream, alley by alley, until the marks made the shape of a root system.  The map was finished; the reading was not, because reading needed the sextant's index mirror, and the mirror was still bolted into a ruined frame. 

Tonight the storm broke open and let the moon into the tower, and Odo climbed to do the taking apart.  Rain came sideways through the shutters; lightning lit the brass; then the cloud tore and the moon came down white on the floor.  He had learned the hard way what moonlight did to that instrument.  Brass healed like skin: the cracked arc knitted, the stripped threads filled, the screws he had already drawn crept home.  Three times he had gotten the mirror half free, and three times a stray beam had undone an hour.  So he worked in the cloud's shadow, under his spread coat, by the storm's blue flashes, deconstructing the thing his hands could feel and not see.  Arc off, laid in darkness.  Shade glasses off, wrapped.  Index arm off, set face down on the boards.  When the moon came again he held the mirror under his shirt and let the empty frame mend itself, greedy and pointless, like a wound closing over nothing.  One fastener remained, Venn's calibration pin, a brass sliver she had used to make her unit honest. 

He carried the mirror down the stair and set it in the center court, angled to throw moonlight across the root-shaped map.  The corridors there eat sound; the dreams see to that; Venn had built the silence on purpose.  Odo held the pin above the flagstone and let it go.  It rang.  For as long as that small bright sound lasted, the map answered it, the marks standing up in the moonlight like a tree's whole memory.  Sela, in rain, under the lime, shifting her bag to the other shoulder, pressing her thumb into the bark the way you test bread.  Nineteen years of Thursdays, and the tree had kept the smallest one. 

The number came with it, the way a weight comes with a parcel: four hundred and six pins.  He had been afraid of a figure too large to lift.  Four hundred and six he could carry, and set down, and pick up again tomorrow.  He wrote it in the maintenance log beside the lamp counts, because that was the book he had.  Then he swept the court, leaving the mirror propped for whoever came next, and stood a while in the moon with his hands open, feeling the lye cracks close.
