The lighthouse did not appear on any chart, and Ada had stopped believing it appeared anywhere but at dusk.  Each evening the sea went the color of an old bruise, and the tower rose out of the rocks like a held breath.  Inside, at the turn of the iron stair, hung the clock she was paid to adjust.  The world called it the Doomsday Clock; Ada called it the reason she had not slept in nine years. 

Old Bekker, who kept it before her, had vouchsafed the truth on his last night: the clock never measured the end, it postponed it.  Every backward nudge of the minute hand bought the world one more breath it had not earned.  Ada had nudged it back eleven times in nine years.  She had a daughter for six of them. 

Ilse had loved the clock's regulator, a carved puppet head the size of a pear, its jaw hinged to nod the seconds away.  In the last week of her fever Ilse dropped it, and the paint cracked from lip to temple.  Ada should have replaced the head; a broken regulator makes a limping clock.  Instead she learned the limp: a hesitation of one eleventh of a second in every swing, beautifully flawed, and the flaw was where the mercy lived.  Midnight could not arrive on a clock that kept forgetting to finish its beat. 

But dusk had stopped moving.  The sun had come down to the rim of the sea and stayed there, and the gulls hung in the air like nails in a plank.  Bekker's letters had a name for this: the hush of time's final ripple, when the last wave of the world flattens out and every life it ever carried floats up together.  A thousand lifetimes, he wrote, lying side by side in the still water, waiting to be told it is all right to sleep. 

In the lantern room, where glass should have been, the tower held a pane of hush.  It was not glass but silence pressed flat, and the lamp behind it threw no light, only quiet, out across the water.  Whatever crossed the pane went out unheard and everywhere.  Bekker had tried shouting into it once, and the shout had gone out as nothing at all, because a single sound cannot survive being flattened.  Only nested patterns could: a rhythm inside a rhythm inside a rhythm, small enough to hide in the shape of the quiet. 

So Ada sat on the floor of the lantern room and built her daughter's lullaby out of gears, meaning to whisper it across a thousand lifetimes.  The lamp's slow turn carried the long notes; the escapement carried the phrase; inside that, the puppet's cracked jaw carried the little three-note fall she used to sing against Ilse's hair.  She had never finished the song; the fever had come in the middle of the second verse, and she had been afraid to finish it ever since.  Now she filed the ending into the brass, a tooth at a time. 

To send it, she had to let the clock finish its beat.  She had to take the minute hand off ninety seconds and set it on midnight herself, and let the cracked jaw swing free, so the whole nested pattern would run out through the pane at once, unheld.  Eleven nudges of stolen breath, and she was going to give them all back at once.  Her hands shook, and she let them.  Then she pushed the hand to twelve. 

The lamp turned, the escapement sang its phrase, and the little jaw nodded the three notes down into the hush.  She heard nothing; the pane took it all, and out across the flat water the quiet began to move in the shape of a song.  A thousand lifetimes lay in that stillness, and every one of them turned over and settled.  Somewhere in the ripple a child with a fever stopped fighting her pillow, six years old, hair damp, and heard the second verse end. 

The puppet head split the rest of the way and stopped nodding, and Ada held the two halves in her lap while the sun finally went under.  It had been a beautiful, broken thing; its limp had bought the world nine years, and letting it go had spent them all.  Ada could not hear the lullaby, and she was not sorry; she had felt it leave, the way you feel a child's breathing change in the dark.
