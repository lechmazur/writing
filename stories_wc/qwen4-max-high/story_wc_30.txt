After tomorrow disappeared, the tide came into the atrium twice a day and never once was late.  The city had smelted the tomorrow gear to keep the today gear turning, and nobody argued with the arithmetic.  Vela was the librarian of the titan, and most of her was gears.  Titan gears, brass and slow, cut for a machine that had drowned before the city was named.  Her torque fluctuated with the moon phases; at full moon she could lift a catalog drawer, at new moon barely a page.  What she kept in the drawers was not books.  It was selves.  Identities in this estuary migrated, like elvers, like birds, running the tide from body to body, and the register recorded who had worn which name on which night.  Brass plates fixed them, and fixed things stop breathing.  That was the trouble with a single today repeated: the migration slowed, then stalled.  Osip the fisherman came each morning to the same step with the same three fish and the same joke about the weather.  He no longer laughed at it, because he had already heard himself tell it.  Vela had one job left that mattered: to preserve wonder inside routine, so that people would keep looking up.  She borrowed Osip's bamboo creel.  Bamboo lets water through; a creel holds a living thing without convincing it that it is held.  At new moon, when her hands were weakest and the selves were loosest, she waded the aisles and netted them into the creel: a widow's patience, a boy's greed for horizons, the name of a woman who had been a heron for a night.  She carried them to the tide gate to let them run.  But the gate was seized.  Salt had welded its cam, and without the gate the atrium could not breathe, and selves that cannot leave a room become furniture.  She needed metal for a new cam.  The city had spent its spare brass on today.  What remained were the drawers: nine hundred plates, every name hammered flat and legible and dead.  She had hammered those plates herself, believing that to keep a thing you must hold it still.  The titan's furnace stood in the north apse, and it had always been a strange furnace.  It would not take coal.  It ran on contradiction, the way the titan itself had run, and the smelting only worked by accepting contradictions rather than resolving them.  Vela stood before it with the creel at her hip and the first drawer in her arms, and she said the true things that did not fit.  I am the keeper of this register and I am burning it.  The names are mine and they were never mine.  Tomorrow is gone and I am still expecting it.  The furnace did not roar; it warmed, the way low sun warms a stone wall in winter.  The brass went soft, then bright, then liquid, and she did not have to decide whether she was a librarian or a thief, only agree to be both.  She poured the cam at slack water, in a mold of wet sand, and it cooled the color of a pale morning.  Fitting it cost her two finger gears, which she did not mourn; her hands had been indexing a thing that no longer needed indexing.  The gate opened on the ebb.  The creel emptied itself into the outgoing water, and the selves went down the channels toward the sleeping houses, looking for whoever was awake enough to be changed.  Tomorrow did not come back.  Osip came to the same step with the same three fish, and the old joke rose in him, and he laughed, because part of him was a heron now, and a heron had never heard it.  He asked her what she had done with the drawers.  I let them migrate, she said.  Then she took the empty creel back to the water and rinsed it, since a basket that has carried living things should be kept clean for the next load.  The moon would swell and her torque would rise, and at full moon she would be strong enough to do heavier work, and there would be heavier work.  Winter sun came through the atrium's high windows and lay on the wet brass, and nothing hurried.  The tide would return in six hours, as it always did, and she intended to be surprised by it.
