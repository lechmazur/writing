The tide went out like a breath held too long and finally let go, and the hunger stone rose from the water.  Every islander knew its carvings: famine years, wreck years, and beneath them, in a hand nobody living had written, the names of those the sea would take next.  Aurel Voss had read his daughter's name there in the spring, three weeks before the boat went down.  He had read his own beside it, dated for the coming winter.  He was a clockmaker, and he had spent forty years being neatly inventive in the wrong direction.  His shop was full of counterclockwise clocks, escapements cut backward, hands sweeping widdershins over faces numbered the wrong way round.  Widows bought them.  They ran beautifully and they undid nothing; a mirror of a river is not a river running home.  So he walked out across the exposed flats to the lighthouse, which stood on a stack of black rock and had no keeper, no oil, and no fire, and had burned every night for two hundred years.  Its lamp was a stone.  Its beam swept clockwise, and each low tide the hunger stone came up freshly carved, and the island called this the design of things and went on mending nets.  Aurel called it a mechanism, because a mechanism has a maker, and a maker can be argued with. 

In the tower's dry cellar he found the salvage chest the wreckers left: coins, a sextant, and a thing of two terracotta discs on a bronze axle, wound with a cord gone brown as blood.  A child's yoyo, older than the lighthouse, older than the language of the names.  He took it apart on the cellar floor, and being who he was, he did it neatly: cord coiled in a dish, axle laid parallel to the seam of the flagstones, every measurement written in a small square hand.  The puzzle took him nine tides.  The groove in the axle was not a groove but a slip, a clutch that let the discs sleep at the bottom of the fall, spinning free, holding everything they had gathered on the way down.  One tug and the sleep broke, and the fall climbed back up the string into the hand.  That was the thing his backward clocks had never understood: you do not reverse a fall by turning the dial the other way.  You let it fall all the way, and you store it, and then you pull. 

The hunger stone was the same kind of device.  He had known it since childhood, when his mother said the stone was hungry, and had never once asked what it ate.  It ate light.  Two centuries of the lamp's clockwise sweeping had been fed down into it, and it held that light latent, the way a spun yoyo holds a fall, and the carving was only the receipt.  Latent radiance, he wrote in his book, and underlined it twice, and his hands shook so much the second line wandered.  He built the clutch into the tower's turning gear in four days, bronze and terracotta and a cord plaited from rigging that had come home without her.  Then he waited for the water to leave. 

Under the slow exhale of low tide the flats came up streaming and grey, and the hunger stone stood bare with his name on it and his winter beside it.  He put his hand on the cord.  He understood the price the way you understand a step in the dark: the stone would give back everything it had swallowed, and the ledger would go with it, every famine, every wreck, every name.  She would not come back.  But nothing would be written for him either, no winter, no design, and he would stand outside fate's whole neat machinery with his own hand on the string. 

He initiated the return.  The tug was small, a wrist's flick, the neat gesture of a man who had wound ten thousand clocks.  Above him the lamp went out, and then the stone let go of two hundred years.  Light came up out of the rock like water finding its level, cold and enormous, and the beam began to sweep the other way, counterclockwise, unwriting.  The carvings softened, blurred, ran off the stone like sand.  His daughter's name went, and his own went after it, and he stood in the terrible bright with nothing owed and nothing promised, laughing, unauthored, alone, while the tide turned and came in to cover a stone that was only a stone.
