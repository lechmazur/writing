Mist fills the valley to the brim each dawn, and the tea house floats on it.  The rock beneath it is pumice, hollow as a bone, and it rides the warm breath off the river until its three chains pull taut against the cliff.  I climbed the pegged stair six months after receiving the donor heart, when my chest had healed enough to bear the altitude.  Sova, the dye master, was already at her table, watching the mist the way other people watch a road.  She had a ledger open, and a blue morpho wing under a square of glass.  "You are the one who got the courier's heart," she said, not looking up.  I said yes, and told her about the four strangers who had wept their lives out to me on the switchbacks that month.  "And you told them nothing," she said, and it was not a question.  This was the thing I had come to ask.  Mira, the courier, had carried gossip and grain between the valley towns, and people had leaned into her like plants toward a window.  Now they leaned into me, and I wanted to know why some people magnetize stories, and whether it could be learned or only inherited.  Sova poured tea the color of wet bark and told me to wait for the light.  She had spent forty years tracking sun trails: notching the rock's rim where the sun crested each ridge, marking the floor with charcoal lines that crept a fingerwidth a week.  A dyer who judges cloth at noon sells lies, she said; color is an angle, not a substance.  So she knew to the hour when the sun would duck under the mist and come in flat across the table.  Until then she watched me, and her watching was not idle.  Sova was quietly explosive; she spoke low and moved little, and every sentence she set down went off inside you a beat later, like a stone dropped down a well.  "You want the wing to be blue," she said.  "It is not blue."  "There is no blue dye in it, no pigment at all, only stacked shelves of chitin with air between them."  "The blue is made of the gaps."  I looked at the wing, brown as a dead leaf in the gray light, and felt my borrowed heart knock once, hard.  "The morpho flashes to be seen and then to be gone," she said.  "Iridescence is caution: it gives the bird something dazzling to chase, and the butterfly goes on living somewhere the blue was not."  Then the sun came under the mist.  It slid along a charcoal line she had drawn eleven days before and struck the glass, and the wing detonated.  Blue rose out of nothing, a blue with no substance in it, and it illuminated her face, the steam, the scar showing above my collar.  For a moment the whole floating rock seemed to hang inside that color, chains and all.  "Mira dyed for me one summer," Sova said.  "She had no stories of her own to tell, and she never gave advice."  "She left a gap the shape of a person, and people filled it."  Then the light slid past the line, the wing went brown, and her sentence went off in me.  I had come up here hoping the heart was a vessel, that Mira's gift sat inside it like pigment in a jar, waiting to be poured into me.  Instead: air between shelves.  Instead: a scar down my sternum that strangers could see, and a silence in me shaped exactly like a question.  "So it can be learned," I said.  "It can be practiced," she said, "which is harder, because you have to keep the gap open and take nothing for yourself."  "Caution, of a shimmering kind: let them see the flash, never the whole butterfly."  Below us the mist thinned and the valley came up in pieces, terraces and slate roofs and the river doing its slow silver work.  The rock sank a little on its chains as the air cooled.  A woman climbing the pegged stair with a basket of tea leaves stopped at our table to catch her breath, and looked at my scar, and did not look away.  I did not tell her about the heart, or the wing, or Mira.  I moved my cup aside and left the space open, and she sat down and began.
