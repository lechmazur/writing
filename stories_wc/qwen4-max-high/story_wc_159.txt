Above the clouds, the opal library takes the dawn before the world does.  Its nine galleries are the museum of extinct feelings, ordered by the year each feeling was last felt, and the caretaker of glass orchids walks them backward, newest to oldest, lamp to lamp, gently systematic.  Each orchid was blown around the name of the last person ever to feel what the case beneath it describes.  Warm glass breathes, so she lights the lamps to keep the names from going unsaid.  The opal walls throw blue and rose down the stairwell, and the orchids drink the color and give back sound.  The marvels still work: the aurora loom, the rain-organ, the tide-clock.  No one has ridden the funicular up in eleven years; deserted marvels keep perfect time for nobody, and she keeps it with them. 

At the stairhead hangs the chime the builders left: seven bamboo tubes cut from their scaffolding, seven notes for nine orchids, so two share.  She trues every orchid against it, because glass sags over centuries and a sagging name becomes noise.  The fourth tube is cracked.  Frost split it two winters back; its note has slid flat, so she has stopped truing the two orchids that answer to it, and both have begun to cloud.  One is the oldest orchid here, holding a girl four centuries dead.  The other holds her own name. 

The oldest case is labeled with a feeling she can read about but never had: the gladness of being called for.  She blew her own and trued it to the fourth tube, the winter her daughter stood at the funicular calling her down by name.  She felt the summons and none of the gladness, only the hook in it, and she could not bear the hook.  A name in glass cannot reach you; that was the point.  The car went down empty and has not come back up. 

Foxglove grows in a crack of the opal stair, wind-seeded from below, the only living thing at this altitude.  A measured bite of its leaf steadies her heart in the thin air; a second bite would stop it.  She is as careful with her regret as with everything else, and she doses it daily. 

No bamboo grows above the clouds, so the cracked tube cannot be replaced; its true note must be found somewhere else.  It survives in one place: under the dome of the oldest gallery, where the echo of the glassblowers' breath-song still circles.  They sang seven notes to time their gathers at the furnace, and the words are gone, and the tune has worn thinner for four hundred years, the way a stair wears. 

For the first time in eleven years she leaves the other lamps unlit and goes straight down.  Under the dome she stands in the dark with her mouth open, waiting for the fourth note to come around.  It comes, thin as a hair, and she takes it into her throat.  An echo can be carried but never copied; spent once, it is spent.  One breath, two orchids, nine flights between them.  She could climb with it and save the sound of herself.  Instead she kneels in the oldest gallery, where the last of the gladness is hanging. 

She lights the oldest lamp, warms the glass until it softens, and breathes the note into it.  The flower clears and says, plainly, a name no living mouth has said in four hundred years: Anneke.  Then the echo is out of her, and she climbs the nine flights humming anyway, finding nothing in her throat but the shape of her own voice. 

At the stairhead her own orchid has clouded to the color of a fingernail.  She lights it, and it makes the sound weather makes.  Below the clouds her daughter may still be saying it; the glass no longer knows how.  She lights the other eight, newest to oldest, because the order is what she has.  In the ledger she writes nine lamps, one truing, one loss, and signs the blank she has left for her name since the winter of the empty car.  Then she takes her measured bite of foxglove: bitter, beautiful, exactly enough to keep the heart going, and she stands at the rail watching the cloud take the light.  Tomorrow, nine lamps, left to right, oldest last.
