The last petal fell on Tuesday, and on Wednesday Aurel Venn came back to Ashfield with a satchel and a forgery he had made himself.  He was a historian of things that had never happened.  The Ministry paid him to compose founding myths, and he was good at it, because a lie that heals is built from the same beams as a truth that hurts.  Nine years ago he had written the Ashfield Charter, which said that the forty-one men and boys lost in the Number Three shaft had gone down singing.  It was a noble lie, and it worked: the company kept its license, the town kept its pride, and the widows kept their pensions.  His brother Tomas was the youngest of the forty-one.  Aurel had not wept at the funeral; he had counted the coffins twice, and then counted the ventilation shafts on the company plan, and found one where the plan promised three.  Grief, in him, arrived as arithmetic, and he had never learned to distrust it for that. 

The committee met in the old railway station, the last room in Ashfield the town still owned.  Ivy had come through the ticket window and gone up the departures board, so that the trains now left for nowhere, leaf by leaf.  They were voting to demolish it on Monday, now that the rose was done, and with it the strongroom under the parcel office, where the shaft records sat in a pit of paper the company called an archive.  Aurel asked to speak before the vote. 

He set the Charter on the stationmaster's desk and opened it to the illuminated letter he had painted with his own hand, a great gold T wound about with ivy.  Forty-one leaves.  He had put them there the winter after the funeral, one for each name, and told himself it was ornament.  Then he pointed at the wall behind them, where the real ivy had swallowed the memorial plaque, and asked the committee to look at both vines and say which one was lying. 

He worked as he always worked, by drawing parallels.  He read his own sentence aloud: "the earth took them in her own good time."  Then he read the instructions the company had sent with the commission, dated three weeks before he began, which used the same nine words.  Then he read a parish chronicle from 1490, a village drowned by a lord's broken dam, where the priest had written that the water took them in its own good time.  Five hundred years apart, the same soft hands closing over the same hard fact.  "That is my trade," he said.  "We do not invent lies; we inherit them, and dress them in gold." 

Mrs. Alder, whose husband was number nineteen, asked why he had written it at all.  "Because they told me the pensions depended on the company surviving, and because I wanted my brother to have died for something instead of nothing."  "I called that mercy, and mercy is only a lie that has been loved long enough."  His voice did not break, but his hands did, shaking so that the gold leaf threw light across the ceiling; he waited for them to be still, and went on. 

"The strongroom holds the ventilation orders," he said.  "They will show one shaft where three were promised, and they will show that the company knew, and you can read them before the machines come."  "Then we can recapture the station, and the forty-one, and the truth I buried under my own beautiful letter."  "I have spent nine years gilding the lid of a pit," he said, "and I am asking you to help me lift it." 

They voted eleven to two to keep the walls standing until the strongroom was emptied.  The company's site agent, outnumbered, gave up the keys.  Mrs. Alder carried the Charter out to platform two, where the old rosebush his mother had planted stood bare against the brick, its season finished, its last petal somewhere in the gravel.  She held the gold T up against the ivy on the wall and counted the leaves aloud, forty-one, and then began on the names.  Aurel counted with her, and for the first time in nine years he let himself cry while he did it, because the numbers had finally stopped being a place to hide.  By Thursday they had the strongroom open, and the truth came up out of the pit smelling of coal and damp, and it was ugly, and it was theirs.
