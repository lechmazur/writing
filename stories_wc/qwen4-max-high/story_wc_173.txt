In Aumry it snowed only while the towers sang, as if the sky needed permission.  Vessa watched the silver courtyard fill from the window of the House of Hours, where she was the most expensive courtesan in the city and the only one who had never been touched.  Her clients did not buy her body.  They bought the bodily poetic, the old discipline in which meter is carried by wrist and shoulder and a poem is spoken by a spine.  She had not said a word aloud since she was eleven.  The Concord could harvest a voice; it had never learned to harvest a gesture.  So she kept everything she was in her hands, and rented out the recitations, and stayed whole.  Below her stood the prism sanctuary: twelve feet of roofless glass, open to starlight, holding the harvest of echoes.  Every winter the wardens went house to house and gathered what people had almost said.  The refusal swallowed at a father's bedside.  The name of a god said only inside the teeth.  The word no, softened into a cough.  The glass refracted them all into harmless light, and the city slept, and the city smiled.  Among those echoes was one thin blue thread that had been her mother's.  Her mother had refused something once, quietly, and the quiet had been taken out of her, and she had lived twenty years afterward as a very pleasant woman.  Vessa remembered the shape of her mother's song and not the sound of it.  Tonight she meant to unlock the sanctuary.  Not by force: glass that has swallowed a city does not break, it only tightens.  The lock was a knot, every echo tied as a thread, thousands snarled into one heart of glass.  It would open only by untangling threads, in the order they had been lived, and one impatient pull would cinch it shut forever.  A thief would have failed in a minute.  A woman whose hands could hold a meter for nine hours without hurrying might not.  She went down barefoot, dressed in white, so the snow would not know her from itself.  The wardens watched from the four gates with their lanterns and their patient faces.  They saw a courtesan kneeling at the glass with her hands inside the knot, moving like slow water, and they were not alarmed.  Nothing in Aumry had been alarming for a hundred years, and crossing the snow to stop her sounded like effort.  That was the thing she had come to break: not the glass, but the calm.  She meant to defy the lullaby of conformity, which forbade nothing.  It only made refusal feel like a chore and obedience feel like rest, and it sang all night, and under it the snow came down thin enough to let the stars through.  She worked in the meter of the poems she had never spoken.  A laugh a boy had swallowed at a funeral came loose on a downbeat and went up out of the roofless glass as a line of pale light.  Then a woman's refusal to say yes at her wedding.  Then a whole street's worth of names.  Her fingers ached and her shoulders burned, and she did not hurry, and the not hurrying was the hardest discipline of her life.  Toward dawn the wardens stopped smiling, which was the first frightening thing that had happened in that courtyard in a century.  They did not draw weapons; they had forgotten how; they simply stood in the snow with their lanterns, serene and beginning to be afraid.  One thread remained, thin and blue.  She could have pulled it out and kept it, one small voice for her own house, and the knot would have closed on everyone else.  Instead she waited for the last note.  The towers stopped.  All across Aumry, people let out the long sigh that ends the lullaby, and had not yet arrived at the smile.  Between sigh and smile there is a moment when a person belongs to nobody, and she had built nine years of her life to fit inside it.  She untangled the blue thread and let it go with all the rest.  The sanctuary opened like a hand.  The echoes came out under the failing stars and went home to their owners, and the snow carried the sound the way snow carries everything, without comment.  In a warm room across the city, a very pleasant woman heard her own refusal come back to her, and stopped being pleasant.  The wardens listened, and their faces came apart, quietly, in the snow.  Vessa stood in the silver courtyard, untouched, unspeaking, and lifted her hands to begin the first line of something nobody had commissioned.
