The hydraulics failed in October, and after that the great dish sat locked at eleven degrees, staring at a piece of sky nobody needed.  Sela stayed at the signal station because someone had to, and because the road down led to a valley that was no longer there.  They called her a patchwork navigator, and it was fair.  Her charts were sewn from what the departing columns left behind: a fisherman's tide table, a schoolgirl's route to a well, a bus timetable gone soft at the folds.  She stitched the fragments into one country and steered people through it.  In November the birds came back north.  Geese over the station in December, warblers threading the frost, all of them flying the wrong way, home to a place that had drowned.  Then the people began to return the same way, uphill out of the south, and Sela understood that the world had put itself into reverse.  The reverse migration fluctuated with moon phases; she had eleven months of tallies to prove it.  At the full moon the road filled with walkers, and at the dark it emptied to nothing but wind.  Nobody knew why, and Sela stopped needing to know.  Her brother Teo had gone south in the first column, against her advice, carrying their mother's bronze censer.  In April a package had come back up the line with no letter, only the censer's foot, snapped off at the stem, green with patina, heavy as a fist.  She kept it on the chart table to hold down the corner where the valley used to be.  The copper phone still worked; the exchange ran on batteries and stubbornness.  It rang at midnight, and only at midnight, and the voice was Teo's, and it never said her name.  "Nine degrees off your north."  "Count the green foot twice."  "Full, not dark."  Their old flood cipher, the one they had invented at nine years old under a blanket, when the water first came.  Each call gave her a bearing and a distance, and each bearing plotted a line coming up out of the south.  He was walking home, and he was telling her where to put the light.  So she built.  With the hydraulics dead she could not tilt the dish, so she strung a wire array from the mast to the graveyard fence, cut and re-cut the lengths by his numbers, and hung the censer foot as a plumb bob to true the mast against the stars.  The bronze swung, green and steady, and the wire sang when she got it right.  She worked under a sky so starlit the snow threw shadows.  The beacon she built was ugly, a lamp in a tin drum, but it could be seen from the ford nine miles south.  On the last call he said, "Not for me."  Then the line gave her the hum it gives when there is nothing at the far end.  She sat with the receiver until the batteries sagged, saying bearings into it anyway, the way you say a name to a grave.  Grief, she had learned, was a country with no coast, and you could not sail out of it; you could only learn its passes and its water.  That was the whole of her work now: to navigate the geography of loss, one patch at a time, so that whoever came next would not walk it blind.  The moon filled.  On the night it was full she lit the drum, and the reverse migration came up the road: forty, then sixty, then more, coats over pajamas, a man carrying a door.  They came into the light and stood breathing hard, and she read them the bearings, one by one, like names.  Teo was not among them, and she had not expected him, and she looked anyway.  A girl with frost in her braids asked what the green thing was, and Sela said, "A foot from my mother's censer," and let her hold it, and the child held it like a bird.  Afterward she sewed a blank square into the chart where his line ended, and hung the green foot back on the wire.  The moon would empty and fill again, and the road would empty and fill, and she would be here with the lamp, saying the numbers into the dark until somebody answered.
