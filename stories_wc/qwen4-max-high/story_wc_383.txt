Mira had never believed the garden was haunted, only tuned.  That was the skeptic in her, and she wore it like a coat.  Still, every twilight she walked the velvet garden, where the moss grew thick enough to swallow footsteps and the dusk-flowers opened their black throats to hum.  The hum was the archive.  Not shelves, not books: resonance structures, lattices of grown quartz that held a memory the way a struck bell holds a note.  Her brother had called them singing bones, and she had laughed at him, and she had loved him for saying it.  Teodor was quarantined now.  Not his body; his body had finished dying eleven years ago.  His memory was the contagion.  He had been tragically radiant, the kind of bright that made strangers follow him down streets they had not meant to walk, and when he burned out, everyone who had ever held him in mind caught the same fever of missing.  So the keepers sealed him into the lattice, and the town stopped dreaming of him, and Mira, who was keeper then and keeper still, kept the quarantine intact.  Everyone but herself.  A keeper is permitted one leak.  Hers was half a seashell, pale as a fingernail, that whispered.  The other half sat in the deepest structure, and the two halves wanted each other across the garden, the way paired shells do.  She told herself the whisper was interference, a standing wave, her own grief bouncing back with an accent.  She told herself this every evening, and every evening she pressed the shell to her ear anyway, charmed past her own objections.  Tonight the whisper had changed.  It said her name, and then it said, patiently, you are the only room I have left.  Mira sat down on the velvet moss and understood, with the cold clarity of a woman who has never once fooled herself for long, that quarantine had not been medicine.  It had been a bedroom she refused to leave.  She had built her whole life around a locked door and called it devotion.  Isolation, she thought, is not the same as standing on your own.  One is a cell; the other is a spine.  The structures could only be rewritten in the hinge of the night, when the last star fades into morning light and the lattices slacken, hungry for a new note.  She had the length of one night, and she spent it walking the rows, saying him out loud to the quartz.  His laugh, which was too loud for churches.  The way he peeled an orange in one unbroken ribbon.  The fever, the terrible brightness at the end, the hand that would not stop reaching.  She did not soften any of it; an archive that lies is only a haunting with better manners.  This was the work she had trained for and never once performed on herself: to archive a quarantined memory through resonance structures, moving it out of the body that hoarded it into quartz that could hold it without catching fire.  The town would still be safe.  She would simply be able to visit him, the way you visit a grave you no longer sleep in.  The sky went gray, then the color of watered milk.  One star held on above the eastern hedge, stubborn as she was.  Mira set the whispering half against the lattice's open seam.  She felt the pull go through her wrist, through her teeth, through the eleven years.  When the last star faded into morning light, the two halves met, and the note resolved, and the garden stopped humming and simply sang.  Then it was quiet.  The shell she took back was only calcium now, a broken thing, ordinary and light.  She waited to be devastated.  Instead she stood up, and her knees hurt, and she was hungry, and she was one person in a garden, which is a different number than two but is not zero.  "I don't believe you're in there," she told the lattice, and her voice did not shake.  "I believe I loved you, and that it is mine, and that I can carry it."  The quartz gave back one soft chord, agreeing, not answering.  Mira brushed the velvet moss from her skirt and walked out of the garden gate to buy bread, alone, upright, and unaccompanied by any ghost she had not chosen.
