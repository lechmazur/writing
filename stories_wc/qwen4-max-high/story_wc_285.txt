The fog came up off the river in flecks, like spray that had forgotten to fall.  Below Gloom Hill the fog-bound crossing lay quiet, the water running wide and unhurried, the ferry chained to its post until the weather chose otherwise.  Every night the Gloom Hill coachman brought his last load down the switchbacks and waited at the toll house with whoever needed crossing.  The rule he gave every passenger was the same: we wait until rain gutters stop ticking.  The toll house smelled of wet wool and lamp oil, and its stove had a way of making everyone honest. 

He was a quiet, heavy man, closely attentive in a way that made strangers say more than they meant to.  He never asked questions.  He worked through ambient awareness, letting the whole room percolate: the kettle's small complaint, a boot heel turning, the rain gutters ticking into the barrel by the door.  What rose to the top was always the essence.  People came down the hill with an hour of tangled grievance and left holding one sentence.  "You are not angry at your son," he told a drover once.  "You are afraid of your own hands."  They called it a gift; he called it arithmetic, the patient cancelling of everything that did not matter.  It was not kindness, exactly; it was hunger.  He had become good at it because he could not do it for himself. 

Inside his hat, on the linen band, his wife had stitched in red wool: Wait for me at the crossing.  Eleven years ago she went over the far bank to nurse her mother, and the fever took her there instead.  Before she died she sent the band back, and over the red she had sewn, in gray: Do not wait.  She had not cut the old thread out.  Both vows showed, one ghosting through the other, a palimpsest promise he could not read twice the same way.  Her yarn needle, blunt as a whisper, still rode in the band beside the words.  In eleven years he had never once crossed the river.  He drove down, he waited, he drove back up, and the hill kept its name. 

Tonight there was one passenger, a girl with a seed tin on her knees and her grandmother's ring on a string.  She talked a long while about the orchard, the buyer, the fairness of it, the price.  He listened to the kettle and the gutters and to the places where her breathing stopped.  When the tin rattled he said, "You are not selling trees."  "You are asking whether leaving is a kind of theft."  She was quiet, and then she nodded, and her shoulders came down off the hill.  "It is not," he said. 

"And you?" she asked.  "What are you waiting for?" 

He took off his hat.  He slid the yarn needle under a gray stitch and lifted, the way she had taught him to lift a knot without breaking the cloth.  Red beneath: Wait for me.  Gray above: Do not wait.  For eleven years he had tried to choose which line was the true one, and the choosing was the tangle.  He cancelled it now the way he had cancelled the drover's anger and the girl's orchard.  Strip the wool of its grammar and both lines said one small thing: come across.  She was on the far bank now, and had been for eleven years, and the verbs were only wool.  She had not left him two orders.  She had left him one, said twice, the way you say a thing twice to a man slow to hear.  The girl watched him do it, and did not ask him to explain. 

The gutters slowed, ticked once, and stopped.  Out on the water the fog went up in spindrift, thin and bright, and the ferryman began to haul the chain.  The coachman paid two fares, which he had never done.  He put the yarn needle into the girl's palm, blunt end first, the way you hand someone a tool and not a weapon.  The band went back into his hat with both vows intact. 

Gloom Hill stood behind him, its lamps going out one by one.  The far bank, which had been a word for eleven years, turned out to be a place with mud on it.  He stepped into the mud, and it held him.
