The orchard grew in the lighthouse's lee, twelve apple trees espaliered against brick the lamp kept warm, and below them the floes went grinding south like a city rearranging its streets.  Vess walked the rows at dusk, counting windfalls, her shadow arriving a half second after she did.  The salt burn had laced her with dark, seams of it up her arms and throat, and the floe clans called her the peacekeeper the way you name a weather.  She had never minded; a weather does not argue, it arrives and things adjust.  In her coat pocket rode a fragment of the arbiter's crystal ball, an edge still sharp enough to open her thumb.  Three winters back she had read its glimpse of a man named Orr reaching for something at his belt, and she had broken his jaw before he could draw it.  It was a splint.  His daughter's arm was broken, and he had been walking to the orchard to ask for a straight branch.  The oil house at the orchard's foot had become a vintage store, salvage from drowned Halloway: brass beds, spectacles, forty mirrors the divers brought up wrapped in sailcloth.  The ball had fallen from the table in that very room, back when it held the arbiter's bench, and shattered, and Vess had never told anyone she was glad.  Old silver keeps light the way old wood keeps smoke.  The shopkeeper, Anse, let Vess stand among them, and she learned that a shattered scrying glass spills its glimpses into whatever mirrors are near.  So she went mirror to mirror with the fragment in her palm, and each one gave back a sliver of what the ball had once shown.  Not futures, she understood now, but pauses: the weighted breath before a reply, when a person is still two people at once.  In a shaving mirror she watched Orr's breath again and saw what she had refused to see: his eyes were wet, his hand was shaking, and neither of those is a weapon.  Glimpse by glimpse the fragment thickened, glass growing where the light went in, until on the last morning of winter she reconstituted the sphere whole in a basin of orchard soil, to learn whether it had ever once been right.  One mirror held no glimpse at all, only a memory.  It showed a woman on the ice handing her water skin to the man who had killed her brother, and no fragment answered, because the ball reckoned only the likely, and courage is an anomaly.  Anse called it a flaw in the silver.  Vess called it the only honest glass in the room.  The thaw brought the dispute up the cliff: a floe boy named Ket had cut the lighthouse boat's mooring, and the orchard men wanted his hands for it.  The boat was gone; so, since dusk, was his brother.  Vess stood between them with the sphere whole in her pocket, warm as a held egg.  She had asked the question already: why the cable, Ket.  The sphere showed her his answer the way it always had, the likely shape of the next breath: the boy lunging, the men closing, the ice taking him.  Her shadow gathered at her heels, ready, the way it had gathered before Orr.  But she was calmly intuitive, and intuition is not prophecy; it is attention that has stopped being afraid.  She saw the rope burn across his palms, fresh, and the salt frozen in his lashes, and a boy who had been pulling on something for hours.  In the weighted breath before his reply, she stopped waiting for the glass to tell her and mapped what she felt onto what she could do.  She took the lamp key from her neck and put it in his cut hands.  Swing the beam onto the floes, she said, and tell me which lead your brother went into.  The orchard men shouted about the boat.  He did not lunge.  He said, the third one past the black rock, and his voice broke on it, and that was the anomaly the glass could never hold: a boy choosing to be helped.  Vess drew the lace out of her arms, the dark she had been ashamed of, and went down with him to lay it across the lead as a line.  In her pocket the sphere went cold and cracked along an old seam, and she let it.  They found the brother at midnight, blue and breathing, under the beam Vess had turned.  She kept one fragment, sharp enough to open a thumb, to remind her that a pause is not a verdict, and that she is the one who fills it.
