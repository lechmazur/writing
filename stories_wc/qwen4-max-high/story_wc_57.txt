The fog came in over the harbor the way it always did here, without wind, without hurry, erasing the far breakwater first and then the boats.  Under the snow, the dock still showed its gold.  Two centuries of pine tar and beeswax had gilded the planks, and the flakes landing on them melted into bright coins.  Maren sat on a bollard with her hands inside her sleeves and waited. 

She was a seed collector, which is to say she was infinitely patient, which is to say she had learned that almost nothing worth keeping can be hurried.  In her satchel: four hundred paper envelopes, each holding a grain or a bean or a bitter little pip that some dead woman had chosen to save.  Seeds were easy.  You could carry a seed away in your pocket and it would still be itself a hundred years later.  Methods were not.  A method lived only in hands, and hands died. 

That was why she had come back to this dock for nine winters, to coax an old fisherman named Eilert into letting her read his rope.  His grandmother had been one of the secret weavers.  When the guilds burned the women's pattern books, the weavers had not stopped weaving; they had simply moved their knowledge into the cordage itself, where a customs man saw only rope.  Flax retted in seawater, the count of the twist, the sowing calendar for the barley that grew on this cold coast: all of it knotted in, hidden in plain use, hauled up wet every morning for eighty years.  Half the barley in her satchel was useless without that calendar, and the last woman who had known it by heart died before Maren was born. 

Eilert came down the steps at slack tide, as he always did, carrying the coil on his shoulder like something asleep.  He did not greet her.  He set the rope between them on the gold planks and looked out at the fog, which had taken the harbor mouth now and was working inward, quiet as a held breath. 

Maren did not ask.  She took from her pocket a piece of fulgurite glass, a hollow grey tube no longer than her finger, and laid it beside the coil.  Lightning had struck the dune where she was gathering marram seed, and the sand had remembered it in glass.  Eilert's hand went still. 

"My mother had one of those sewn into the eye of her net," he said.  "A marker," Maren said.  "Where the reading starts." 

He turned the coil until he found it: a bead of the same grey glass, worn soft, threaded at the head of the rope.  Then, because she had waited nine winters and never once raised her voice, he let her touch it. 

She began at the bead and moved outward, reading with her thumbs.  A knot turned left was a no; turned right, a yes; the spacing between them counted the days.  She spoke it slowly, decoding the knot patterns aloud so that the old man could correct her, and he did, twice, and the second time his voice cracked.  "Nine days in the sea water," he said.  "Not eight.  My grandmother sang eight but her hands did nine." 

The fog reached the dock and stood around them, and the snow came down into it without sound, and the water under the planks lifted and set itself down again.  Maren had her notebook open, but she found she was not writing.  Eilert's hands had begun to move on their own, retting, twisting, counting, keeping time with the rope, and his mouth had begun to name what they did.  The method was coming up out of him like a tide through sand. 

She understood then that she had been wrong about what she came to take.  A rope in a museum case is only a rope.  What she had come to save was not the thing but the verb.  She closed the notebook and coaxed him onward with questions a child might ask, and he answered them for an hour, until the fog thinned and the breakwater came back. 

"Bring the girl from the chandlery tomorrow," he said at last.  "She has hands." 

Maren left the fulgurite on the gold plank beside the coil, a marker for where the reading starts, and went up the steps with her seeds and her patience, taking nothing that could not be grown again.
