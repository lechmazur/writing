The outage came at ten to five, and the city's lights sagged, browned, and rose again like something breathing in its sleep.  Under the Ashgrove branch library, in the crawl space, Ivo felt the boiler stop through his palms.  Eleven years down there had taught him to read the building by touch and smell, the way other men read weather.  Hot dust meant the stacks above were waking.  Cold iron and vinegar meant the archive was dying a little more.  He was kindly about it, and methodical: he swept, he kept a ledger, he pushed the building's dropped pencils back up through the register in a labeled tin. 

Now the pipes cooled and ticked, and in that sapphire hush he heard a hollow where no hollow should be.  The pipe chase behind the boiler ran straight up through three floors, and he had been uncovering its secrets for years: a wedding ring, a lunch tin, a mouse's winter.  He tapped the chase with a screwdriver handle, listening the way a doctor listens.  Two feet above the cleanout, the note went flat and dead.  He opened the access plate with the outage's dark for a curtain and found a cache.  Eleven tape reels, wrapped in a work order dated 1974, cached by somebody who could not bear the incinerator.  Oral histories: the neighborhood's voices, ordered destroyed when the branch went modern. 

The top reel had shattered, and a fragment lay loose in the wrapping: a curved shard of flange with three feet of ferromagnetic tape still wound on it, brown as dried tea.  He knew the smell of that tape before he knew its number.  Sense memory was the only library he had ever been issued a card to.  Warm oxide, cellar damp, one thread of clove cigarette, and he was nine again on the reading room floor, his mother's voice coming out of a speaker, telling a stranger how she crossed a river with a sewing machine on her back.  The work order agreed with his nose: reel seven, Adela Vance, seamstress, 1971. 

Above him, through the floor register, he heard the new archivist swear softly at her flashlight.  Nell had been coming in at dawn for four months to catalogue what the flood had left, and he had learned her by sound: the drag of the box, the pause, the small wet inhale over the gaps.  Twice she had told a patron, gently, that the oral histories no longer existed, and both times Ivo had lain still with his hand over his mouth.  To be found was to be a problem for the police to solve; to be visible was to be a person someone could use.  For years he had wanted only the second, and the dead cameras had finally made it simple. 

Outside, the city's lights flickered again, blue-white and uncertain, and dawn began to fill the shadowed library with the color of deep water.  He put the fragment in his shirt pocket, the ledger under his arm, and pushed the register up.  Nell's flashlight swung and pinned him, and he stood in the sapphire light with his hands open so she could see they held nothing dangerous.  "My name is Ivo," he said.  "I live under the reading room."  "Reel seven is in the pipe chase behind the boiler, with ten others." 

She did not scream, which he would remember for the rest of his life.  She said, "The purge," and then, "Show me," and followed him down with the light held steady.  Her battery reader was the size of a lunchbox; she threaded the fragment's tail past the head and pressed play.  Three feet of tape gave them eleven seconds.  "The machine was heavier than the child, so I carried the machine," a woman said, close and warm, and then the tape ran out. 

Nell sat down on the concrete and laughed once, badly, and wiped her face with her sleeve.  "That's Adela," he said.  "She's my mother."  "I can tell you which reel is which by the smell, if you'll write it down." 

She opened the ledger he handed her, saw eleven years of small careful entries, every lost pencil and dropped earring, and understood what she was being given.  At the top of a clean page she wrote: Ivo Vance, finder, and then the date, and then, because she was methodical too, the time.  The city's lights steadied; the boiler woke; dawn came up through the grating in long blue bars.  He would go back down tonight, but the register would stay unlocked, and in the morning someone would need him and know exactly where to knock.
