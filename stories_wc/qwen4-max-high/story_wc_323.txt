The benthic city had no windows, only edges.  Marit Solberg had been a navigation instructor for thirty-one years above the water; now she sat nine kilometers under it, holding one link of a boat anchor chain.  The link was the last piece of her husband's trawler anyone had recovered. 

After the discovery of the aliens, the sea had risen eleven meters in a season, taking the harbor, the academy, and him.  The visitors had not meant to drown anything.  They had simply come home to the deep, and built, and the water had made room for them.  Now their city glowed along the trench wall in slow blue terraces, and the humans who had lost the surface lived in its lit shells like guests who could never repay the invitation. 

Marit had come down for one reason: to learn to navigate by starlight.  Forty ships still rode the surface under a sky veiled by the dust of the arrival, compasses useless in the city's field, and they needed someone to reckon their positions from below.  The aliens could.  They had crossed the dark between stars without ever seeing a sky, and they had offered to teach. 

Their assessor had studied Marit for six days and then given her a phrase the translators rendered as balancedly curious.  It meant she wanted the unknown and the known in equal weight, that she could grieve a thing and still measure it.  It was, they said, the only temperament that survived their arithmetic. 

Her teacher was called Ninth Edge, and it began by taking the chain link from her hand.  Humans measure centers, it said through the translator at her throat.  We measure edges, where a thing stops being itself.  That was edge geometry, the whole of their navigation: a discipline of boundaries, of the exact places where light quit one medium and entered another.  Starlight came down through nine kilometers of water bent, folded, and lied about, but every lie had an edge, and an edge could be measured, and a measurement could be unfolded back into the truth. 

Ninth Edge set the link on the table.  A horizon, it said. 

Marit almost laughed, and instead she cried, quietly, in the way the deep taught you to do everything quietly.  She had spent her life telling students that a horizon was a line you could never reach.  Here it was a closed loop of iron, worn thin on one side by forty years of dragging over sand. 

A horizon folded into a circle, Ninth Edge said, so that it can be carried.  To navigate, you must expand it. 

The city's tool was a slow field that persuaded metal to remember it had once been straight.  Marit put her hand over the link.  To open it was to lose the last closed thing she had of him.  Above, forty ships waited in the dark with their engines idling and their children sleeping in the wheelhouses.  She thought about weight, equal on both sides, and she nodded. 

The link opened.  It sighed, it lengthened, it became a bar of iron with a hook at each end, and Ninth Edge laid it across the table like a coastline.  There, it said.  Unfolded horizons. 

Then it taught her to lay the star's bent light along that straightened edge, to mark where the water stopped being water, to expand each measurement outward until the whole sky lay flat and honest in front of her.  It took eleven hours.  Somewhere in the ninth hour she found Altair, the star he had steered by, and she did not stop working, and that was the hardest thing she had ever done. 

At dawn, by the clock the city kept for its guests, she gave the first heading to the fleet, and the fleet turned.  She was their sky now, one woman with an iron bar and a discipline of edges, and she would be careful with them, and she would be curious about everything. 

Later she hung the straightened link above the door of the room where she would teach the first human class.  Her students would ask what it was.  A horizon, she would tell them, and watch them look at the ceiling, at nine kilometers of black water, and understand that they had been carrying one all along.
