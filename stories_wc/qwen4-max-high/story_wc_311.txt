The observatory had been ancient when the town below it was built, and the reaper came every dusk anyway.  She had found the recordings in March, spooled in a cabinet beneath the great cracked lens: forty wax cylinders, labeled in a hand she knew.  Ivo had been an astronomer, and also a listener.  In the plague years he sat with the dying and spoke their last words into a recording horn, so that nothing would be lost.  She had taken him in the winter of his sixty-first year, on this floor, under this lens.  That was the part she could not put down. 

A reaper does not carry souls; she carries their last phrases, and she gives each fear a name so the soul can set it down and go.  She had given her own name away to make room for theirs; what came out of her mouth was always a phrase some living person had forgotten.  The naming is the whole of the work.  Unnamed fear does not dissolve; it crystallizes.  For eleven months she had left Ivo's fear unnamed, and the lens above her had been frosting over.  Not frost: a black glass, growing in ferns and feathers across the aperture, hard as a held breath.  Through it, dusk could no longer scatter properly. 

She read the cylinders with light, not a needle: the sun's last angle broken through the cracked lens and spread across the grooves, so that every word on the wax sounded at once.  Scattered light was the only way to hear a whole life in a single minute.  Now the glass choked the beam, and the dead arrived faster than she could name them.  They stood in the dome's shadow like people waiting for a ferry, and their fears began to crystallize on the floor around their feet.  She had done this to them, by keeping one. 

Ivo's atlas page was still pinned beside the door, the southern sky he had never been able to measure.  Where the tables ran out he had drawn sea monsters, and named them: the Drowned Choir, the Anchored Whale, the Long Waiting.  "You cannot sail a blank," he had told her once, not looking up.  Draw the monster and the water becomes a road.  She had thought it was a habit of cartographers.  It was her whole profession, described by a man who had guessed it early and never asked. 

She had read thirty-nine cylinders; she took the fortieth from its sleeve and set it on the spindle.  She had saved it because the label said only: for her, when she is ready.  Then she cranked the shutter open a hand's width, and forced what light was left through the ruin of the lens.  The light broke into a hundred pieces and lay across the wax in bands.  The dome filled with his voice, from every direction, saying things nobody living remembered.  A woman's request that someone feed the grey cat.  A boy asking whether the horse had been found.  Then Ivo, close, out of breath, alone in this room with the machine. 

"I am not afraid of the dark," he said.  "I am afraid she will keep me."  "She will hold my last word in her mouth and never say it, and the work will stop, and she will call that love." 

The echo went round the dome twice and thinned.  She stood with her hands at her sides, the way she stood at bedsides, and let herself be told.  He had given up his staying so that she could keep going; that was the shape of what he had done, and she had been standing inside it for eleven months, calling it grief. 

She went to the atlas page and wrote in the blank margin below the Anchored Whale, in the small hand she used for the dead: the fear of being kept.  Then she said it aloud, which is the only way a name becomes true.  The black glass on the lens did not shatter; it unwrote itself, fern by fern, and the dusk came through clean and scattered.  Somewhere under her ribs she felt the held word let go.  The waiting dead began to move, one and then another, their floor-frost softening, and she named them as they passed. 

She left the page pinned where it was, with one more monster on it, and went down the hill to the town, where the dying were.  Behind her the observatory said, in his voice, feed the grey cat, and then nothing at all.
