The sky had been glass since the makers, and the great fire had cracked it.  Now shards came down over the plaza like slow snow, and the cold of the void came with them.  Sevda crossed the flagstones with her hands sheathed in fresh iron, as always.  Cloth was no good; the past came through cloth.  Every stone in that ancient square remembered the burning hour, and one touch dropped her into it: the same hour, the same screaming, forever.  Only the mosaic stepping stone at the threshold was safe.  Its patterns had lost tiles to nine centuries of feet.  The gaps were holes in its memory.  Standing in those holes, she was in the present.  She stood on it to eat, to argue, to be alive.  The city called her gentle, and meant it as an accusation.  She had never thrown a stone or raised her voice; she had only asked, in her flat quiet way, for the one thing nobody would give.  The Kindler still knelt at the plaza's north edge.  It was the engine that had done the burning, forty feet of riveted iron, and the council had voted to break it and scatter the pieces in the salt flats.  Sevda wanted its arms.  Not to punish them; to recast them.  New steel remembers nothing yet.  Melted and poured, it comes out blank, and blank steel is the only thing she can hold a child with.  The burned children needed holding, and her bare hands could not do it; she would fall into the fire and let them drop.  There were forty children and not enough blank iron.  So she meant to cradle empathy in arms of steel, and the council called that mercy misdirected, an aberration, a spit in the face of the dead.  They were not wrong.  It was aberrant mercy, mercy pointed at the wrong object, and she practiced it anyway.  But the law was older than the council: no named iron may be unmade.  The Kindler's name lay in the threshold stone, spelled the old way, in absences.  Chips in the mosaic made the strokes; the missing tiles were the letters.  To rename a thing you filled its holes and cut new ones.  Sevda had spent the winter filling them, one glass cube at a time.  Each tile she set stole a little of her present.  Unnamed iron could be broken by anyone, and the council had men waiting in the arcade.  Now one gap remained, the last stroke of the last letter, and in her pocket lay the tessera she had pried from the fallen minaret.  Blue, the size of a tooth, still bearing half of the word for mercy from the inscription band.  She had carried it since the morning after the fire, when the tower came down and the sky went to pieces.  It was warm from her hand; glass keeps heat longer than iron does.  A girl came across the plaza, six years old, wrapped in a curtain, her arms in the first braces Sevda had cast.  The girl stopped at the stone's edge and would not step on it.  "They say you are saving the thing that killed my mother," she said.  "I am saving its arms," Sevda said.  "Who will they hold?"  "You."  She knelt and set the blue tessera into the last hole.  The stone's memory closed over like water, and the fire took her.  She was in the burning hour again, the tower falling, the sky splitting, everyone screaming the names of the dead.  Her hands, blind in that hour, found the chisel and the mallet she had left ready.  She cut three strokes by feel, the letters the naming women had taught her, the word for cradle.  Glass flew; new holes opened; the present came back cold and quiet.  The old name was gone, and the new one bound the iron to a different making.  "Read it," the girl said.  Sevda traced the fresh chips with one iron finger.  "Cradle," she said.  The girl stepped onto the stone, into the present, and held out her arms.  Sevda lifted her with her own blank steel arms and felt nothing but the girl's weight, which was the whole point.  Above them the broken sky let down its cold.  Steel is cold until someone is inside it; then it takes their heat and keeps it, and Sevda stood in the falling glass and held on.
