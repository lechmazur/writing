Emil Voss sold maps of places he had never been.  His shop smelled of foxed paper and glue, and the only voice in it was his own, pricing coastlines aloud.  He was not a guessing man.  He dated atlases by licking a corner, weighed charts against a known ounce, held plates to the window until the watermark confessed its century.  Thirty years of testing had made his intuition feel like memory, which buyers trust and widowers do not.  In his waistcoat pocket he carried a type foundry matrix chip, a copper square the size of a thumbnail, punched deep with an ampersand.  Mira had cut it at her father's foundry the summer they met, and she said the ampersand was the only letter brave enough to refuse the word or.  On her last morning she asked him for the map he had never drawn: the cavern above the creek, the fork underground where he had turned back and let her go alone.  He had promised, and then he had gone on selling other men's surveys for eleven years. 

At midsummer the creek rose, as it did every year when the snow above the valley let go.  The mouth they had used eleven years ago was already drowned, brown water spinning in it.  Emil walked the bank with a blank sheet rolled under his arm and watched ants cross his boot, each hauling a white petal fragment uphill.  Laden ants walk straight home; he had proved it with sugar crumbs at nine, and again last week.  Petals meant a colony that farmed fungus, and such a colony needed a room that never froze and never dried.  In this valley that meant limestone, and it meant a room larger than his shop.  So he followed the ants home. 

They took him through nettle and hazel to a seam behind a curtain of roots, where the air breathed out cool and sweet.  He wetted a finger, held it up, felt the draft lean steady inward, and went in on his belly.  Forty paces later the floor dropped into a chamber, and the chamber was blooming.  A collapse in the roof let one shaft of solstice noon fall straight down, and under it grew pale flowers he had no name for, opening only in that hour of that week.  The ants carried their petals down into the dark, to feed whatever they farmed there. 

Beyond the light the passage split, and Emil stood at the doorway of decisions with his heart going like a bird in a room.  Left went low and dry; right went up into black.  Eleven years ago he had taken the low road because he could see the end of it, and Mira had gone up laughing, and come back with a description he never wrote down, because writing it would mean signing his name to having stayed behind. 

Behind him the creek was coming.  He heard it first as a change in the draft, then as a sound like paper tearing, and the low passage began to drink.  Emil held the matrix chip into the shaft of sun and the copper glimmered, throwing a small reversed ampersand onto the wet cave clay.  Not or, she had said.  He pressed the chip into the clay at the mouth of the rising branch, deep, the way her father struck a matrix, and left it there: the first mark he had ever made instead of copied. 

Then he went up into the black, counting paces, trailing his fingers along the wall, naming what he touched so he would remember it in ink.  The water climbed behind him and closed the low road, and he did not mind having the choice taken away, since he had already made it.  The upper gallery ran two hundred and twelve paces and opened at the end into a second skylight above the flooded valley. 

He sat there until evening with the sheet unrolled on his knees, drawing the fork, the flowers, the ant road, the copper mark.  It was not accurate.  He would test it again next midsummer, and the one after, until the drawing was as true as his hands could make it.  He would not sell this one.  He would carry it to the stone on the hill with her name cut shallow in it and read the legend aloud, pricing nothing, so the only voice there was still his, but saying her words.  Late, he told her, is not the same as never.
