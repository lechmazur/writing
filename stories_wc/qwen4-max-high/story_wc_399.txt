The prophet arrived on the third night, and by morning the pavilion had begun to murmur.  It always did, after a prophet came: the silk walls took the words and passed them along, hand to hand, until the whole structure sounded like a crowd remembering a dream. 

Sefa walked the line outside, half-masked, listening with the bare side of her face.  The mask was fired clay, glazed white, and it covered her left ear and cheek so that the field arrived to her only from the right, softened, sidelong.  Line walkers never faced a thing directly.  Look straight at a whisper and it thins into weather; attend to what stands beside it, and it keeps its shape.  So she worked through peripheral awareness, the way her teacher had drilled her: eyes on the sand, ears on the edges of sound. 

Beneath the dunes ran the kinetic parapet, a buried rampart of counterweighted plates that crawled a hand's width each hour, shouldering the sand aside and letting it fall.  Where its crest passed, the whisperfield loosened.  Clearings opened above it, conjured out of nothing but motion, and inside them a voice could stand whole for a while.  Sefa felt the crest through her heels, a slow pressure like a held breath, and stepped where it was going rather than where it had been.  Her work was to consecrate the clearings before the parapet moved on and let them close.  Unconsecrated, they folded, and whatever had gathered in them scattered back into the general murmur, sanded down to syllables. 

The pavilion keeper, Oren, came out at noon with his ledger and his worry.  "The last saying is in the ninth clearing," he said.  "The one about the wells."  "Then I will hold it," Sefa said.  "Hold the useful part," Oren said.  "The people are frightened.  They need the wells, not the stammer before them." 

She said nothing, because the mask made speech a labor, and because she had heard the echo already.  Echoes here were strangely honest.  They returned not what a mouth had shaped but what a person had meant, hesitation and all, the way water returns the whole stone and not its pleasant side.  The prophet had said, "Dig east of the salt," and before it, quietly, "I do not know, but I have walked there, and this is what I saw."  Oren wanted the instruction without the walking.  Sefa wanted the echo of wisdom, which is a different animal: not the answer, but the shape of a mind arriving at one, so that others could learn to arrive.  Her teacher had left one clearing consecrated with a confession, and Sefa had learned more from that stammer than from all the pavilion's certainties. 

She found the ninth clearing at dusk, a bowl of stillness in the murmur, wide as a threshing floor.  She did not look at it.  She knelt at its rim with her bare cheek turned aside and listened to the sand ticking down the parapet's far flank, and let the saying come in from the corner of her hearing.  Consecration cost a true thing, spoken into the clearing, since only truth has enough weight to keep a conjured space from closing.  Oren's trimmed version would not have held; a lie is a lighter gas, and the clearing would have shut like a mouth. 

Sefa lifted the mask.  "I wanted him to be certain," she said into the stillness.  "When he hesitated, I was angry, because then the walking is mine to do."  The clearing took the weight and settled, its edges firming like cooling glass.  Beneath her, the crest slid on, and the clearing stayed.  Inside it the prophet's voice went on being unsure, and then sure, and the unsureness was the part that would teach. 

Oren stood a long while at the edge with his ledger closed.  "They will hear the doubt," he said.  "They will hear where the wells came from," she said, and put the mask back on, and the field returned to her softly, from one side only. 

By full dark the pavilion had learned the whole saying, murmuring it wall to wall, hesitation intact.  Sefa walked out along the buried line, feeling for the next pressure under her heels, attending to everything beside the thing she was saving.  The dunes breathed.  The whisperfield lay calm from horizon to horizon, and she left it that way, clearing by clearing, all night.
