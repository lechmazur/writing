Nell Ferrier was Bellwether's archivist of puzzles, a job nobody had invented and nobody paid for.  She kept a ledger of the town's unsolved small things: why the church clock struck thirteen in August, whose dog dug up the memorial tulips, what the soap works had meant to build in its yard.  She never announced an answer; she left it lying where the right person would stub a toe on it.  The soap works had burned forty years before she was born, taking eleven names with it.  Entry forty-one read: why do the old people hop, all of them, at the same empty spot behind the ruins?  Her mother had drafted plans for that yard and died with them still on the board.  What survived came to Nell in a dove gray map case, her mother's initials stamped by the clasp.  Inside lay one blueprint, water-ruined, its middle washed to a pale blank.  Around the blank a curve began and stopped, began and stopped, like a sentence someone kept forgetting.  Nell wanted to finish it and build it: not a plaque, not a minute of silence, but pain fed through some patient machinery until it came out beautiful and useful.  Behind the rusted ruins, where the drying sheds had stood, lavender had come up out of spilled oil and seed.  The town fenced the field and told itself to keep moving.  Nell went there to linger, timing her work by the vines that climbed the stack a hand's width each week.  Then she began asking, one household at a time, for a single photograph of the old yard and one puzzle for the ledger.  People brought birthdays, a christening, a man asleep against a wall, all black and white, all with the whole unburned works standing behind them.  She laid tissue over each print and traced the arcs.  A skipping rope mid-swing, water flung from a hose, a hoop, a ball's guessed flight, a girl's arm reaching for her brother's cap.  For thirty years children had played along a chalk line nobody bothered to scrub: the footprint of the building that never got built.  Their play had kept the curve.  The grandmothers were hopping it still.  Overlaid, forty arcs agreed into one long line, and her mother's blueprint had a middle again.  Mrs. Pell, who had lost a husband and a sister among the eleven, came to shout at Nell for digging.  Nell let her shout and noticed, calmly, that the woman's hands kept drawing the same curve in the air, the way you describe what you cannot say.  "You have been drawing it on tablecloths for years," Nell said.  Mrs. Pell sat down in the lavender and stayed an hour.  By the time the vines reached the stack's third window, the ledger had filled with puzzles that were all one puzzle: what do we do with the yard.  Nell answered sideways, as always, pinning the finished curve to the bakery door with the arithmetic beside it, and the town solved itself into a work crew.  The smith bent ribs from the sheds' iron; the glazier's daughter cut the chapel's retired windows to fit.  They raised it in the lavender: a long curved glass shelter, open at both ends, with the photographs sealed into its spine, forty arcs in black and white, so the line was drawn twice, once in glass and once in play.  They called it the Arc, because Mrs. Pell said the word memorial only made people walk faster.  Inside ran a bench the whole length of the curve, room enough for eleven names and everybody who missed them.  People came to linger, which was the one thing Bellwether had forbidden itself.  The bees were skeptical, then enthusiastic.  Nell put the whole blueprint back in the dove gray map case and left the case open on the bench, so anyone could add a photograph, or a puzzle.  As the vines climbed past the fourth window, the arcs kept arriving: a wedding, a dog mid-leap, a boy's paper airplane bending exactly along her mother's line.  Her last ledger entry that summer asked why the lavender smelled of soap on hot days.  She left the answer where somebody would stub a toe on it: the masterpiece was not the glass, but a town that could finally stand still in one place together.
