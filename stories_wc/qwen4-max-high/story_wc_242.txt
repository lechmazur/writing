The booth stood in a subway utility tunnel under Ash Street, a steel box the transit authority had forgotten it owned.  Mira kept it alive.  The last train had gone at one, and the tunnel smelled of hot iron and wet rope.  Officially she tested copper pairs for the night crew; unofficially she caught the calls that fell through the cracks of the city's numbering.  Wrong numbers, mostly, dialed by people who could not sleep.  Above her the sodium streetlamps hummed on a rolling relay, block by block, and between one hum and the next lay nine seconds of clean silence.  She lived in those seams.  In the hush she could hear a caller breathe before they spoke. 

Her regulars knew her only as the voice that answered when the number they wanted was gone.  A woman dialing a bakery that had closed in spring.  A boy dialing his own old house.  Mr. Aday, who every night at two forty dialed his wife's line, three years disconnected.  She never gave a name, never asked for one, never called anybody back.  This was not coldness; it was cultivation.  Estrangement, tended daily like a bed of pale flowers, kept the distance a stranger needs to hold what a friend would drop.  She had weeded her own life the same way, until no right number of hers remained in service. 

For endings she used the disc.  A glass harmonica disc, chipped at the rim, salvaged from a busker's flooded case at the tunnel's low end.  She had mounted it on a spindle geared to a cable reel, and when she wetted her thumb and touched the turning rim, the note went up the copper and into the caller's ear.  The dead lines down here were melodically haunted; every apprentice had heard singing in a pair with nothing on the far end.  Mira had learned to give the haunting a shape and a purpose.  The note meant: this is the last of it, and the last of something is a door.  She was not in the business of comfort.  She taught endings how to begin. 

Mr. Aday was her hardest student.  He would talk to the empty line about the price of tomatoes, then dial again, then again, looping the same seven digits until the lamps came around.  Each night she answered, and each night she played the disc, and each night he said, "Wait," and started over. 

On Thursday the authority posted the notice: copper cut at dawn Friday, fiber in, the tunnel booth dark.  She read it twice and felt the particular vertigo of a teacher handed her own lesson.  At two forty the seam opened between the hum of the Ash Street lamps and the hum of the ones on Kelso, and Mr. Aday came through it.  He said his daughter had asked him to breakfast.  He said he had said yes.  Then he asked, for the first time in three years, what he should do with the number. 

Mira looped the patch cord back into its own jack, a ring of copper with no far end, so that for once he would hear only himself.  "Say it to her," she told him.  In the nine clean seconds she heard him say goodbye in his own voice, and then she wetted her thumb and let the disc sing the ending open.  He hung up first.  That was the whole curriculum. 

The tunnel went quiet in a way it never had.  She sat with the estrangement she had grown so carefully, and saw it for what it was: a garden with no gate.  Somewhere above, her sister had kept the same number for eleven years.  Mira had dialed it a thousand times in her head and never once with her hands.  The cut crews would be here in four hours.  She unclipped the disc from the spindle and put it in her coat pocket, chipped rim against her thumb.  Then she waited for the seam.  When the Ash Street hum fell away and the Kelso hum had not yet risen, she dialed a right number.  It rang into the hum.  She let it.  On the fourth ring a woman said hello, and Mira, who had spent years being nobody's voice, said her own name.  Above her the lamps took up their humming, block by block, and under it the line stayed open.
