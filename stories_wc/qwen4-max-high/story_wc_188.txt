The fortress had stopped being a fortress and become a trellis.  Night-blooming cereus had climbed the gun ports, and at dusk the whole south wall opened its white mouths at once, so the soldiers' grandchildren smelled sugar where their grandparents had smelled powder.  Vesna walked its length chanting, as she always walked, because a chant is a fuse you can carry in your mouth.  Sixty-two paces, four beats to the pace: she had timed shells that way since she was nine.  She was a fireworks designer with no city, which is a polite way of saying she was a wanderer with wet powder.  The marsh road had gotten into her cartridges, and the fortress did not care.  Three months of blockade had taught them that hope is a thing that falls.  The grain ships had turned back, the relief fleet had turned back, and the clouds had come down low and stayed; there had not been a star over the courtyard in eleven nights.  "Save your fire for cooking," the gatekeeper told her, not unkindly.  She had not lit a shell in four months, and had begun to suspect she was only a woman carrying mud. 

By full dark, a boy was spinning fire in the plaza.  He had a chain, and at the end of the chain a knot of rag the size of a fist, and the knot drank kerosene and threw a slow orange ring across the mosaic.  Nobody watched him; the plaza kept the hush of a night with no stars.  That was the part that hurt: a boy making a circle of light in a town that had stopped looking up.  Vesna sat on the well curb and let her eyes go soft, the way she did when a shell climbed and she had to hold the whole sky at once instead of one bright point.  That was when the floor spoke.  Every time the burning knot swung low, the mosaic answered: a scatter of tiles that took the light differently, glossy where the rest were matte, flickering at the edge of her vision and gone whenever she looked straight at it. 

"There's a note in it," she said.  The boy stopped spinning.  "The woman who laid this floor left a note," he said.  His grandmother's grandmother had hunted for it with a lamp and found nothing, and the priest called it a story to sell tiles.  "A lamp held high is the wrong sun," Vesna said.  "You need the light to crawl." 

But his flame spat and guttered; the fortress kerosene was thick with rust and grit, and a stuttering light tells no secrets.  She untied his knot and stretched the rag over the mouth of the oil can.  She filtered the whole tin through the cloth, twice, until the grit stayed behind and the kerosene ran clear, then tied the knot back onto the chain.  She soaked the knot, lit it, and told him to swing it flat and low, ankle height, the flame lying down along the tiles.  "Don't look at the fire," she said, "look at the room."  She chanted her four beats and walked the plaza's edge, keeping the whole floor in the loose net of her attention, and the note rose out of the stone like a face out of water. 

Glazed tiles, hundreds of them, laid in the shapes of constellations: the Ship, the Furrow, the Nine Sisters.  And along the border, in letters a hand wide, the mosaicist's note: FOR THE NIGHTS THE SKY FORGETS US.  Two hundred years ago somebody had also sat under a lid of cloud and had refused to accept the ceiling.  Vesna laughed out loud, once, the way she laughed when a fuse behaved.  All her life she had argued with gravity, throwing light up so it would fall beautifully; here was a woman who had argued with it by putting the stars down where feet could reach them. 

Her powder was mud, so she did the only honest thing: she redesigned the show.  They woke the households, and every lamp, every rag knot, every twist of cloth and drop of kerosene in the fortress came out into the plaza.  She taught them the four-beat chant and set them walking the glazed lines, low flames trailing from their hands, so the Ship sailed and the Nine Sisters burned along the ground.  The gatekeeper wept and pretended it was smoke.  Above them the clouds held, starless and indifferent; the cereus opened its white mouths on the wall; and eighty people counted paces over a sky that could not fall.  Vesna walked the outer ring, chanting, timing nothing, defying gravity at ankle height.
