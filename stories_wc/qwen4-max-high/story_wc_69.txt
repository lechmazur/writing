The bus let her down at the crossroads and drove on, and Marta stood with one suitcase in the road, listening.  Nine years of choirs in other countries had taught her the sound of a place holding its breath.  The valley had begun unlearning hatred, and no one could say how long the lesson would take.  She had left at nineteen hating every river accent, and nine years of foreign choirs had worn that hatred smooth. 

The singing hall had burned in the last week of the war, so the library lent her its reading room.  Velvet curtains, velvet spines, velvet dust; autumn came through the tall windows the color of weak tea.  Twenty children came the first Thursday, eleven from the ridge and nine from the river, and they sat on opposite sides of the room like two weather systems.  She did not tell them to love each other.  She had come to braid their voices into harmony, which is a slower and more honest work. 

"We are not going to sing louder," she said.  "We are going to take the noise away."  She had them stop scraping chairs, stop coughing on purpose, stop the low hum of insult that passed for a greeting.  It took three Thursdays to get the room quiet enough to hear the leaves come down outside.  Then a river boy named Teo sang four notes, and nobody laughed, which surprised everybody.  She taught them harmony by subtracting noise, the way a carver makes a hole by taking wood away. 

There was one carver left in the valley, an old river man named Petar, who made zithers no one bought anymore.  The factories had won; the young wanted speakers, not strings; his craft was vanishing the way a dialect vanishes, one funeral at a time.  Marta walked to his shop with a broken zither she had found in the library basement.  Its belly was split, its neck warped, but the soundhole rosette was whole: a lace of pear wood cut through in fourteen petals.  Petar turned it in the light and said his father had cut it, and that the fourteen petals denoted the fourteen villages of the valley, ridge and river alike.  Two of those villages were gone now, burned and not rebuilt, and he had kept cutting all fourteen anyway.  He was expectantly resigned, setting out his knives each morning for customers who never came.  The zither could not be saved, he said, but he pried the rosette free and gave it to her, because a hole is a thing you can carry. 

She hung it in the library window, where the autumn light came through the petals and laid fourteen small shapes on the floor.  The children learned to read the pattern: this petal is your village, that one is mine, and the wood between them is what holds.  In the second autumn, a ridge girl and Teo sang a third apart and held it, and the room went still in a new way.  One ridge father forbade his daughter to sing with river boys; Marta did not argue, she only kept a chair for him.  In the fourth, the ridge fathers came to listen, standing at the back with their caps in their hands. 

In the sixth, Petar died, and the choir sang at his funeral, and there was no one left who could cut a rosette.  Marta tried to learn from his notebooks and failed; her hands were shaped for conducting, not knives.  Vanishing arts do not always leave heirs; sometimes they leave only a lesson and a hole in the shape of a flower.  She kept the lesson: take away until what remains can be heard. 

By the ninth autumn the choir had thirty voices and no sides, and the war was a thing the youngest ones asked about.  Teo, grown, taught the little ones to breathe from the floor up.  The stillness she had carried through nine countries had finally found a room to sit down in.  Outside, the leaves came down; inside the velvet room, the noise fell away, and thirty voices braided into one sound with fourteen villages in it.  She held the rosette up to the light and let the empty petals do their work.
