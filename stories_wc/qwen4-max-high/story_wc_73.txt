The staircase has no top, and Mira climbs it anyway, which is her whole philosophy in one gesture.  She calls herself a grim optimist: things are bad, and bad is a direction, and directions can be walked.  Chalk in her pocket, a pair of dice in her fist, one of them chipped at the corner where the six should be.  The chip came from the only fall she ever took, back when she still believed down was a place. 

Every landing holds a door.  Every door is the same door: green paint, brass handle worn pale, a draft underneath that smells of rain and other people's dinners.  She declined it the first time.  She had rolled the dice for courage, gotten a poor sum, and honored the arithmetic.  Her courage has always been balancedly daring: brave exactly as far as the odds permit, never one step past the ledger.  Now she does not want the door opened; she wants its texture written down, the precise grain of a chance she let close.  She keeps a notebook of it: the handle's coolness, the shy click of the latch, the exact weight of not turning it. 

The staircase, meanwhile, does not move her.  Steps rise out of the dark below and sink into the dark above, an escalator of stone, perpetual motion with no cargo delivered.  She climbs, the stairs descend, and the sum is a woman held perfectly still inside infinity.  There is room to go anywhere, and nowhere to arrive; the walls are made of distance. 

She learned the mechanism the only way she knows how: through mathematical proofs, written on the risers in a small, patient hand.  Lemma: the staircase is generated by deduction.  Every theorem she completes becomes a flight of stairs, because a proven thing must exist somewhere, and here is the only somewhere there is.  Her optimism is the engine; her hope is the fuel; she has been building her own cage out of correct reasoning.  The dice confirmed it.  Rolled to choose a landing, they gave the same lopsided sequence over and over, the chipped corner catching the stone, the six refusing to arrive.  She wrote: nothing random survives here; the house eats every accident and calls it a step.  A grim optimist does not need the world to be kind, only legible. 

So she stopped trying to escape and started trying to prove the place impossible.  If the staircase must contain every proof, then let it contain a proof that it cannot contain.  She built it carefully, the way she does everything, checking each line twice, daring only where the ledger allowed.  Chalk squeaked; the flights above her hesitated.  The last line required a step that the staircase could not build without unbuilding itself.  And because the staircase is honest, because it is nothing but obedience to proof, it ruptured. 

A seam split the stone from landing to landing, a wound with no bottom, and through it came rain, and the smell of other people's dinners.  The green door stood in the rupture, close enough to touch, its brass handle worn pale by hands that were not hers.  She could have written it down.  She had come all this way to capture the texture of the thing she missed, and here was the grain of it, the coolness, the shy latch, the whole lost opportunity offered like a specimen.  Description, she understood then, was the last cage: a way of holding a door forever without walking through it. 

The rupture was closing, the proofs already rewriting themselves into new flights, the engine of her hope warming up again.  She rolled the dice.  Old habit: let the odds decide, be brave exactly as far as the ledger permits.  They clattered, the chipped one tumbling wrong, and she did not look.  She turned the handle instead, unbalanced, unproven, and stepped into the rain. 

Behind her the dice lay unread, the chipped one showing a face she would never learn.  The staircase went on climbing itself, forever, patiently, without her.  Whatever waited out here would be bad, she knew, and bad was a direction, and she had finally learned to walk without counting.
