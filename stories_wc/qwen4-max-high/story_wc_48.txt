Mara Voss had written one true book and was being paid to write it again.  The Drowned Orchard told how the reservoir took her mother's village, and readers wept on trains, and her editor wanted a second drowning by spring.  So each dawn she walked out to the feral nexus below the sluice, where the river, the tide, and the old canal cut fought each other into mist.  Nothing there was owned or tended; herons stood in the reeds like bad ideas, dogs went wild along the shingle, and the water could not decide which way to go.  Sound went into that mist and came back thinner, so everything said there sounded like a secret being kept. 

In her coat pocket she kept a spiral seashell fragment, a broken whorl the flood had carried nine miles inland and left in the branches of her mother's apple tree.  It was the only shape of hope she knew by heart: the curve that keeps returning to the same place, smaller.  That was the trouble with the new book.  She could recreate grief forever, but she had never learned the other shapes, and she would not fake them.  So she had begun collecting the shapes of different hopes. 

People came to the nexus to promise things, because no church would have them: runaways, dry drunks, sisters not speaking, a man who wanted to be forgiven by a river.  Mara made herself useful in ephemeral cameo appearances, a stranger's extra hands for ninety seconds: holding a rope, a coat, a sleeping child, the shutter of a phone.  Then she stepped back into the mist and wrote down what their hoping looked like from outside.  Her notebook filled with other people's shapes; her manuscript stayed at chapter thirty-nine.  Everyone called her lucidly watchful and meant it as praise; her sister had once meant it as an accusation.  "You attend everything, Mara, and you never arrive." 

That morning a girl sat on the ferry bench reading The Drowned Orchard, four pages from the end.  Mara knew those pages the way a runner knows a hill: five minutes, then the soft whisper of closing pages, then a face lifted and changed.  Slack water at the nexus lasted about that long.  Whatever she did that morning, she would do it inside that whisper. 

Out on the sandbar, two people were waiting for someone who was not coming.  A woman in a hospital cardigan and a young man who kept apologizing to her.  They wanted a promise witnessed, and the ferryman had refused, and the priest upriver had refused twice, because a vow to leave sounds like a vow to abandon.  The boy called to Mara, because she was the one with the notebook, the one always standing near.  Would she solemnize it, he asked, since nobody else would. 

The sandbar lay forty feet out and the tide was already leaning upstream, so she went against the current, water shoving her knees back toward shore.  Every step was an argument she had to win.  Her cameo rules said ninety seconds and no names. 

The woman was dying, and the promise she wanted was that her son would go away, to sea or school or anywhere, and not spend the year attending her.  "He watches," she said.  "He will stand in the doorway and watch me the whole way and call it love." 

Mara's hand closed on the fragment in her pocket.  A spiral is a hope that only ever comes back to itself, tighter each time.  She had written thirty-nine chapters of that. 

She put the woman's hand and the boy's hand together and made him say it: that he would go, and be missed, and come back carrying something she had never seen.  He said it badly, twice, until it was true.  "Witnessed," Mara said, and the word came out of her like something set down after a long carry.  She pressed the broken shell into the woman's palm, so the vow would have a weight, and because she was tired of carrying the only shape she knew. 

On the bank the girl turned the last leaf, and the whisper came across the water, and the slack ended.  The current came back hard and the boy went first, breaking it for the two women behind him.  On the bench the girl looked up, changed, and Mara, who had watched everything for years, walked over to be seen.  Chapter forty would begin with a door left open and nobody standing in it.
