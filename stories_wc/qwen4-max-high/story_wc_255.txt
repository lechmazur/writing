The phoenix rose from the glassblower's furnace, as it did every nineteen years, and its ash put the town to sleep.  Vesna found her daughter face down among the glass pots in the courtyard, breathing, unreachable.  She did not call her name.  A human voice sinks the ash-sleepers deeper, so the courtyard traded in silence, in stems and pots.  Dov, who had blown glass beside the phoenix for forty years, kept the only instrument that could wake them.  His concertina had sixty reeds when he inherited it.  Three days of ash had left it with one.  Each waking cost one reed tongue, prised out with a knife and pressed into the woken hand, and the town's music thinned as its sleepers woke.  Awakenings were bartered, never bought with coin.  You gave something your hands had made and could not bear to lose.  Vesna was a painter, and she had been born without color.  Her skies came out green, her lips slate, her daughter's hair the gray of dishwater, and she had spent thirty years arguing that none of this ruined anything.  She laid her best canvas on the bench and set a sprig of rosemary beside it: remember me, take this, wake her.  Dov answered by standing a snapped chrysanthemum in an empty pot.  Flawed goods.  He had refused her work for years, politely, in flowers.  But the payment had already been made, and by a better negotiator.  Nine years old, Mira had carried her mother's portrait of her into the courtyard three days before the rising, wrapped in wet moss.  She had set three stems of mint around a cracked glass bird: this is the most beautiful thing here.  One waking, to be spent when she asked for it.  Dov had looked at the wrong-colored child in the wrong-colored grass and found he could not tell a nine-year-old that beauty had to be correct.  He took the painting and gave her the promise, and she went home smiling with her whole face.  She had not lied once, which was the cunning of it.  Now Dov opened the concertina's case and turned the brass frame toward her, and Vesna saw the last tongue.  A hairline crack ran up its length; it would buzz, split, sag flat of the note.  He set a whole glass rose beside it, then a wilted one, and shook his head.  In forty years no flawed reed had woken anybody, and he would not spend the town's last waking on a maybe.  Vesna's hands were shaking, so she made herself work slowly, the way she mixed a color she would never see.  She took the cracked pot from the sill, the one Dov kept because a seedling had come up through its fracture, and set it between them.  Then she pointed at Mira, at the crack, at the seedling, and waited.  Perfect things lull.  That was the whole of her argument, and she had lived it.  A flawless glass rose asks nothing of the eye; a cracked one with a seedling climbing out of it will not let you look away.  Sleep is the perfect place, and the ash gives them nothing to catch on.  He had taken her daughter's wrong colors and called the bargain fair.  She snapped a stem of mint in half and laid the halves against the cracked reed.  Let it buzz.  Let the wrongness be the hook that pulls her up.  Dov stood a long time with his hands flat on the bench.  Then he fitted the cracked tongue into its slot, closed the bellows, and knelt beside the girl.  The note came out ugly.  It buzzed and split and sagged, two pitches fighting inside one brass tongue, a sound like a hinge that needed oil, like a bird with a burr in its throat.  Mira's fingers moved.  She turned her head toward the ugliness the way a plant turns toward a window, and she woke.  Dov prised the tongue out, still warm, and pressed it into her palm, and her fist closed on it.  The concertina was empty now; the lanes were full of sleepers, and there would be no more music until the phoenix rose again.  Vesna painted her daughter waking: dishwater hair, slate mouth, the light through the glass pots a color she would never know and had gotten right anyway.  Mira looked at the empty brass frame, then at the lanes, and her face went sly and gentle at once.  She set the cracked bird beside the seedling and pushed the arrangement toward Dov: nineteen years, then, and we start saving now.  Vesna laughed without sound and kept painting the wrong, beautiful world.
