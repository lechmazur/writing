The shouting had stopped, and no one had found a sentence small enough to fit the hush it left.  Inside the decommissioned radar dome, the fire in the oil drum lit half of every face and left the other half to the dark.  The antenna had gone for scrap, but the shell still gathered whispers and passed them around, so nobody had ever been able to say anything quietly here.  Above them, through the split panel, a meteor drew its line and was gone, and everyone waited for the sound that never comes. 

Anselm sat with his cuffs turned back twice, urbane even here, a calligrapher who could make a bar's name look like a cathedral's.  He lettered signs down in the port, and on Thursdays he worked puppet shows in the seedy bars along Slip Street, subversive little farces about the base and the men who had run it.  Puppets could say what a man could not: a goose is not a named officer, and a sock cannot sue.  The customs officer was a goose.  The station commander was a sock with medals.  And Ilse, who had been the last listener in this dome, was a wooden woman in a headscarf who heard the end of the world and calmly finished her tea.  Men who had worked the gates loved that calm; it made their own silence look like wisdom. 

Ilse herself was uncommonly serene, the way deep water is serene.  She had brought the story stick up every August since the station closed, a campfire rule her father had kept on the moor: one end charred black, and whoever holds the burnt end speaks, and nobody interrupts, and what is said stays inside the dome.  Two Augusts ago Ilse had held the stick and told them about a return on her screen that she never logged.  Anselm had listened, and then he had carried it down the hill and made it laugh.  Last Thursday she had gone down to Slip Street and stood at the back and watched herself finish her tea.  That was the argument, and Ilse had won it, and winning had left nothing behind but this hush. 

Anselm turned his pen over in his fingers, the way he did when he was thinking about letters.  "I wanted to know if a story can be told about someone without being taken from them," he said.  "That is the only thing I have ever wanted to find out."  "You found out," Ilse said. 

He had always had his best ideas in half-light, and had never once suspected them for it.  The fire showed him her mouth and not her eyes, and he understood that he had built a whole woman out of the half of her the stick had lit.  The puppet was serene because he had needed her to be; serenity reads well from the back of a bar.  He had filled the dark half with his own hand, the way he filled a flourish, and men had laughed at his invention and called it her. 

He reached for the stick where it lay across the drum's rim, and he turned it end for end.  "Reverse it," he said.  "Hold the pale end and you speak."  "Hold the char and you listen, and you may not ask for the rest."  He took the burnt end in both hands, like a man taking back a tool he had misused.  He laid his pen on the far side of the drum, where he could not reach it without standing up. 

Ilse held the pale end and looked into the drum for a while.  Then she told him the other half.  She had not been at her screen that night; she had gone out to the fence to watch the meteors, and when she came back the tape held a return she could not explain, and she erased it, and went home, and slept well.  "I was not calm," she said.  "I was absent."  "Your puppet is the woman I have let them believe in for thirty years, and you sold her for beer money." 

He had guessed the mask exactly, and made it beautiful, and sold it back to the town.  That was the theft: not the lie, but the half-truth made permanent.  He did not ask her anything.  He did not reach for the pen.  Above the dome another meteor went over, and everyone held still, listening for a sound the sky does not make, and the story stayed half in the dark, where the rest of it belonged.
