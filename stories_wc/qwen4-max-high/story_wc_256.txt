After the power grid stabilized, the Laboratory of Lost Sounds could finally keep its wax cold and its amplifiers warm.  For six blackout years it had worked by flame, and Oriel missed the flame.  She was the last prism-song bell ringer, hanging cut glass in her bells' mouths so one strike came out as a chord and a scatter of golden shadows. 

Now the Trust wanted her for the tenth station of its Artificial Pilgrimage.  Nine loudspeaker shrines lined the old canal road, each playing a recovered extinction: a frog, a dialect, a river that no longer ran.  Pilgrims carried stamped booklets and walked the whole route in one afternoon.  The director called it a pilgrimage because people needed one and there was nowhere left to go.  Oriel asked what they were meant to find.  "Connection," the director said, and showed her the satisfaction surveys. 

She had rung bells through the blackouts, when the tower was the only thing everyone could hear, and she wanted to know what had actually passed between them.  Not whether it passed, but the texture of connection itself, its grain.  Now the current was reliable and nobody came out to stand together anymore. 

So she began questioning assumptions, politely, the way she pulled a rope.  Did the pilgrims weep at the frog, or at the stranger weeping beside them?  She stood at station four and asked people what they remembered.  They remembered a cough, a shared canteen, the smell of a wet coat; almost nobody could hum the frog.  The lab assumed fidelity was the bridge; Oriel suspected the bridge was simply standing close enough to be heard breathing. 

The director argued that a manufactured pilgrimage was a lie told to the lonely.  "Every pilgrimage was manufactured," she said.  "Someone built the road." 

For the tenth station, at the reservoir where the drowned village's bells lay silent, she refused the loudspeakers.  Instead she borrowed her father's miner's carbide lamp from the shelf of obsolete instruments, where it had smoked glass plates through all the dark years.  Acetylene flame lays down soot like black velvet; a horn and a bristle draw sound into it as a wavering line you cannot play, only look at.  The lab had shelved the phonautograph as a failure, since it could write a sound but never say it back.  The miner's carbide lamp lens, ground to gather a flame underground, doubled as the laboratory's finest magnifier.  She would not record her bells alone.  She would record the pilgrims hearing them. 

There was no engine, only a pole, so she took twelve pilgrims at a time out to the buoy.  Rope years had made her powerfully gentle: she could propel a ton of oak and children without a lurch, and lift a sooted plate without smudging one crest of its wave.  On the water she hung one prism bell from the boat's frame and struck it with the heel of her hand.  Late sun came through the cut glass and laid golden shadows on twelve upturned faces.  The horn stood on the thwart, its bristle trembling on smoked glass, catching not the bell but everything around it: the oar, the breath, a child asking whether the town below was still there.  Nobody shushed the child.  That was the sound she wanted. 

Back in the laboratory she set the plate under the carbide lens and threw its tracing on the wall with the new electric lamp.  The bell's stroke was a clean spike; around it ran a thick, fibrous, shivering band, twelve breaths' worth of small noises braided into one line.  There it was: texture.  Connection was not the note traveling; it was the fuzz the note traveled through, the ordinary rustle of people who had agreed to be quiet together. 

The director looked a long time at the wall.  Then he asked, quietly, whether they should stop restoring frogs.  "No," she said.  "Give them something worth standing beside each other for." 

The tenth station opened in spring: no speaker, one prism bell, a boat, and a blank sooted plate for every crossing.  Pilgrims stamped nothing, and were asked only to lift the lamp's lens to their own line and read who had stood beside them.  Oriel poled, and rang, and held the plates by their edges, powerfully gentle, while golden shadows moved on the water and the archive learned the difference between a sound saved and a sound shared.
