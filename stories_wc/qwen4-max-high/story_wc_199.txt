Halla sorted seeds in the loft of the ashen arbor, the tree that hung above the valley on its own smoke, and she sorted them the way her grandmother had taught her: by weight, by ridge, by the small sound they made falling into tin.  Moss grew along the sorting rails, damp and green and pleased with itself, and Halla was pleased with it too.  Grey leaves fell past the loft all year, and the other sorters let them go.  Halla caught them, because their veins were letters.  Everyone in the valley knew the arbor's rule: whatever fell down through its hollow roots came back up changed, always, never once the same.  Children were warned not to drop wishes in; nobody could choose the shape of the return.  But her grandmother had known the older half of the story, the part about the hush: ten heartbeats between the last leaf of the old year and the first leaf of the new, when past and prophecy stood in the same doorway.  In that stillness the leaves fell in order, and in order they could be read.  Halla had spent nine years learning to read them.  Her ledger held four hundred tracings, and not one of them would give up its word.  For every seed she could not name, she built a tiny monument.  Husk cairns, thistle spires, a bridge of two pine scales for the orchard the fire had taken before she was born.  They stood in rows along the mossy rail, small enough to hide under a thimble, and the moss grew up around them like a green crowd at a dedication.  Monuments kept a thing from being wholly forgotten, her grandmother said, even when its name was gone.  The last monument was unfinished, and it was for her grandmother's ring.  A moonstone cabochon, thumb worn for sixty years, until the dome went flat, then thin, then powdery, until nothing was left but one dust speck folded in paper.  Halla could not build a cairn for a speck; there was nothing smaller to stack.  So she kept it, and waited for the hush, and told herself she was being orderly about it.  Moonstone holds moonlight the way a seed holds a tree, her grandmother used to say, all of it folded into the smallest part.  Halla had written that down too, under folklore, under things she hoped were true.  The hush came at dusk on the last day of the year.  The wind quit; the moss stopped its faint creaking; the arbor held its breath between what it had been and what it would be.  Leaves came down one at a time, slow as coins in water, and Halla laid them in a line on the bench.  Nine leaves, nine words, and the ash grey veins would not resolve.  Ash writing needs moonlight to be read, and the sky was ash, and there was no moon.  She had one speck of moon.  Halla unfolded the paper, took her sorting needle, and gave the speck the smallest prod she had ever given anything.  It rolled onto the middle leaf, and it opened.  Blue light came out of it, cold and milky and enormous, the whole sixty years of thumbs, every night the ring had stood in.  The veins stood up out of the ash like wet ink, and the nine words read themselves to her.  "Build for the forgotten; we return them changed, always."  Halla laughed, a damp, delighted, mossy laugh, because she had been answering that letter for nine years without knowing it.  Every husk cairn was a request.  She tipped the monuments into the hollow one at a time, and then, with the needle, prodded the speck after them.  The hush ended; the wind came back; the first leaf of the new year went down unread, and Halla let it go.  She waited with her hands flat on the bench, orderly, patient, counting.  Up through the roots came the smell of apple blossom from the orchard burned before her birth, and three notes of a bird nobody living had heard.  Then a seed rose past the rail and settled in her palm, white and warm, and it was not a moonstone and it was not a ring.  It was the speck, come back the way everything came back.  Halla opened her ledger to a clean page, wrote the weight, the ridge, the small sound it made falling into tin, and then, in a column no sorter had ever ruled, what it had become.  Then she planted it in the moss, and the moss, delighted, closed over.  She had three hundred and ninety one leaves left to build for.
