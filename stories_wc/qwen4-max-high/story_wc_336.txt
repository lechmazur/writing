The orchard above the falls held its breath.  The buds had swollen to the size of held words and would not open until morning: the year's one hush of unspent hope.  Odile walked the parapet, that old stone lip furred with moss and saxifrage, where the water went over and the world went with it. 

She was the pruner here, and had been for sixty springs.  Wisdom, in her trade, meant knowing that every cut is a question the tree will answer, whether or not you like the answer.  It also meant knowing what could not be cut.  She was insatiably creative, which the orchard had learned to forgive.  Ninety-one grafts she had made along this green wall: pear on hawthorn, plum on wild rose, an apple that fruited twice a year out of sheer curiosity. 

The grafts were not decoration.  They were stitches.  The waterfall was the warp, thousands of white threads falling forever, and Odile wove across it, binding the orchard world to the world downstream.  Downstream, everything had been named.  That was the trouble with names: once a thing was named, people stopped seeing it, and the color it had worn before its naming went out like heat from a stone.  Odile had spent her life keeping that color alive on this side of the water.  She could not keep it alive on the other. 

The boy came up the mist-stair before dawn, grey as a cellar stone, through the narrow gaps that only the empty-handed can pass.  His hands shook, and his eyes went past the trees without landing on them.  He said his mother had sent him for help, because down there the last unnamed thing had been named that winter, and now nothing would catch the eye at all. 

Odile had given succor before.  She had sent cuttings down in baskets for forty years, and every one had rooted, grown, been called pear or plum or apple, and gone grey.  A cutting carries a shape, she said, and a shape can be recognized, and recognition is the end of wonder. 

Then she took the paper from her breast pocket.  Inside was one heirloom seed, brown, ridged, unremarkable, the last from her mother's unnamed tree.  The tree had died the spring her mother died, forty years ago, and Odile had carried the seed ever since.  Here was the shadow of her gift: an unspent seed is a thousand orchards, and a planted one is only a tree.  Every spring she had invented another way to save the color without spending the seed, and every spring the water had gone on falling.  Now a boy stood on the parapet with nothing left in his eyes to catch a color with.  Wisdom is only the moment you stop being clever. 

She knelt and pressed the seed into his palm, and closed his fingers over it.  A seed does not carry a shape, she told him; it carries essence, the appetite that makes an apple, the hunger a color has to be looked at.  Plant it on your side of the water, on whatever green wall you have, and do not name it.  Not for a year, not for a hundred; call it the tree, call it that one, and let it stay unfinished. 

To let him back through with something living she had to open the weave, and the weave opens only in this hush, while the sap rises and nothing has yet been spent.  She went to her first graft, made the spring her mother died, and cut it out.  The seam sighed open; the falls leaned; the boy went down the mist-stair with his fist shut like a bud.  Odile bound the gap with a scion of medlar on blackthorn, her ninety-second invention, and her hands were steady. 

Then the sun came over the ridge, and the hush broke, and every bud on the parapet opened at once with a sound like a small rain.  She stood in the radiant quiet afterward, in the roar that is a kind of silence, and watched the water carry the light down.  She would never see the tree.  That was the price of succor given properly: you send the color where you cannot follow it. 

She pinned the empty paper to the wall beside the new stitch, and let the wind read it.  All that day, threading new stitches through the warp, she imagined a wall she had never seen, and a boy saying that one, and something green rising unnamed out of the grey.  Toward evening a thread of mist rose from below wearing a color she could not name, and Odile did not reach for her knife.
