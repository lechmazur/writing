The orchard hung in the ravine on cables, ninety baskets of soil slung between two cliffs, and every storm came up the gorge to shake them.  Marta walked the wires at dawn with a rake and a ledger, smelling the root balls for the black sweetness that meant truffle. 

Nine years after the empathy virus, nobody could keep a thought behind their teeth.  The fever had burned out the small muscle that chooses silence, and left the world honest and flinching.  Her son Teo was eleven, and he could not stop saying what he saw in people's faces.  "You smell like my father did before he drowned," he told Ivo the smuggler, and Ivo struck him with a rope end.  Nobody was lying anymore, and everybody was bruised.  He had learned to say the true thing and then cover his head, which was the worst thing Marta had ever measured. 

In her ledger, under the truffle weights, Marta wrote the problem the way she wrote soil pH: Goal, a place where a true sentence costs nothing.  She did not pray; she traded.  Truffles bought contraband, and contraband, that season, was books.  The Quarantine burned anything made before the fevers, so the old medical texts came up the ravine wrapped in oilcloth inside her own soil sacks, discredited, illegal, and full of rumored cures.  She read them by lantern while the baskets swung: the ocular theory of contagion, the lacrimal gate, the doctrine that truth escaped the body through the wet channel of the eye.  Occlude the channel with glass fired before the fevers, the books said, and the nerve will quiet.  Marta knew the anatomy was nonsense; she also knew that a hypothesis you refuse to test is only a superstition with better manners. 

So she paid Ivo four kilos of black truffle for the relic he had been hiding in his boot: an antique glass eye.  It had been made in the sealed city for a woman who wanted one face that gave nothing away, and its painted iris was the brown of wet bark.  Marta had a socket to put it in.  A snapped cable had taken her left eye three storms ago, and she wore a wool patch that itched.  She would not experiment on a child. 

She fitted the eye herself, occluding the socket, and began the count.  Baseline, forty-one involuntary truths in a working day; with the glass in place, forty-three.  She still told Ivo his hands shook, still told the pickers the orchard would fail, still could not swallow a single fact.  The cure was worthless, and she wrote that down too. 

But on the fourth day she noticed the pickers had stopped bracing when she turned toward them.  Glass does not flicker; glass does not narrow; nobody could tell where she was looking, and so nobody could read a verdict coming.  Teo came along the wire with a basket and said, "You are afraid the orchard will fall," and then he did not duck.  He waited.  That was the whole of it: the boy had stopped guessing what her face would do to him, and had started waiting for her answer. 

Safety was not silence.  Safety was what came after the sentence.  "Yes," she said.  "The cables are old, and I am afraid, and you are still going to stand here with me."  She began to teach it like a grafting technique: when a truth lands, name it, thank it, stay.  Ivo learned it badly, then less badly; the pickers learned it because the alternative was losing the harvest and the only kitchen in the ravine. 

In the ledger she crossed out cure and wrote instrument, then, under it: the books were wrong about the nerve and right about the face.  The Quarantine would burn the eye if they found it; she wore it anyway, and hid the books under the seed bench. 

Storm season came up the gorge again and the orchard swung like something breathing hard.  Marta occluded the lantern with her palm so no one would have to read a face in the swinging light, and counted the baskets by sound.  "Eighty-nine," Teo said, which was true, and nobody hit him.  The glass eye held what little light there was, unperturbed, while the real one wept in the wind, and Marta let it, and stayed.
