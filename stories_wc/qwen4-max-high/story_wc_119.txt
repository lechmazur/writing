After last orders, when the dishwashers went home and the neon fish went dark, Su Lin wiped table nine and wrote fortunes on the backs of old order slips.  The Golden Lantern bought its cookies from a factory in Oakland, but the slips inside were hers, forty a night, folded small as pills.  She wrote sideways because she had been trained to.  Years ago she had written specifications for microdot film at an optics plant upriver: emulsion grain, lines resolved per millimeter, a reduction ratio of four hundred to one.  A page of numbers that could shrink a contract to a freckle.  The specs were public, printed in any photographic manual, which was the joke of the trade: the secret was never the dot, only which page of ordinary numbers would teach a man to read it.  Harbor people understood that arrangement.  Everyone knew the lighthouse on Gull Rock still turned, though the Coast Guard had decommissioned it in 1979; everyone knew someone rowed out with propane on Tuesdays; nobody said so, the way nobody said whose money built the new pier.  The rock was a knuckle of granite a mile offshore, and the beam still swept the harbor every twelve seconds, faithful as a rumor.  An open secret is the safest room in the world; no one looks for a door in a wall they have agreed not to see.  Her fortunes were like that: plain enough to print, true enough to sting.  She had written "You will find what you did not lose," and a crabber hauled up his drowned brother's watch.  She had written "The light goes out on the ninth night," meaning only that the moon would be new, and on the ninth night the lamp on Gull Rock failed.  People began leaving tips for the fortune writer, not the waitress.  She was not a prophet; she was a woman who watched tides, coats, and ledgers, and who had learned that a true thing said slantwise gets believed.  In October a man she had not seen in eleven years ate the fish head soup and left a folded page under his bowl.  The specs, updated: new emulsion, new ratio, the recipe for reading the dots she had been gluing, as periods, onto her own fortune slips for eleven years.  She had never asked what the periods said; that was the arrangement, and the arrangement had kept her alive.  "Carry it out to the Rock on Tuesday," he said, "and then you're finished."  Finished meant retired; finished had also meant, in the old grammar, something else.  She wanted out, but not the way a lamp goes out.  She wanted the same tune in a different key: still words, still slantwise, still the sea, but sung by her, for her, at a pitch that did not require permission.  So she did not run, and she did not refuse; she orchestrated.  She could not order anyone to do anything, but she could direct the flow of a small town the way she had once directed light through a lens: by placing the right sentence in the right hand at the right hour.  To the harbormaster's wife: "Tuesday is lucky for crossing water."  To the Boy Scout troop's cookie order: "An old light deserves witnesses."  To the propane man: "Bring two tanks; generosity returns."  To the newspaper boy, who read every slip aloud on the pier: "The ninth night is Tuesday."  They went because she had been right before.  By dusk on Tuesday eleven boats were anchored off Gull Rock, lanterns strung, children counting the beam's slow turn, a thermos of coffee going hand to hand.  She rowed out through the flotilla with the page in her sleeve, and the soup man watched from the harbormaster's launch, unable to be anything but a tourist.  The keeper took the specs, held them against the lamp, and whistled at how boring they looked.  Numbers anyone could buy, and therefore numbers no one would ever hide.  She left him her final fortune, the period at its end still a dot she had never read: "The song is yours; change the key."  In the morning she took the southbound ferry, waving from the rail, named by everyone aboard.  The restaurant would open again, and the factory cookies would still arrive, but the slips inside would be somebody else's, plainer and less lucky.  After last orders, in a room above a chandlery two hundred miles down the coast, she wrote a fortune for herself and did not fold it.  It said: "You will be seen everywhere, and no one will look."
