Eleven years after the fossil fuel ban, our sea level station still runs on a wind turbine and a lead-acid battery, and the supply sail comes once a month.  I record the water.  Bawan records nothing, people say, since the stroke took his speech and his right hand.  He sits at the tide desk and taps.  When I arrived he tapped four quick, a pause, three slow, on the wood, on the rail, on my wrist when I brought his tea.  I called it habit, the way a moored boat still swings at anchor. 

I was sixteen the summer the reef went white.  Bawan was my dive master then, and we hung at nine meters above the Threadneedle, the pass the ferries used, and watched the coral glow like bone before it began to fray.  He tapped my tank twice, our old signal for look up, and I have been looking up ever since.  What I want is small and stubborn: to know why some paths disappear.  The Threadneedle shoaled, the ferries stopped, the women quit walking the reef flat at low water, and the village forgot the way across. 

In the station's paper atlas, the page holding our lagoon is gone.  Only a ragged stub remains at the binding, and for years I imagined Bawan tearing it out with his left hand, out of grief, to unmake the place where he lost the reef.  Guilt erases things; that was a story I could hold. 

Every high water he taps his phrase, and every high water I write the gauge height in the ledger and nod at him.  The tide is periodically eternal, twice a day forever, and I took his rhythm for the same kind of thing: a loop, a comfort, going nowhere. 

Then the battery failed in March, and prying the tide desk open to rewire it, I found a page taped flat against the underside, where the damp could not reach.  Our lagoon, printed before the ban, and across its face a faint grid of pencil numbers in a shaky left hand, with every row dated.  Soundings across the reef flat, taken at low water with a marked pole, one row of them each year, in a hand that got slower.  The Threadneedle column ran six point four, five point eight, four point three, two point four.  The last row was three weeks old, and at the Threadneedle it said two point one. 

I sat with the page until the tide turned, and then I heard him tapping above me, and for the first time in six years I listened instead of nodding.  Two quick, a pause, one slow.  Quick taps for meters, slow for tenths, the arithmetic of a tank knocked in dark water.  Two point one.  He had not been fidgeting; he had been reporting, twice a day, for six years, and the phrase had been shortening while I called it habit.  I had let his sentence become scenery, the way the village let the flat become sea.  He tore the page out not to erase the Threadneedle but because the printed depth was a lie, and he needed the paper. 

Here is why paths disappear.  When the coral died it stopped building, and the storms broke its skeletons into rubble, and the rubble crept west with every swell until it packed the pass shut.  A channel is not a line on paper; it is work, done by living things or by feet, and it closes when the work stops. 

So I assembled the chart.  I taped the torn page to the desk, entered every row against the ledger's tide heights, and reduced them to one datum, and the bar of rubble rose out of the numbers like a spine.  East of it, where nobody had looked because the atlas said shallows, the water had scoured a new gut, four meters and holding.  He had been walking the forgotten path for eighteen years to learn where the path had gone. 

In the morning I rowed him out on the high water and dropped the marked pole in the new gut, and he read it and tapped the gunwale four times, quick.  I tapped it back, and he laughed, which is only air, and then he tapped it again so I would write it down.  The supply sail will use it, and then the ferries, and the women will find the flat again, and in November the coral will spawn on the same moon it always has, periodically eternal, giving the rubble something to become.  We are moored here, and drifting, and the water keeps its own ledger.
