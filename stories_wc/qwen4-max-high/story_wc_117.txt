Sage Ridge kept its own calendar, and the deepest page was the six weeks the orchard people called the hush of dreaming roots, when the sap stayed down and the trees did nothing but wait.  Marta worked beneath the hush of dreaming roots three afternoons a week, with a guitar and a list of names.  She was a music therapist, mellow enough that families sometimes mistook her for slow.  She was not slow; she was softly adaptive, tuning to whatever key a room already hummed, dragging her strum slower until it matched somebody's breath.  She believed in ordinary magic: no sparks, no cures, only the strange fact that two people breathing in time begin to feel like one animal. 

In February, Wren brought a biscuit tin to the care home's kitchen door.  Inside, folded in wax paper, lay a curl of baby hair the color of dry sage.  Her father's, cut by his mother when he was one and kept through two houses and one fire.  Odell was ninety-one and had not spoken since his stroke in autumn.  "Make him come back," Wren said, and Marta heard the whole weight of a family leaning on four words.  So she was careful.  "I can't promise words," she said.  "I can promise three minutes of everybody looking at the same thing." 

On the twenty-eighth the moon's shadow would cross the ridge, the first time in sixty-three years.  Odell had been twenty-eight the last time, out in the rows with his mother, who wore that curl in a locket at her throat.  Wren knew the story because he told it every winter, and telling it was the last thing he had stopped doing. 

Marta spent nine days preparing, and most of the preparing was caution.  She checked the cloud forecasts, signed out eclipse glasses and a wheelchair blanket, and wrote a paragraph to read to Wren if nothing happened at all.  What she wanted was not a recovery but a seed: something a seven-year-old would still be turning over at ninety.  Then she went looking for the song.  Wren sang four bars of a lullaby about apples sleeping under snow, and Marta set the guitar beneath it, dropping the key until it sat where an old man's chest could reach.  She taught the chords to Wren's granddaughter, Pilar, who was seven and furious with impatience.  "Will he talk?" Pilar asked.  "Maybe a note," Marta said.  "A note counts."  She let herself want it carefully, the way you carry a full cup down a hall. 

The morning came gray, and then, unreasonably, clear.  They wheeled Odell to the fence line where the sage ran silver to the drop, and Marta folded the curl into his palm, because his hands were the only part of him that still went looking for things.  Pilar took the other one.  The light thinned to the color of a nickel, the hens went quiet, and the cold rose out of the ground like a second shadow.  Marta started the lullaby; Wren came in under her, then Pilar, then two aides in the doorway, and the sound linked them the way a rope links buckets down a well, nothing miraculous, just something to pull on.  In the last of the dark, Odell hummed.  One note, low, a third below hers, steady as a root.  Then the sun spilled back over the ridge and everybody stood around blaming the cold for their faces.  Marta did not whoop; she wrote the time in her notebook, and underlined nothing. 

He never said a word, that day or after, and he died in April when the sap came up.  But in the truck afterward Pilar asked whether the roots had known the dark was coming, and Marta said she did not know, and that not knowing was the good part.  Wren gave her the curl.  It lives in Marta's guitar case, under the spare strings, not a relic but a reminder that wonder is not a spark, it is a root: buried in the ordinary, dreaming through the hush, feeding somebody sixty-three years later.  She tells Pilar the story every winter, and every winter Pilar adds a question, which is how you keep a thing alive.
