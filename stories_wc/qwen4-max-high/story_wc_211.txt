The foghorn stopped at eleven minutes past four, and the whole city surfaced out of sleep at once, listening to the hole where the sound had been.  By five, Anselm Rooke had climbed forty-one flights to the orchard, because in that city an oath was binding only under its trees.  He was ninety-one, the venerable oath scribe, and he spoke six languages no living mouth had argued in for two thousand years.  That was the point of them: a promise made in Hittite or Etruscan could not drift, could not be renegotiated by a dictionary.  The orchard was older than the tower under it.  Its trees had come up in barges a century ago and had been grafted into one another ever since, so that nine trunks held forty kinds of apple and nobody could say where one tree ended.  Beneath them stood his cabinet, and in the cabinet, the shavings.  Every oath was cut into a wax cylinder; when the oath was kept or forgiven, the cylinder was shaved smooth for the next promise, and the shaving came away in one long curl, still carrying its groove.  The city archive kept the words; Rooke kept the curls, sorted not by year or name but by tremor.  Grief-shaped, relief-shaped, dread-shaped.  He had wanted, for sixty years, a record of how it felt to be a person, since words lie and the body's music does not.  He had learned the trick in third grade, on a field trip to this same roof.  The scribe then was a woman named Iseult, and she let him immerse a curl in her thermos cup of warm water until it went slack, then lay it along a glass plate and draw a needle down it.  A stranger's voice rose out of the water, promising to love someone until death, and eight-year-old Rooke asked the curious question that organized his life: what happens to the parts you shave off?  They wait, Iseult said.  All that afternoon the horn had sounded every forty seconds, and he had counted it like a pulse.  Now the horn had stopped, and Rooke understood that whatever had been paying to keep the water out had stopped paying.  He carried the dread drawer to the potting bench and lit the alcohol lamp.  One by one he immersed the curls, laid them flat, and joined them end to end with a warm blade, in the order he had always kept them: deepest tremor to shallowest.  Sixty years of strangers, sorted by feeling, made one ribbon eleven feet long.  He had never played them together; there had never been a reason.  The needle went down, and the ribbon spoke.  It was not babble.  Fragments from forty mouths across forty years had fallen into grammar the way sorted sand falls into ripples, and the grammar was Hittite, which no one alive but Rooke could hear as speech.  It said: when the horn is quiet, men will climb the stairs to buy the trees, and the keeper will open the gate for them, and afterward he will swear that he was never asked.  Rooke sat very still in the smell of wet wax and rotting windfalls.  He had meant only to keep the shapes of feeling, and it turned out that dread, arranged honestly, is a sentence.  The first curl, the deepest tremor, was his own at nineteen, swearing to keep the orchard alive.  Then the stairwell door rang, three knocks, polite as a doctor.  Two surveyors and a clerk with a fresh cylinder in a velvet case: the sale needed a witness oath, sworn in the old tongue, under the trees.  The clerk spoke Hittite beautifully and had no idea what he was saying.  Rooke's hands were already trembling in the pattern the drawer had taught him.  He could refuse, and the sale would stall a season, perhaps two.  But he was ninety-one, and the trees needed water he could no longer carry up forty-one flights.  He opened the gate.  Under the nine trunks he witnessed the sale, and the needle bit the wax, and the clerk's voice went in smooth as cream, without one tremor, because the man did not know he was promising anything.  Afterward the clerk asked, for the record, whether the keeper had been consulted and had agreed.  In the dead tongue, softly, Rooke swore that nobody had ever asked him anything.  It was the only lie in sixty years of cylinders, and he made it so the archive would never show a man who had chosen.  The shaving came away in one long curl.  He would file it where it belonged, and someday someone would sort it into its place, and hear the sentence again.
