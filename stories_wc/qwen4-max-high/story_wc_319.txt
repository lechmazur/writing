The library had swallowed the garden whole, and had not finished digesting it.  Ice held the roses mid-nod; mist moved between the beds like something reading over their shoulders.  Where the lamplight stopped, the gravel stopped too, and beyond that edge there was only the soft unfinished dark. 

The guardian walked that edge on four limbs, because two had begun to feel like a suggestion she no longer took.  Her nails were black from turning pages and from other work.  She had a name once, in a world where light did not end but only thinned forever, like a road; here it stopped, clean as a cut, and she was homesick in a way that had no word in either language. 

The cultists had come in winter, eleven of them, wanting to widen the dark.  She had been faster than their candles, and had never asked why anyone would want the dark made wider.  Afterward she found a pendant in the frost of the swallowed garden: a bone ring around a lens of black glass, still warm, as if the throat it had hung on were somewhere else and doing fine. 

This was the library's whole art: it took outsides and made them insides.  Weather became a chapter; a hillside became a shelf; the garden had been a hedge maze outside, and now it was a room with no ceiling and frost on everything, and its mist was the last of its outdoors, breathing.  The guardian's job was to keep the swallowing tidy.  Anything that crossed the boundary of light uninvited, she tore. 

The pendant showed her the reverse.  Against her closed eyelid it turned the room back into a hillside, and past the hillside, past a green sky, her own street with its lamps bleeding softly outward forever.  It was a lens for dreaming, not for looking.  It worked through the veil of sleep, and she had not slept in three years, because a guardian who sleeps is a door. 

The garden was dying.  Frost had crept from the beds into the stacks; the books were furring with ice, and the mist got thinner each night, like breath in a body running out.  She knew what the library did to rooms it could not finish: it closed them.  She had heard the shelves swallow whole wings, and afterward the catalogue simply had fewer lines.  To save the garden she had to let it go back outside, and the pendant could only do that from inside a dream.  Her watching body was the lock. 

She had tried twice, and both times her body had thrown her awake snarling at the dark, because the dark was a thing with teeth and she had never once let it come close enough to learn its face.  That was the work, then.  Not guarding.  Making friends with uncertainty. 

She lay down in the frozen garden with the pendant in her fist and did the hardest thing she knew: she waited.  Mist came and read her.  The lamp guttered; the boundary crept toward her like a tide; she did not get up.  Her legs wanted to run the perimeter; she let them want.  Ice cracked somewhere in the roses, a sound like a knuckle.  She waited until waiting stopped being a held breath and became a lying-down one, and sleep came over her like mist over gravel. 

Through the veil of sleep she stood at the boundary and saw it truly: not a wall, a shore.  The dark beyond was not empty; it was unwritten, which is different.  Something came out of it walking on four limbs, wearing her shape, and she did what a feral thing does when it has decided to trust: she rolled and showed her throat.  It put its cold nose there and breathed, and its breath was the garden's mist, and the pendant in her sleeping hand went warm as a held finger. 

She woke to dripping.  The roses were nodding, finally, all the way down; water ran off the beds and into the stacks, and the books drank it without ruining, which is a thing books do only outdoors.  The lamp still burned, but its edge had moved a hand's width past where it used to stop, and the gravel there was real, and cold, and hers to walk on. 

She was still homesick; the green sky was still elsewhere.  But home had stopped being behind her.  She put the pendant on, lay down at the new edge, and waited, and the dark came and sat with her, and neither of them hurried.
