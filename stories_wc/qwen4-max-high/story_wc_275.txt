The morning after the tower fell, ash lay on the dome in a gray drift, and inside, the desert roses opened as if nothing had happened.  Nell knelt in the red sand and worked down the row she had named Patience.  She had raised them on repetition, the way her mother had raised her: say the value aloud, prune toward it, water only when the plant has earned the wait.  Ten years of that, and Patience held its blooms nine days instead of three.  The rows behind it were Mercy, which had no thorns, and Continuance, which she had never let die.  Her father's airship came in low over the glass, throwing sand against the panels.  He had cut his ballast to climb the debris, and the empty sandbags trailed from the gondola like shed skin.  Nell was senselessly perceptive; it was not a gift she could spend.  She could see the quarry grit in his boot seams, the tremor in his left hand, the fact that the blood on his sleeve was not his, and none of it told her what to do.  Perception without a use was just weather in her skull.  He came through the airlock with a scrap of ballast canvas in his fist and his ledger under his arm.  The last name in it was crossed out.  "The guild's gone," he said.  "The tower's gone, and every man I ever hunted is loose in the flats, and this dome is the only thing out here worth stealing."  He set her mother's knife on the potting bench.  Along the blade, in letters worn shallow by forty years of whetstone, someone had engraved QUIET.  Her mother had cut rose stems with it, and her father had cut men, and the word had stayed on through both.  "Take it," he said.  "I won't," Nell said.  She had never once raised a hand, and the farm was her argument.  "Then you'll lose the farm to the first hungry man who walks in."  He was not cruel; he was arithmetic, and the arithmetic of the flats had changed overnight.  Nell picked up the knife and turned it over, and the sun through the ash came down red and level, and the whole dome burned without moving.  This was the light she worked in: a blaze with no wind in it, heat you could hear the glass holding.  She knelt at Mercy and opened a slit in the thornless rootstock.  Into it she slid a scion from Patience, so that one plant would carry both values instead of choosing between them.  That was the work: not stopping the chaos, but threading something living through it so the far side had a shape.  She tore the ballast scrap into strips and bound the union tight.  The canvas smelled of the tower's quarry, because that was where he had loaded his ballast, and now the tower was in her hands as sand.  She scattered it through the beds, a spoonful to a row, the way you teach a plant something new: slowly, and by the roots.  With the rest of the scrap she sewed a sheath, doubled and stitched with waxed thread, and it fit the blade exactly.  She slid the knife home and the engraved word went dark.  Quiet was not the absence of the blade; it was the blade kept, covered, and carried where it could not cut.  She hung it on his belt, not hers.  "Keep it sheathed," she said.  "And stay for the blooming."  He looked at the graft, at the row with no thorns, at the sand he had dropped from the sky now spread through her soil.  He had spent thirty years ending things; she was asking him to be present for something that continued.  He sat down on the bench, which was the first still thing he had done in years.  Outside, the flats smoked and the dome held its red heat, and inside, nine days of bloom were coming, and the ledger lay unopened under the bench.  She could still see everything, and for once the seeing had somewhere to go.
