The tide left twice a day, and twice a day the island remembered it was only a hill.  Ilse painted the leaving.  Eleven years after the water wars she had four hundred canvases of the same causeway at the same hour, and no sleep worth the name.  Sleeplessness makes you honest, she said, because it leaves no energy for lying.  The island lived on cisterns and fog nets now.  Nobody fought over rain; you cannot own weather yet.  What the wars had drowned was the mainland: reservoirs gone brackish, the town below the causeway silted to its chimneys.  In every canvas, at the edge of the road, there was a small gold glint.  She painted it every time and called it a fault in her eye.  It was her mother's amber brooch, a midge inside it, still stepping through a resin that had once been a wound.  On the causeway, in the last dry summer, she had traded it to a water carter for two liters.  She had meant to carry the water back to the column of walkers behind her.  She drank it herself, walking, and lived.  Aud gleaned the flats at low water and sold what the mud gave back: buckles, teeth, a bell clapper.  Aud found Ilse at the easel at three in the morning and did not remark on it.  "You look like something the tide refused," Aud said.  "Twice," Ilse said.  "I keep coming back until the picture tells me what it was."  Painting is threshing, she added: you beat the same sheaf until something edible falls out.  After the equinox rains, Aud knocked with her boots full of sand.  "Follow the rain trails," she said.  Fresh water cuts the sand where the old works lie, because the fill is softer than the field.  "Where the trails braid together, dig."  Ilse went down before dawn with a trowel and her heart going like a moth in a jar.  The tide was out past the third stake.  Rain had written the whole flat over in a script of small channels, running, joining, running.  She followed them the way you follow a sentence, keeping the thread, losing it, finding it again.  They gathered toward one hollow, and the hollow was the crossing.  There the scour had laid bare the crown of the old road and the water main, side by side, then touching.  She had painted them apart in four hundred canvases, the way she kept them apart in her head: the people over here, the water over there.  They had never been apart.  The column must have camped on the main all night, over water nobody knew was there.  And in the mud at the joining, gold.  The carter would have gone back down the road with the walkers; the brooch lay where his pocket or his hand had let it go.  The sea had kept it eleven years and handed it to a rain trail.  The midge was still stepping.  Ilse sat down in the wet and made a sound she had not made in eleven years.  Eleven years of guessing, and the mud had simply held the answer, the way mud does.  Aud came down the flat with her sack.  "Everything the flats give is gleanings," Aud said, "and gleanings are mine to trade."  Ilse had no money that meant anything, and offered her the canvases.  Aud shook her head.  "My brother was in that column," she said.  "Give me the hour."  "All of it, including the part you leave out."  So Ilse traded.  She told it standing in the cold: the brooch price, the two liters, the walking, the drinking, the not turning around.  She told about the pipe under the sleepers, which she had learned at dawn, and which changed nothing and everything.  Aud listened with her jaw set, then put the brooch into Ilse's hand and closed the fingers over it.  "That is the grain," Aud said.  "You have been beating chaff."  The tide turned while they walked back, and Ilse let it chase her, laughing in a wrecked sort of way.  In the studio she set the amber on the sill, where the light came through it and laid a small gold glint on the floor.  She had blamed her eye; it had been the world, waiting to be dug up.  She stretched a new canvas and painted rain trails braiding into one hollow.  Then she slept four hours, and the tide came and went without her.
