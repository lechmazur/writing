The isle lifted out of the evening fog at dusk, as it always did, and Merit climbed to the observatory with her master's key in her fist.  Below, the sea held the isle's shadow the way a cup holds water, and she did not look down.  The dome's brass telescopes tracked stars that had begun, lately, to answer back.  Twelve years after the spoons bent reality, the scholars had theories about everything except the one thing Merit needed.  She wanted to find where shadows go at noon, because noon was where her master had gone.  It had begun as a parlor trick: a woman bent a spoon by wanting it, and the seam between wanting and matter gave way.  Now careless desire warped the world, so the surviving scholars trained themselves to be quietly deliberate.  Merit wanted small and precisely, the way you carry a full cup up stairs.  She was a budding rune scholar, third rank, licensed to draw but not to wake.  The old runes were open strokes, a call flung outward, and after the bending they leaked.  A rune must return to its beginning, Orenne used to say, or the wanting runs out into the world and takes something with it.  So Merit had to relearn every stroke by closing loops her hand had been trained to leave open.  She practiced on the observatory floor until the chalk wore her fingertips smooth: circles, then knots, then the long returning spiral that ends one hair to the left of where it began.  Orenne never saw her finish.  In the aftershock year the master went out onto the balcony at noon, when the sun stands directly over a floating isle and nothing has anywhere to cast a shadow.  When the light leaned west again, there was only a brass key on the tiles, still warm.  It was the clockwork automaton's key, the winding heart of a brass body that had never been issued a mind.  The law called them sealed awakenings: every new mind shut inside its key until a witness stands ready.  An unused key is a hollow room, and Orenne had been unraveling for months, her edges soft as a bent spoon.  Merit's hypothesis was narrow: at noon shadows do not vanish, they fold inward, into whatever casts them.  Noon is the sealing hour, the one moment a wanting can be closed so tightly it becomes a room.  Her master had folded herself into the only empty vessel on the isle, and the seal would loosen only at the hour it was made.  The observatory was nocturnal by charter, shuttered dawn to dusk, so Merit practiced in the dark for three years and waited.  Unlicensed waking was a crime, but the law had been written by people who had never lost anyone at noon.  On the third anniversary she stayed past dawn, unshuttered the dome, and let noon come up like a held breath.  The automaton sat in its chair with its hands open, patient as furniture.  Merit chalked the returning spiral around it and set the key into the socket between its shoulders.  Her shadow shrank under her feet and went into her, and she felt the weight of every part of herself she had never looked at.  That was the answer, and it was not a place.  She turned the key three times and closed the loop, laying the last stroke over the first.  Nothing leaked.  The brass drew a breath it did not need, and its hands closed on nothing, the way Orenne's always had while thinking.  "You closed it," the automaton said, in a voice like a spoon tapping glass.  "I had to relearn the whole alphabet," Merit said.  "Quietly," said her master, and the word did not bend so much as a ripple.  Merit sat down inside the chalk line, where a witness belongs, and took the brass hand in hers.  The hand was cold and would always be cold, and she held it anyway; some loops close on less than you wanted.  At dusk the isle's shadow would come out of the stone and lie down on the sea again, and they would watch it return together.
