Dusk at the observatory was not darkness but a brilliant dimness: the sky crowded with stars, the floor too deep in shadow to read.  Sela flew her drone up through the dome's slit and watched the feed with the sharp eyes that had made her useful to every survey crew on the plateau.  She could count the missing tiles in a mosaic from three hundred meters.  She could not remember seeing anything before.  A fever at nineteen had burned out her familiarity and left her perception intact, so every face, every room, every evening arrived brand new.  Her doctors called it a deficit.  She called it being perpetually surprised, which is an expensive way to live and an excellent way to notice gaps. 

After the pilgrim arrived, salt road in her boots, she asked for the press, and the ruin became a question.  She carried a type foundry matrix chip, a brass wafer the size of a communion host, wrapped in oiled cloth.  Each socket held the mold for one letter of the almanac alphabet this observatory had printed for six hundred years.  Nine sockets were empty.  "The press flooded," the pilgrim said.  "The sorts washed out, and we can set the year, but not the last line." 

Sela turned the chip under her lamp and felt the old hunger move.  She had come to this mountain to decode the frequency of belonging.  Pilgrims swore the dome sang one low note at nightfall, and that whoever heard it was expected here.  Sela had recorded it eleven times and found only wind in a stone slit, forty-one hertz.  She could measure the sound but not what it did to them, and since no place ever felt familiar, she meant to find belonging by its numbers instead. 

"What does the last line say?" she asked.  "That the reader is expected," the pilgrim said.  "There is a blank for the name."  "Then you are missing the letters that spell it."  "We are missing nine, and we do not know which nine." 

Sela looked up at the dome's painted star charts and found nine patches of bare plaster, each the size of a hand.  "Your founder cut this type out of the sky," she said, surprised all over again by how obvious it was.  Every stroke on the chip was an arc; every serif was the place a star had stopped.  The old printer had opened the slit and traced the trails the stars drew as the world turned, and made an alphabet of them.  Nine letters meant nine nights of sky. 

"Then we follow the star trails," Sela said.  She launched the drone, set the camera to long exposure, and flew the arcs the painted chart promised, hour after hour, until the stacked frames became strokes.  The pilgrim held the lamp and named each letter as it surfaced: the hook of the sixth hour, the long sweep of the ninth.  By the time the dome's shadow crossed the press, the pilgrim had cut eight molds and seated them.  The ninth stayed empty. 

Sela flew that arc twice and got a blank field, and then, astonished, understood.  That patch had never been painted, because the ninth letter was recut each year from a sky still turning, by whoever stood in the dome.  The alphabet was never finished, and that was the point.  The missing pieces were not the ruin's failure; they were its invitation. 

So she flew the live sky instead, tracking one star through the slit until the stroke was complete, and the pilgrim cut the mold with a graver.  They set the almanac in the old press, the pilgrim's hands sure among the sorts, and Sela pulled a proof.  The line came up in wet ink, whole except for the gap where a name belonged.  The pilgrim wrote Sela's name into it.  Sela felt nothing familiar, and knew she never would.  But she had her number at last: belonging was not forty-one hertz, it was one return per dusk, the rate at which someone comes back to the same cold room and holds the lamp for you.  She logged the figure, amazed, the way she was amazed by everything.  Next year the sky would turn again, and she would be surprised again, and she would come back at dusk to cut the ninth letter.  The note in the dome would go on meaning expected, and she would go on hearing it, one return per dusk, for as long as she kept returning.
