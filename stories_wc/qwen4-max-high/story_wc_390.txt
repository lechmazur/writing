The sun came back for eleven minutes that evening, laying gold along the drift ice like a hand laid on a fever.  Ilse Varga, lighthouse engineer, climbed the stair in her azure coat and counted the minutes aloud, the way she counted everything, so the numbers would be real.  Below her, the marina lay locked in floes that ground together all winter, a hundred hulls held like teeth in a jaw.  Under the tower floor was the crypt, and in the crypt hung the bronze throat the old keepers had cast to forgive the drowned.  The crypt keeper's key hung against her sternum on a bootlace, warm as flesh, its shank filed by nine generations of hands to a single pure pitch.  A key that opened a lock and, struck, opened something else.  Hallgrim's salvage barge waited at the ice mouth with a winch and a bill of sale from the diocese.  Bronze was bronze; the bishop had a roof to mend.  They would come at full dark, when the light no longer needed her.  The drowned were still in the water.  She could hear them in the hulls at night, a sound like men breathing through wool.  Absolution was only physics, Ilse had decided long ago: grief has a frequency, and forgiveness is that frequency met exactly, so the two waves cancel and the water goes quiet.  She had written it in her ledger, and beneath it she had written the air temperature at which she cried, because a number was a thing she could hold.  Her father was on the list of the drowned, ninth from the top, in her own handwriting.  For three winters she had wanted to strike the key at his pitch alone, and for three winters she had not, because the tone must fit the whole harbor or it fits nothing.  This year the ice had come in wrong.  The floes had wedged into the marina's throat and shortened it, and a shortened throat sings higher; the old figure in her ledger was now a lie.  She would have to adapt, and she had eleven minutes of gold to do it in, because ice only finds its voice when the sun leaves and the cold pulls it tight.  She could hide the throat in the ice and keep it, and the drowned would wait another year; or she could spend it tonight, out loud, where the men would hear.  She went down.  The crypt smelled of tallow and wet stone, and the bronze throat hung in its cradle like a sleeping animal, taller than she was, sweating the cold.  Above, through the floor, she heard the barge winch start early.  Ilse put her cheek to the metal, then her breastbone, then the soft place under her jaw, listening with the bones of her skull the way she had learned from her father.  She hummed low, felt the floes answer wrong, raised it, felt them answer wrong again, and went on adjusting until the shiver in her teeth and the shiver in the ice were one shiver.  Vibrational matching, the ledger called it; her father had called it standing close enough to be forgiven too.  The note she found was not his note.  It was the harbor's note, higher and colder, and it went through her ribs and the salt of her blood, and she was flesh and she was a wire.  She struck the key against the bronze.  The tone went out through the crypt floor, into the frozen marina, along every hull, and the water under the ice let go of its dead.  She heard them go the way you hear a held breath end.  Ninth from the top, her father, and she could not keep him back, and she did not try.  On the barge, Hallgrim dropped the winch handle.  Nine winters ago he had cut his tow line and run from the ice and left his brother's boat behind, and now, through the steel deck under his boots, his brother's voice came up and said his name without hatred in it.  Men like Hallgrim do not crate what has forgiven them.  She heard the barge chain come slack, then the engines, then nothing.  The gold went off the ice like breath off a window.  Ilse sat on the crypt floor with the key against her sternum and wrote the new figure in the ledger, and beside it the temperature, and beside that, in the same steady hand, that she had loved him and let him go.  Numbers were things she could hold.  Above her the lamp turned, and the bronze hung silent in the dark, spent until the ice came back, and safe.
