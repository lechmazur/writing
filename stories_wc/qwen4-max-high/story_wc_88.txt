After the procedure, they handed me a coat, a transit pass, and a baseball I did not recognize.  The clerk said the ball was mine, that I had refused to surrender it, that men like me sometimes kept one thing.  Men like me.  That was the whole biography they gave back: a category.  I had asked for the clean slate myself, signed for it, paid in installments.  What I wanted was to stop hearing the sound a chair makes when it is bolted to a floor.  The clinic took the sound.  It also took whatever in me had once flinched, and I did not notice the difference for eleven days.  Then a boy fell under the tram on Kessler Street, and I watched the crowd's faces the way you watch weather.  Nothing moved in me, and I understood that a slate is clean because nothing is written on it, including the parts that let you read other people.  So I went looking for the lake.  The newspapers were no help and all help.  Since the Amnesties, every issue came with holes: white rectangles where names had been lifted out mid-paragraph.  You could not read the articles, but you could read between the lines, and the gaps had a grammar.  Widths gave syllables; the rhythm of the blanks gave street names; three narrow gaps in a column, repeated Thursdays, gave a stairwell behind the fish market.  I had been, I think, a man who made those gaps.  Now I used them as a map, which is the only joke the state ever told me.  The journey took four nights: freight tunnel, culvert, a ladder of rungs slick as fish bone.  Then the rock opened and I stopped being clever.  The lake lay under a mile of stone, lit from inside by algae that ate what the clinics flushed away.  Every erased hour in the city ended here and became light.  Blue, then a green so soft it seemed polite.  I had seen a lot of beauty sold at a markup; this was the first thing in years that did not want anything from me.  Around the water the tunnels forked and forked again.  The ferryman, an old woman with a punt pole, called it the labyrinth of choices.  Each fork was a decision somebody had unmade; walk one and you got that hour back, and only that one, and you could not walk twice.  "You can have one," she said.  "Most men take the hour they were happy."  I stood at the mouth of a hundred corridors and felt nothing pull.  That was the trouble with a clean slate: no chalk, no preference.  So I used the only evidence I had left, and turned the baseball over.  The signature ran across the sweet spot in violet ink: Ines Vail, 14 June 2071.  Forty-six years past the calendar on the wall.  The ink was a violet no press in our city could mix.  The leather was scuffed, the stitching delicately robust, decades of handling and still holding its shape.  A woman had signed a ball in a year that had not happened, and someone had let her keep it long enough to hand it to me.  I did not believe in time travelers.  I believed in the ball, and in the fact that my hands had refused to give it up while the machines took everything else.  That was a choice I had made once, and I wanted it back.  I found the corridor whose light bent violet.  It took an hour to walk and an hour to live.  A room, a chair bolted down, a woman with a split lip saying, "You will need this later," and me laughing, and taking it anyway, and writing her name into a gap.  I came out of the tunnel on my knees in the cold blue shine, making a sound I had never made.  Not for me.  For her, who had reached across forty-six years to put a ball in the hands of the man who would erase her.  The ferryman watched without much interest; she had seen it before, and it never lasted, she said.  I let her say it.  I gave the ball back to the ferryman, to keep for the next clean man who came down with nothing in his hands.  Then I climbed toward the fish market and the Thursday paper.  I know the widths of the blanks.  I know how many letters a name needs.  Starting with hers, I am going to write them back in, one gap at a time, and let the city read between the lines.
