The port keeps two clocks, one for the cranes and one for the water.  I work by the second.  At low tide the lower tiers of the container stacks stand open, rust corridors under nine levels of steel, and I go in with a lamp and a satchel.  At high tide the water takes them back, and I climb up and listen.  Nobody calls me anything. 

Eleven years ago a clerk typed two digits of a certificate number in the wrong order, and the state agreed that I had died of poisoning in a warehouse on this pier.  They found me asleep beside a leaking drum and did not check my pulse twice.  The error was never corrected; correcting it would have meant admitting that a whole shelf of files had already been pulped.  My apartment went to a nephew, my photographs to a landfill, my name to a drawer that no longer opens.  I kept one thing from that warehouse: a label peeled off a bottle, skull and crossbones, the chemical name scraped away by a thumbnail I do not remember using.  It is the only document I own, and it says nothing. 

Since then the world arrives to me as texture instead of picture.  Faces slide off; voices stay, as burlap, as oilcloth, as the slick of a fish skin.  The doctors would call it damage.  I call it a different sieve.  So I collect what falls through other people's mouths.  On the high catwalks the crane crews talk while the water rises, and I lie flat above them and take pieces.  A mother's kitchen in a country with no more coastline.  A dog that waited at a bus stop for nine years.  Half a lullaby, the second half lost to a winch.  I write each fragment on paper I dry from scrap pulp, and I layer them.  Sheet over sheet, glued with starch, pressed under a bolt until the words show through as ridges.  Then I read with my fingers, which is the only reading I have left.  I am not building an archive.  I am trying to learn what a memory feels like, so that I might recognize my own if I ever touch it. 

In my ninth year I overheard a watchman complain about a container of municipal records that nobody would sign for.  Dead files, he said, the whole erased shelf of the city, going out with the scrap paper on the spring tide.  He gave the number to a colleague, and the number came to me as coarse tweed, and I kept it.  The box sat in the drowned tier, four levels down, where the harbor lives twice a day.  For two years I mapped the labyrinth against the tide tables, learning which corridors drained and which held their breath.  Ordinary low water gave me forty minutes; I needed ninety.  So I waited for the year's lowest ebb, and while I waited I layered. 

It came on a black morning in March, the water sliding out farther than I had ever seen, barnacles standing naked and ticking.  I went down through four levels of rust, counting doors by touch.  The seal broke like a knuckle.  Inside: paper, swollen, half-rotted, the city's forgotten dead pressed into a single grey brick.  I put my hands in and could not tell one life from another.  Every file had the same nap, the same salt-stiff warmth, like the skin on cooling milk.  That was the answer, and it was not the one I came for.  Memory has no fingerprint.  Mine would not feel different from a stevedore's or a stranger's; there was no texture that would say, this was yours.  The tide turned somewhere above me; I heard it start to speak in the pipes.  I took one handful, and I left the rest to the water. 

On the catwalk that night I layered the handful into my sheets, and last of all I laid in the poison label, its scraped blank facing up.  Under my fingers the erased place was the smoothest part, and I finally understood that this was what I had been feeling all along.  Now I go up where the crews talk, and I talk too.  I give them a woman who died on paper and kept walking, a bottle with no name, a city dissolved into a brick.  They will get the details wrong.  Let them; that is how a history survives here, in pieces, in other people's mouths, carried out and back on the tide.  I have no name to leave behind, only a texture, and someone will layer it.
