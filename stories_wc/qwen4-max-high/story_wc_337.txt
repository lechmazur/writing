The sanctuary had no walls, only prisms, and starlight came apart in them.  Vera knelt in the wreckage of that light and restored the faces of the drowned.  Forty-one masks, chin by chin, cheek by cheek, glued from the shards the sea gave back.  Forty-one masks, and still the dead would not lie down.  They drifted through the prisms in colored pieces, restless as light with nowhere to land.  Her own mask was cracked across the jaw, porcelain, and she never repaired it.  A restorer who cannot mend herself learns to be exact about everything else. 

Anselm, the keeper, said the faces were not the problem.  The dead need weight, he said, and weight is only silence that has been kept long enough.  He showed her the net.  It hung from the sanctuary's ribs, three centuries of it, gray as old salt.  Every fisherman who went out had tied one knot into it, and into the knot he put the thing he could not say.  The net accumulated silence the way a hull accumulates barnacles, and it sagged with the weight of it.  Vera understood then what she had been making: faces without ballast. 

She took her mother's tapestry needle from her kit, blunt-tipped, wide-eyed, made for parting threads without cutting them.  A knife would spill the hush.  Unraveled, knot by knot, the silence stayed interlaced, one long thread of held breath.  That was the whole art: to keep the hush interlaced while taking it apart. 

There was a condition, Anselm said, and he was embarrassed by it.  The hush only holds while something true is being told.  A secret, spoken start to finish, no stalling in the middle.  Stop talking and the thread goes slack and worthless.  So she began, and the work and the telling took the same length of time. 

She said her brother's name, Tomas, and the first knot opened like a mouth.  She said she was fifteen and holding the line off the rocks.  The needle worked; the hush came off the net in a slow gray ribbon onto the spool.  She said the rope had burned through her palms and she had let go on purpose.  Above her, a smear of green light stopped moving and listened.  She said she had told everyone the rope broke.  She said she had restored his mask first, before all the others, and had made him beautiful and calm, and it had not helped. 

Sixty knots, eighty, the spool fat with other men's unsaid things.  Her hands shook and the blunt needle would not cut anything, which was mercy.  She said she had been glad, for one second, that it was him and not her.  That was the secret's floor, and she said it plainly, and the last knot came undone.  The net hung empty and light, three hundred years of keeping spent into one spool. 

Anselm brought the spade.  The drowned lay under the sanctuary floor, so the starlight could reach them through the prisms.  She lifted the slab marked with his name and began to exhume him.  Salt sand, dry as flour, then the shroud, then the small architecture of a boy.  His mask sat where she had put it, perfect, useless.  She wound the interlaced hush through his ribs, around the wrists, between the teeth.  Three centuries of not saying, laid into a body that had waited for weight.  He went down into himself the way a stone goes down in water. 

All through the sanctuary the colored pieces gathered and fell.  The prisms, with nothing left to scatter, gave back plain white starlight.  Forty-one dead, quiet in the ground, and the floor stone warm under her palms.  Vera sat in the light and took off her cracked mask and set it on the grave.  She had no more silence to give anyone.  The needle lay in her kit, threaded with nothing, blunt and honest.  Anselm had not once looked away, which was its own kind of repair.  She would be restless for a while, and awake, and that seemed like the correct price.
