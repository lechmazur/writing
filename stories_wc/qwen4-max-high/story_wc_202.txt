The alchemist's market hung above the rooftops on cables thick as a wrist, swaying whenever the wind came off the water.  Mira crossed it the way she had at nine years old, one hand on the rail, her satchel of anchors knocking against her hip.  She was a between-worlds translator, licensed to carry meaning from the drowned city up into the dry one.  The work was not vocabulary.  It was anchors of intuition: a weight, a smell, a scrap of texture that held a feeling steady while she carried it across.  Without an anchor, a word arrived on the far side hollow, correct and dead.  Her best anchor was a fragment of lighthouse lens, a wedge of glass the size of a plum, strung on a cord at her throat.  Her mother had ground it, and taught her to read paint through it.  Through its ridges, light came apart into layers, and the world turned elusively tangible, like a hand you could feel but never close on.  That was the point.  Her mother's presence had the same quality: lamp oil, a warm wrist, gone when she reached.  At the last stall a delegate from below waited in a coat of oiled canvas, water still beading on his shoulders.  He had come to buy a translation of the market's founding charter.  The charter was a painting.  Everything official up here was: the alchemists distrusted ink, which could be scraped, and trusted pigment, which layered.  The panel showed nine founders in red robes, raising the market on its cables above a flat and empty sea.  Mira had translated it twice before, and both times the sentence about the sea had come out of her mouth like a stone.  Hollow.  This time she lifted the lens fragment and looked through it at the painted water.  Hidden details rose through the top layer the way fish rise under ice.  Beneath the empty sea there was a city: roofs, chimneys, a lighthouse, and along the lighthouse gallery a line of small figures with rags in their hands.  Women, polishing the lens.  Under the nine red robes, in the underpainting, were nine more figures in oilskin, and beside each one a name in a hand she knew from the margins of her schoolbooks.  The market had not been raised by nine men.  It had been raised by the lamp keepers of the drowned city, who strung the first cables so their light could keep burning above the flood.  The alchemists had painted over the keepers' own charter and called the sea empty.  Her mother's name was fourth.  Between childhood and now, Mira had believed the light went out because the water came.  It went out because the men who owned the market stopped buying oil for a light they no longer needed.  The delegate was waiting.  In his language there was no word for founder that did not also mean the one who is owed.  To say the nine names correctly she needed an anchor the drowned could hold too, something with the weight of glass and lamp oil and a warm wrist.  She had exactly one.  The stall keeper pointed to the bezel at the panel's corner, where a translator fixed the anchor that made a reading true.  He asked her twice whether she was sure.  Mira unfastened the cord.  The knot had been her mother's, tied when Mira was nine and the light was still lit, and it came apart in the time it takes to notice a thing is gone.  She set the fragment in the bezel, over the painted water.  Anyone who looked through it now would see the oilskin figures, the rags, the nine names.  The delegate looked, and made a sound she did not have to translate.  Then he read the names aloud in his own tongue, and each one arrived on this side whole, because the glass held them steady.  Her mother's name went past her like a warm wrist in the dark, there and not there, elusively tangible, and Mira let it go by.  She had given away the only thing that could summon her, and the market swayed, and the wind smelled of vinegar and hot brass, and the instant did not close.  It has not closed since.  She stands in it still, ninety feet above the flood, holding nothing, keeping the account open.
