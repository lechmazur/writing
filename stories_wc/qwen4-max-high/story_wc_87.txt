Vera reached the coastal kayak launch an hour before slack water.  The boatyard where her father taught her to read water was concrete now, striped with yellow lines, and the rental kayaks stood in a rack like bright teeth.  She had come here to be forgiven, which was the first thing that failed.  Ida, the mate's daughter, had promised to paddle out with her to the shoal.  At six that morning the text arrived: I can't, Vera, maybe next year, I'm sorry.  Nine years Ida had been almost ready, and Vera had built a whole life on the almost.  There is an hour when expectations collapse, and the tide keeps going out regardless.  Vera sat on the ramp and laughed, because she always laughed at the worst of it, a habit her crew had called merrily melancholic and her husband had called unbearable.  People gravitate to a ramp like this at slack water: dog walkers, swimmers, the boy with his radio, all leaning toward the sea as if it were a door left open.  In her lap sat the astronomical compass from the Aletta, salvaged from the wheelhouse before they cut her up.  It did not point north; it asked questions.  You had to tell it your latitude, your date, your hour, and which star you meant, and only then would it give you a bearing.  An instrument that made you admit where you were, and when.  On the night of the shoal she had been forty minutes without a fix, arguing with the mate about his daughter's tuition, sure of herself, running on dead reckoning and pride.  Two people died in the dark while she was certain.  The inquiry blamed the steering gear and cleared her, and she had wanted, badly, to be punished.  Instead she got a pension and nine years of noise.  Regret, she had learned, works like backlash in a gear train: all the slack you have been carrying gathers itself and slams the moment you try to reverse.  Her hands shook now, a navigator broken not by the sea but by arithmetic she could no longer hold in her head.  So she had learned to navigate by utility poles.  The coast road ran north out of the cove, poles numbered in white paint, and if you kept two of them in line behind you, you held a course no compass could argue with.  Pole thirty-one in line with pole thirty-two, where the road bent inland, put you on the shoal.  Fishermen had done it for a century before anyone thought to write it down.  The rental boy pushed her off without asking why a woman in a drysuit laughed at nothing.  She paddled with the poles clicking past on her left, counting them the way she used to count stars.  Twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty.  At thirty-one she stopped stroking and let the hull swing until the two white numbers stood one behind the other, and there it was: the shoal, breathing under her, a paleness in the black water.  Sirius climbed over the headland, blue and unsteady.  She had inked the numbers on her wrist before launching.  She set the latitude drum to forty-four, and reversing it woke the backlash, and the whole nine years came through the gears at once into her wrists.  She said the date aloud, then the hour, then the two names, Tomas and Alma, which she had never once said out loud.  Forty-four north, the ninth of February, seven minutes past six, and I was not where I said I was.  The needle settled.  It gave her a bearing, and the bearing was useless, because she already knew where she was.  That was not the point.  The point was that her hands had finally done what her heart had been asking of them for nine years.  She had wanted alignment, not acquittal.  She laughed again, wet and cheerful and ruined, and told the water she was sorry, and the water did what water does.  Then she turned home.  Faith came back the way Sirius comes back through cloud, guttering, not enough to steer by, enough to keep the next pole in sight.  Maybe next year, Ida had written, and Vera found she could believe it, a little, in the flicker.  Thirty-two, thirty-one, thirty.  She counted poles, and paddled, and for a while her heart and her hands went the same way.
