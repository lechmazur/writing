Lightning comes up through the cloud floor and lights the courtyard white, and I begin to count.  Seventeen seconds, maybe eighteen, before the thunder climbs up after it.  That gap is where I live.  Thin air above the clouds leaves a body driftingly alert, so seconds stretch like warm solder, and I can fit a whole life inside one.  Nobody hears me sing, ordinarily.  The lift engines grind, the fog eats sound, and the menders keep their heads down over their work.  But in the hush after a flash, every hand stops.  That is my window, and I am the balladeer of it, seldom heard, paid in soup.  The courtyard wall is our ledger.  Up here nobody says what they need, because saying it makes it true.  So they paint it.  Spray and grease pencil, layer on layer: a broken buckle, a boot with three nails, a bowl, a window with a valley in it.  My brother Tam taught the wall to talk.  He was the station tinker, and he encoded our needs in graffiti art so the foreman could read a wall and fill a requisition without anybody begging.  His cipher key was our mother's flower press bolt plate, four bolts and a slot, carried up the mountain in a sack of pressed valley violets.  Turn the plate one way and the dots mean bread; turn it a quarter round and they mean boots, or a berth on the down lift, or a name.  The plate still smells of violets and machine oil, which is the smell of my whole childhood.  I carry it on a string and tap it when I sing: tink, tink, tink-a-tink, the old mending rhyme.  Tam died in the spring, in the cable shed, quietly, the way he did everything.  Tonight the mist beads on the whitewash and shows what is under it, the way breath shows a cold window.  A ghost in the code: eleven glyphs, painted and painted over, still cycling their meaning up through the damp.  He hid his own line in the ledger, where nobody would ever requisition it.  Six seconds gone.  I kneel, set the plate against the dots, and rotate.  Bread, no; boots, no; a berth down, no.  Then the slot lines up with the crooked stem he always drew, and the wall says, in my brother's hand: I want to be heard once.  Not fed, not warm, not saved.  Heard.  Nine.  All those years I sang other people's hungers and never his, because he never asked, and I never thought to decode a man who fixed everything.  I had a line under the whitewash myself, painted at fourteen and never read: listen to me.  The mist walks past my knees.  Below, the cloud sea is a floor of milk, and the storm is a lamp moving under it.  Twelve.  So I encode him.  I chalk the bolt plate, press it four times onto the wet whitewash, and set his crooked stem beside every print, high where the foreman's lantern swings at shift change.  A requisition for one voice, filed by the wall.  Fifteen.  Then I do the other thing, the thing I am for.  I stand in the middle of the courtyard with the plate tapping time against my thumb, and I sing his line into the hush.  Tink for the bolt, tink for the plate, tink for the brother who mended the gate; he asked for nothing, he asked for one thing, and the one thing was hearing, so hear him: Tam.  The menders' heads come up.  Old Sefa, who has not spoken since the cable shed, taps her wrench on the rail in time.  Somebody says his name.  Somebody else says it.  Seventeen.  The thunder arrives, late and enormous, rolling up through the cloud floor, and it does not drown us; it only carries the last note out over the milk-white sea, toward the valley where the violets grow.  Next flash I will sing the second verse, the one I painted at fourteen.  The wall has a hundred needs under the whitewash, and I have learned to read the damp.  Tink, tink.  Count for me.
