The dunes have swallowed the old lifeboat house, all but its feral loft, and that is where I live.  Sand fills the stairwell, swallows come and go through the skylight, and a jackdaw keeps a hoard of stolen brightness in the chimney.  I am the penny poet, the vagrant who will set your name in rhyme for a coin, and I sleep beneath a mile of whispering grass. 

My real trade is glass.  I capture sounds in jars: halyards slapping a mast, rain in a frying pan, the Alderney woman laughing at something nobody wrote down.  Forty jars stand on the shelves the boatmen left, each labeled in pencil; open one and its sound comes out once, whole, and is gone forever.  You cannot sell a sound, but you can lend one, and the old people come down the beach at dusk to borrow their dead.  What I want, what I have wanted since I lost the right to my own daughter's ear, is to be the translator between the generations. 

Last week the jackdaw died, and I gleaned the chimney.  Bottle caps, a thimble, three wedding rings, and a music box the color of wet slate.  Inside the lid, in a copperplate hand: For Ines, on her ninth birthday.  Ines Alderney is ninety-one now, deaf as a post, and her granddaughter Ruthie, who is nine, believes her grandmother was born old, in a chair, in this town, with no country behind her.  Some bird in that chimney had stolen the box before Ruthie's mother was born, and forgotten it, which is one way a thing goes missing from a family. 

I wound it, and the tune came out with a hole in the middle, a limp in the dance.  One tooth gone, one note gone, and the whole song falling on its face at the same place every turn.  I could not hand a child a broken thing and call it her inheritance.  I know what a missing note does.  My daughter is in Cardiff; she has not spoken to me in twenty years, and she is right not to.  I was the hole in her tune.  Redemption is a gleaned crop, I have found: you get it after the harvest, on your knees, in the stubble other men left behind. 

So I worked all night in the loft with a candle and a file, and the sand went on breathing above me.  I cut a tooth from a steel pen nib and filed it to pitch, touching the sealed jars to my jawbone so their notes hummed up into my skull.  Halyards were too salt; the gull too cruel; the frying-pan rain too flat.  Then I found the jar labeled sugar spoon, glass bowl, Ines's kitchen, and its note hummed against my teeth, bright and small and near enough to true.  I seated the new tooth in the comb with wax, and the tune walked without its limp.  I missed my daughter then, the way you miss a lit window from a dark road: not wanting to knock, only glad that the light is on. 

At dawn's earliest whistle, the shrimp boat going out past the point, I met them on the promenade.  Ines sat wrapped in a blanket by the rail, deaf to the sea; Ruthie stood beside her with her hands behind her back, nine years old and suspicious of gifts.  I set the box in the old woman's palms and wound it.  She felt the tune come up through the wood before she heard anything, and her face came apart and put itself back together young.  The rust was still in it, sour as a nail, so I opened the sugar jar over the melody and sweetened it, and for four seconds Ines's kitchen stood on the promenade in the grey light: the spoon, the bowl, the sugar going in.  Ines sang the words in the old language, and Ruthie wrote them down in the notebook I gave her, the sounds first and then the meaning underneath.  That is the whole of translation: an old woman's mouth, a child's pencil, and a vagrant in between with a jar he cannot get back. 

Ruthie paid me a penny, because a thing bought is a thing kept, and I wrote her a verse about a girl dancing on a table in a country that is not on the map anymore.  Then I went back under the dunes with one jar rinsed and empty, and I set it on the shelf where the sugar used to be.  I have not filled it yet.  I keep it clean for Cardiff, and the grass above me goes on whispering like a crowd with all the time in the world.
