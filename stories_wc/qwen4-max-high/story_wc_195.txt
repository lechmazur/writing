The knight had not spoken above a whisper since the night the world began revising itself.  He kept the orchard now.  Glass ribs overhead, black sky pressing on them, lamps burning low along the beds.  Inside the greenhouse the trees grew by measure and by miracle, and he recorded both.  They smelled of vinegar and hot brass, and their fruit ticked as it ripened.  His ledger read: tree seven, pear, four ounces, containing a rainstorm that had never fallen; confirmed by scale, by thermometer, by three witnesses who no longer existed.  The impossible was simply another column in the book.  He weighed wonders; he did not weep over them.  When the supply cart came he left his coin on the stone and took the salt without a word, and the carter called him the mute sir.  Outside, the revisions came faster: roads unbuilding, a river deciding it had always been a road.  His order had gone in the third revision, and the boy he had sworn to guard in the fifth.  Only the oath remained, lodged in his throat like a swallowed ring.  He had learned that the revisions listened for anything spoken aloud; a whisper went under them like a root under a wall.  He wanted to capture the shape of possibility: not the boy returned, but the exact contour of the world in which the boy had lived.  If he could hold that shape, the oath would still have something to be sworn to.  Grafting had failed; so had prayer, so had geometry.  In the potting shed, under a bench, he found a marionette theater program, water-stained, abandoned by a company the revisions had eaten.  Six acts, printed in a hand-set type nobody used anymore.  The Dumb Gardener, The Weighing of the Moon, The Boy Made of String.  Beneath each title the puppeteer had printed the movements: wrist, elbow, breath, pause.  He knew those sequences.  The alchemists who built this orchard had learned their hands on puppets first; the grafting gestures were stage gestures, and the program preserved them.  Nobody alive remembered the sequence, but paper did.  Steel would not do; the patterns needed skin.  He unsnapped the clasps of his gauntlets and let them fall.  Bare hands, cracked from cold, shaking.  At tree seven he began act one.  Wrist, elbow, breath, pause; the branch leaned toward him like a puppet finding its string.  Act two weighed the moon, and the sap ran backward, and the lamps burned a color he could measure but not name.  As reality shifted, the glass went soft as skin, and the night outside filled with the sound of pages turning.  He kept the pattern.  Act three, act four; his fingers ached into shapes no living hand had made.  At act five the fruit began to form, and it needed an anchor, and he gave it the only fixed thing he had.  He murmured his allegiance.  "I am yours, and I will hold the door, and I will not be moved."  He had said it once aloud, in a courtyard, to a boy with a wooden sword; now he said it under his breath, to a shape.  The murmuring was enough.  Allegiance is not volume; it is direction.  Act six was the boy made of string, and his hands did that too.  The fruit closed around a contour.  He held it: warm, nine ounces, and inside it a Tuesday where a boy learned a parry and grew up and grew old.  He shook, and the shaking was not grief.  It was bliss, cold and exact, the joy of a man who has finally made his hands useful.  He wrote the entry with his teeth chattering: weight, temperature, the hour, the six acts, the oath.  Fact and wonder in the same column, as always.  By morning, if morning ever came, the potting shed would be a chapel and the program would be a seed catalog and his own name would be something else.  The fruit would stay.  Possibility, once captured, is a fact.  He sat under tree seven in the endless night, holding the shape of a life that might have been, murmuring the allegiance that kept him from being revised away.
